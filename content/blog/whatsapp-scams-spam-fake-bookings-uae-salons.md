Your salon's WhatsApp number is public. It sits on Google, Instagram, your website and the shop window. That is what makes it valuable — and what makes it a target. Across the UAE, salon owners are seeing the same pattern: messages that look like bookings but are not, and links that look official but steal logins. A single scam can drain a deposit, lock you out of your own business number, or send your real clients to a stranger. Most of these attempts follow a script, and once you know it, you can spot it in seconds.

## The fake booking that never arrives

The most common scam is the fake booking. It starts politely: a new number asks for a full bridal package, a group booking for six, or a long colour session on a Saturday evening. Then comes the twist. The "client" cannot pay the deposit through your normal link. They ask for your bank details, offer to send a cheque, or claim they have already paid and send a screenshot that looks convincing.

Real clients behave differently. They ask about price, time and availability, and pay through the method you offer. A stranger in a hurry to move money outside your normal process is not a client. Confirm the service, the price and the deposit, and send your usual payment link. Offer the slot only once payment clears. A genuine client accepts that. A scammer moves on to the next salon.

## The link that steals your account

The second threat is phishing. A message claiming to be from WhatsApp, Meta or a delivery company warns that your business account will be closed, or that a parcel is waiting. It contains a link and asks you to log in or enter a six-digit code. That code is the key to your account: the moment you share it, the scammer can take over your number and message your clients as if they were you.

No genuine platform will ever ask for your verification code on WhatsApp. Treat every such message as hostile: do not tap the link, do not reply, and block and report the number. If a message claims your account is at risk, open the app itself and check — never follow a link from the message. Make this a written rule for your team, not a passing comment.

## The impersonator who messages your clients

A newer trick is impersonation. A scammer copies your salon's name, logo and photos, then messages your clients from a slightly different number, offering a "special price" or asking them to pay a deposit to a new account. Clients who booked with you before have no reason to doubt it. The first you hear of it is an angry message from a regular who paid a stranger.

You cannot stop someone copying a logo, but you can make impersonation easy to spot. Tell every client, at booking and in your WhatsApp greeting message, that your salon uses one number only and never asks for payment to a personal account. Put that same line on your Google profile and your Instagram bio. When a client reports a suspicious message, reply fast, confirm it is not you, and ask them to block the number.

## The spam that drowns real enquiries

Not every threat is criminal. Some of it is just noise: "buy followers" offers, fake job applications and endless promotional messages from suppliers. Left alone, this noise buries real enquiries. A client asking about a Saturday blow-dry can sit unread under ten spam messages, and by the time you find it she has booked elsewhere.

The fix is a short daily routine. Twice a day — once mid-morning and once before the evening rush — one person clears the inbox: archive the spam, block repeat offenders, and label every real enquiry. Numbers that keep spamming get blocked, not answered. Keep the inbox for clients, and keep it clean enough that a real message is never missed.

## The deposit trap

Deposits protect your diary, but they also attract trouble. A scammer books a high-value slot, pays with a stolen card, and the payment is later reversed — leaving you with an empty chair and a chargeback fee. Another variation is the client who pays, cancels, and demands an instant refund to a different account.

Two habits close most of this down. Take deposits only through your normal payment link or card terminal, never by bank transfer to a personal account and never through a link a client sends you. And write your cancellation and refund rule into the booking message itself: deposits are refundable until a stated time, and refunds always go back to the original payment method.

## What to do in the first ten minutes

When something suspicious arrives, speed matters more than cleverness. Do not tap links, do not share codes, and do not send money to a new account. Screenshot the message, block the number, and report it inside WhatsApp. If a client has already paid a scammer pretending to be you, tell them to contact their bank immediately and report the number to the police. Then warn your other clients with one short Status update. Clients forgive a salon that warns them quickly.

Inside the salon, make one person the owner of this. That person checks the inbox twice a day, handles anything suspicious, and keeps a short note of the numbers that were blocked. Once a month, spend ten minutes reviewing what arrived: the same scam twice means your number is on a list, and your team needs to be extra careful for a while.

## Keep the door open, not unguarded

WhatsApp is still the best way for a UAE salon to take bookings. Clients message late at night, send photos, ask questions and book while they are doing something else. None of that needs to change. What needs to change is the assumption that every message is a client. A few seconds of checking — is this our normal payment method, is this our only number, does this message want a code or a click — stops almost every attempt before it costs you anything.
