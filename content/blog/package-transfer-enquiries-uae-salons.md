It arrives on WhatsApp at 9 pm, just as the salon is closing. "I bought the six-facial package but I'm travelling for two months — can my sister use the remaining sessions instead?" Say yes too easily and it becomes a shared family pass; say no too bluntly and a loyal client feels punished for asking.

Packages are everywhere in UAE salons: facials by the course, laser sessions in sixes and eights, blow-dry bundles, monthly nail packages. A client moves city, goes on maternity leave, or will not finish six sessions before the expiry date. The question is whether your team has a kind, consistent answer ready when they do.

## Understand what she is really asking

Most transfer enquiries are not attempts to cheat the salon. Listen for the real reason, because the reason shapes the right answer. The most common one is travel — a long stretch out of the country. The second is a change in circumstances: pregnancy, a new job, a move across town. The third is generosity: gifting remaining sessions to a sister, mother, or friend.

Each deserves a different response; a flat "no transfers" loses goodwill the salon did not need to lose. Train your front desk to ask one gentle question before answering: "May I ask what has changed for you?"

## Decide the rule before the message arrives

The worst time to decide your transfer policy is while a client is waiting for a reply. Decide it now, write it down, and make sure everyone on the team gives the same answer.

A workable rule: packages are sold for one named person, because the price was calculated for one person's skin, hair, and visit pattern. One transfer per package is allowed, to one named person, with the salon's agreement, and the remaining sessions keep their original expiry date. The new person gets a short consultation before the first transferred session, because the treatment was chosen for the original client's skin or hair.

That middle ground protects the salon without punishing the client. One agreed transfer costs almost nothing, and often brings in a second regular — the sister who now has three facials to use.

## The reply that keeps the booking

The reply that works best has four parts, and it fits in one WhatsApp message. First, acknowledge the situation warmly: "Of course, thank you for letting us know — and enjoy your trip." Second, state the rule plainly: "Our packages are for one person, but we do allow one transfer to a family member." Third, name the next step: "Send us your sister's name and we will move the remaining sessions to her and book her first visit." Fourth, protect the value: "The sessions keep their expiry date, and we will do a quick consultation first so the facial suits her skin."

It never asks her to come in, never says "let me check with the manager", and never offers a refund in the same breath — which would turn a solvable request into a negotiation about money.

## When the honest answer is no

Some transfers should not be allowed, and the kindest thing is to say so clearly, with an alternative in the same message. A package bought at a deep promotional price should not become a pass shared between four friends. A laser package should not transfer to someone with a different skin type or medical history, because the plan and safety screening were built for the original client. And a package that expired months ago is not revived by a transfer request.

In each case, offer something real: a first-visit price for the sister, a consultation and a course priced for her own skin, or one grace session if the client rebooks within the week. The client hears "here is what we can do" rather than "no," and the salon keeps its pricing intact.

## Watch for the quiet version of the same request

Not every transfer arrives as a direct question. Sometimes a client simply books her sister under her own name, and the sister turns up for the appointment. The front desk spots a different face and has to decide, in the moment, whether to say something.

With a written rule — one transfer, agreed in advance, new client registered first — the receptionist can smile and say, "Lovely to meet you — let me just register you quickly so your sessions are under your own name." It feels like service, not suspicion. Without the rule, the same moment becomes an awkward confrontation at the front desk at the busiest hour of the evening. Ask at the first visit who the package is for and record it. A two-minute registration step saves a difficult conversation later.

## Put the rule where clients see it

A policy only works if clients know it before they need it — "Nobody told me" turns a fair rule into a grievance.

Put one line in the message you send when a package is sold: "This package is for you, and if your plans change, you can transfer the remaining sessions to one family member — just let us know." The same line belongs on the receipt. Clients who know the rule in advance ask calmly and accept the boundaries gracefully.

Review the policy twice a year, the same way you review your prices. If every third package generates a transfer request, the package itself may be too long or too rigid for how your clients actually live — and a shorter package with a longer expiry may sell better than one that keeps needing exceptions.

## The late-night message is the test

Transfer enquiries rarely arrive during quiet hours. They arrive at 10 pm on a Friday, while the client is packing for a flight. The salon that replies the next morning with a warm, clear answer still wins her. The salon that leaves the message unread until Monday, then replies with a one-word "no," loses her — and quite possibly her sister too.

That is why the rule, the reply, and the registration habit matter: together they let any team member answer in minutes, at any hour, without guessing. The client feels heard, the sister becomes a new regular, and the package does what it was designed to do: keep good clients coming back.
