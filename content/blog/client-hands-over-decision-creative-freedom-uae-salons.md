Every stylist in Dubai has heard it. A client settles into the chair, catches your eye in the mirror and says, "Do whatever you think suits me." No photo, no brief, no boundaries. Just trust — and a quiet test.

It sounds like the easiest appointment of the day. In reality, it is one of the hardest. An open brief is not a blank cheque. It is a client saying she trusts your judgement more than her own words, and that trust is fragile. Get it right and she becomes the regular who books without asking the price and sends her friends. Get it wrong and she will not complain — she will simply never come back.

Here is how the best UAE salons handle the client who hands over the decision, and turn that leap of faith into loyalty.

## Hear the brief inside the brief

"Do whatever you like" never means literally anything. It means: I am tired of deciding, I do not know what suits me, or I have had a bad experience somewhere else and I want a professional to take charge. Your first job is to find out which one.

A good consultation still happens — it just sounds like a conversation, not an interrogation. Ask what she does for work and whether her office has a dress code. Ask how much time she spends on her hair in the morning, honestly. Ask what she liked about her last cut and what she quietly hated. A client who says "anything" but spends four minutes on her hair each morning is not a candidate for a high-maintenance layered cut, however beautiful it would look on her.

## Offer a direction, not a menu

The worst thing you can do with an open brief is hand the decision straight back. Listing six possible cuts and asking "which one?" tells her the expert has no opinion, and it puts the weight of the choice back on the person who just asked you to carry it.

Instead, offer one clear recommendation and one alternative. "I would take you to a collarbone length with soft layers — it will frame your face and you can still tie it up for the gym. If you want to stay longer, we keep the length and just add movement." That is a professional opinion, and clients can feel the difference immediately. She still has the final say, but she is choosing between two expert ideas rather than inventing one from nothing.

This matters even more in the UAE, where your chair holds women from dozens of countries with different hair textures, different ideas of what "short" means and different expectations of a salon visit. A client from Manila, a client from Moscow and a client from Mumbai can all say "do what you like" and mean three completely different things. Your recommendation should show you have understood her hair, her lifestyle and her comfort zone — not just the latest trend.

## Show, don't just tell

Before anything irreversible happens, show her what you mean. A quick photo on your phone of a similar cut on similar hair. A section of hair held up to show where the length will fall. A swatch held against her skin so she can see the colour in the salon light, not just imagine it.

This is especially important with colour. "Something warmer" can mean honey highlights to one client and copper to another, and a misunderstanding discovered at the basin is far more expensive than a two-minute swatch check. In a city where clients compare their result against the bright Dubai daylight the moment they step outside, showing the colour in natural light before you commit is one of the kindest things you can do.

## Keep the safety nets visible

Creative freedom still needs guardrails, and the best salons make them visible without making the client feel fenced in. Three habits make the difference.

First, keep a simple client card. Hair history, colour formulas, what was agreed last time, and one line about what she liked. When she returns in eight weeks and says "same as last time", you can actually deliver it — and when she says "do something different", you know exactly what "different" means relative to.

Second, agree a fallback before you start. "If the layers feel too light once it dries, come back within the week and I will adjust them free of charge." That single sentence removes the fear that stops so many clients from saying yes to change.

Third, price the idea before you begin. An open brief is the easiest place for a bill surprise, because the client never asked for a price on a service she never named. A quick "that will be this much and take about ninety minutes" before you start protects the checkout moment, which is where so much goodwill is quietly lost.

## When she loves it, make it repeatable

The real prize of a successful open brief is not one great haircut. It is a client who now trusts you completely — and trust like that is worth more than any loyalty card. The mistake is treating the next visit as a fresh start and asking "so, what are we doing today?" as if the last appointment never happened.

Instead, open her card, remind her what you did, and build on it. "Last time we went shorter with the layers — shall we keep the shape and go a shade warmer for winter?" She feels remembered, and remembered clients are loyal clients. A short WhatsApp message the next day — "how is the cut settling?" — costs you thirty seconds and turns a good visit into a story she tells her friends.
