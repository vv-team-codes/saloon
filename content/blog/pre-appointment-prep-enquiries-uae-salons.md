Every salon owner in Dubai knows the message. It arrives at 10:47 pm, as the last client is leaving. "Hi, I booked a balayage for Thursday — should I wash my hair before I come?" It looks like a small question. It is not. A client who asks how to prepare is a client who has already decided to come — the quality of your answer is the only thing standing between that message and a confirmed visit.

These questions arrive all day. Should I come with clean skin for a facial? Do I need to remove gel nails before a manicure? Can I wear makeup to a lash appointment? How long should hair be before waxing? Most salons answer from memory, differently every time, and sometimes not until the next morning. That inconsistency quietly costs bookings — and results, because an unprepared client often gets a worse outcome.

## The question behind the question

A preparation question is never really about shampoo. It is a client checking three things at once: that she has chosen the right salon, that she will not embarrass herself by arriving wrong, and that the appointment is worth the trip across town in Dubai traffic.

That is why a vague reply is so damaging. "It's fine either way" answers nothing and leaves the client to guess. A good answer does the opposite: a clear instruction, one short reason, and a step toward the booking.

## What clients actually ask before they arrive

The questions cluster around a handful of services, and your whole team should agree on each answer.

For colour and balayage, arrive with hair that has not been freshly washed that morning — a day-old scalp is more comfortable during colour — and skip heavy styling products and dry shampoo. For a precision cut, clean, dry, product-free hair shows the stylist how it falls naturally. For a facial, arrive with a bare face if you can; coming straight from the office is fine, but the salon should allow time for a proper cleanse. For lash extensions, no mascara and no eye cream that morning. For a manicure, say whether you already have gel or acrylic on, because removal changes the appointment length. For waxing, hair should be roughly a grain of rice in length, and skin free of fake tan, heavy lotion and recent sun.

None of this is complicated. The problem is that it lives in the stylists' heads, not in a written answer the front desk can send in seconds.

## Answer the question, then book the chair

The best replies follow a simple shape: answer first, add the reason, then close with the next step.

"For your balayage on Thursday, please come with hair that wasn't washed this morning — a day-old scalp is more comfortable during colour. We've kept your 6 pm slot with Sara; you'll be done around 9 pm. Shall we send a reminder on Wednesday evening?"

That message answers the question, proves the salon is organised, restates the time and stylist, and ends with a small, easy yes. Compare that with "no need to wash" and a thumbs-up emoji. One message builds confidence; the other ends the conversation.

The same shape works when the client has not booked yet. "For a first balayage, come with unwashed hair — we will wash it here before we start. We have Thursday at 6 pm or Saturday at 11 am with Sara. Which suits you?" A preparation question from someone who has not booked is the warmest enquiry a salon gets — answering it well is often the difference between a booking and a screenshot that never gets a reply.

## When the honest answer is "it depends"

Some questions have no single answer. Whether a client should wash her hair before colour depends on the product, the technique and her scalp. Whether she can wear makeup to a lash appointment depends on the treatment. Whether a facial is safe depends on her skin.

In those cases, say so — and say what happens next. "It depends on your hair, so Sara will check it when you arrive and wash it here if needed. Just come without dry shampoo or heavy styling products." That is honest — and better than a confident guess that turns out wrong. And when the question touches health — a skin condition, a pregnancy, a recent treatment — the answer should always be to check with the stylist first, never to promise that everything will be fine.

## Turn prep answers into fewer no-shows

Preparation messages are also one of the best no-show tools a salon has. A client who has been told what to do before her visit has already invested in the appointment — she has planned around it and is far less likely to forget it or cancel casually.

So do not wait for clients to ask. The day before the appointment, send a short WhatsApp message with the preparation notes for that service: what to wear, what to avoid, how long it takes, and what time to arrive. Clients read it because it is about them, not about the salon.

## Write it down once, use it forever

If every stylist gives a different answer to the same question, the salon sounds disorganised, and the client notices. Sit down for half an hour and write one agreed preparation note for each of your ten most-booked services — colour, balayage, cut, blow-dry, facial, lashes, nails, waxing, keratin and bridal hair will cover most of what arrives in your inbox. Save each one as a WhatsApp quick reply so anyone on the team can send it in seconds.

Review the notes whenever you change a product or supplier — a note that mentions a product you no longer use is worse than no note at all.

## The quiet booking signal

Pre-appointment questions feel like admin, but they are the sound of a client who has already chosen you, asking for one last reason to feel sure. Answer clearly, answer fast, and answer the same way every time. The salon that does that gets clients who arrive on time, get the result they hoped for, and book their next visit before they leave the chair.
