Every UAE salon has a client like her. She has been coming for two years, always the same chair, always the same stylist. She books her colour six weeks ahead, she never cancels, and she knows your receptionist by name. She is a wonderful regular — and she is worth far more than her own bookings, because every working day she sits in an office full of people who also need a salon.

Most salon owners never notice this. They treat each regular as one client, when in a city like Dubai or Abu Dhabi, a regular is often a doorway into a whole workplace. Colleagues notice her hair. They ask where she goes. One happy regular in the right office can quietly become five, six, ten regulars — if the salon makes it easy for her to bring them.

## Why the office is the best referral network you already have

Word of mouth is powerful, but office word of mouth is different. It is repeated daily, face to face, among people who trust each other. When a colleague compliments her blow-dry in the lift, the answer is not a vague recommendation — it is a salon name, a stylist name, and a WhatsApp number, delivered in the moment the interest is highest.

It is also practical. Colleagues share a location and a schedule. If your salon is a ten-minute drive from their office, the same journey works for all of them. They can book back-to-back appointments or slip out during the same lunch break. A salon that understands office rhythms can serve a whole team in a single quiet afternoon, turning a gap in the diary into a block of bookings.

## Make it easy for her to bring them

The first rule is simple: never make your regular do the work of selling your salon. She is happy to recommend you, but she will not chase her colleagues or explain your prices. Your job is to make the introduction effortless.

Start with something she can share in two seconds. A short WhatsApp message with your salon name, your area, and one sentence about what you do best is enough. When she mentions you at work, she can forward it straight away, while her colleague is still interested.

Then make the first visit easy for the newcomer. Offer the colleague a simple first booking — a named stylist, a clear time, and a price range given upfront. New clients often hesitate over two things: what it will cost, and who they will get. Answer both before they ask, and the booking happens.

## The lunch-hour block and the after-work rush

Office clients live by the clock, and the smartest salons plan around it. The lunch hour, roughly 1 to 2 pm, is gold for quick services — a blow-dry, a threading, a gel manicure, a fringe trim. These are services that fit into sixty minutes, and they are exactly what a working client wants midweek.

The after-work window, from about 6 pm onwards, is the other peak. This is when colour, treatments, and longer services happen, because nobody is rushing back to a desk. If your salon is near offices in Business Bay, DIFC, JLT, or Media City, these two windows are where office regulars are won or lost.

Try this: when your regular books her usual slot, ask whether a colleague would like the chair beside her, or the hour after. A shared visit feels like a treat rather than an errand, and two bookings in one afternoon beat one booking and an empty chair.

## Treat the group like a group

Once two or three colleagues are coming to you, stop treating them as separate strangers. Keep a small note on each one — not just their service, but how they connect. A note that says "works with Aisha, prefers Thursday evenings, likes her colour cooler" is the difference between a salon that remembers and a salon that starts from scratch every visit.

Offer the group something small that costs you almost nothing. Priority on the same evening once a month. A WhatsApp message when a slot opens beside a colleague's booking. These are not discounts — they are conveniences, and conveniences are what keep busy working clients loyal.

Be careful with one thing: never discuss one colleague's bookings, prices, or hair with another. Office gossip travels fast, and a salon that leaks a client's details loses the whole office at once. Discretion is part of the service.

## What to do when the first colleague tries you

The first visit from a colleague is the moment that decides everything. If she leaves happy, she tells the office. If she leaves unsure, she tells the office too. So the first visit deserves your best chair, your clearest consultation, and your most honest advice.

Ask her what she liked about her colleague's result, and what she wants for herself. Show her the price before you start, not after. Finish on time — a working client who is late back to the office will not return, however good the result. And before she leaves, book the next visit at the chair. A colleague who walks out with her next appointment already in the diary is a regular in the making.

Follow up the same evening with a short WhatsApp message. Thank her, remind her of the aftercare in one line, and leave the door open. One message, warm and specific, is worth more than any offer.

## The quiet maths of one regular

Do the sums for your own salon. One loyal regular who visits every six weeks is nine visits a year. If she brings two colleagues, and each of them becomes a regular at the same rhythm, that one relationship is suddenly twenty-seven visits a year — three regulars, one source, and no advertising spend at all.

That is why the office regular matters so much. She is not just a booking. She is a bridge into a workplace full of people who need exactly what you do, at exactly the hours you are open, within a few minutes of your door.

So look at your diary this week and ask a different question. Not "how do I find new clients?" but "which of my regulars works in a busy office — and have I made it easy for her to bring them?"
