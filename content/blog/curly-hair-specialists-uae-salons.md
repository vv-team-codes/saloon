Every UAE salon owner knows this client. She walks in with beautiful, thick curls and walks out with hair that looked better before she arrived. The cut was done wet, combed flat, and dried into a triangle. She smiles politely, pays, and simply moves on.

That client is not rare. The UAE is home to one of the most diverse populations in the world, and a large share of residents have curly or wavy hair. Add the Gulf humidity, hard desalinated water, and year-round sun, and you have a market full of people actively looking for a salon that understands their hair. Most salons, though, are still built around straight hair. That gap is the opportunity.

## The clients most salons are quietly losing

Curly-haired clients are some of the most loyal clients in the business — once they find the right stylist. A bad curly cut is not a minor disappointment, it is months of growing out. So when a client finally finds a stylist who understands her curls, she books every six to eight weeks, she books the same person, she brings her sister and her friends, and she does not haggle over price. She has already paid the real price of a bad cut, and she will not go back to it.

The trouble is that most of these clients never get that far. They try a salon, get a cut that ignores their curl pattern, and decide the salon is not for them. The salon never hears why. The diary shows a one-time client; the real story is a client who would have been worth thousands of dirhams a year, lost in a single visit. In Dubai or Abu Dhabi, where new residents arrive every week and ask colleagues and neighbours for recommendations, that quiet loss repeats itself constantly.

## Why curls are a different craft

A curly cut fails for predictable reasons, and none of them are about talent. Cutting curly hair wet, the way straight hair is cut, is the first mistake: curls shrink as they dry, so a wet cut that looks even in the chair can end up two inches shorter and uneven once the hair dries. Combing curls out before cutting is the second: it destroys the natural pattern the stylist is supposed to be shaping. And thinning shears used freely through curly hair create the frizz and the triangle shape clients hate most.

A curl-aware stylist works differently. The hair is cut dry, curl by curl, in its natural fall, so the stylist can see exactly what the finished shape will be. Layers are placed to remove weight where curls bunch up, not to thin the hair out. The consultation is longer and more honest: what the client's curl pattern actually is, how much shrinkage to expect, and what the cut will look like on a humid August day, not just under salon lights.

## What a curl-aware service actually looks like

Adding curly hair to a menu does not mean rebuilding the salon. It means building one honest service and doing it properly. Start with a dedicated curly cut, booked as a longer appointment — usually 60 to 75 minutes including the consultation — with a named stylist who has been trained for it. Ask the client to arrive with her hair as she normally wears it, not freshly straightened, so the consultation happens with the hair dry and in its natural state.

The service itself needs a few small changes that cost almost nothing. A diffuser on the dryers, so curls are dried without being blasted apart. A wide-tooth comb instead of a brush at the basin. Products chosen for moisture and definition rather than hold: leave-in conditioner, curl cream, a light gel. And a finishing routine the stylist can teach in two minutes, because a client who leaves knowing how to recreate the result at home is a client who comes back.

## Pricing it without scaring anyone off

The most common mistake is pricing a curly cut like a standard cut. It takes longer, it needs more skill, and the client knows it. A curly cut priced 30 to 50 percent above a standard cut is fair, and clients who have been burned before will pay it gladly — provided the value is obvious. The consultation, the dry cut, the styling lesson and the home-care card are the value. If the price looks the same as everywhere else, the client assumes the service is the same as everywhere else, and she has already tried that.

## How to tell the market you do curls

This is where most salons fail, because they add the service and then whisper about it. Curly clients find salons the way everyone does now: they search, they watch, and they ask in WhatsApp groups. So the marketing has to show, not tell. Short videos of a real curly cut, before and after, with the client's natural hair, beat any poster. A reel that shows the dry cut, the diffuser, and the finished curls will be saved and shared by exactly the people you want to reach.

The words matter too. Put "curly hair" on the service menu, on the booking page, and in the WhatsApp quick replies, so a client who messages at 10 PM asking "do you cut curly hair?" gets a confident yes with a price, a duration, and a stylist's name. Many of those messages arrive after hours, when the front desk has gone home — which is why a 24/7 AI receptionist like ZYNA, answering enquiries and booking the consultation while the salon is closed, fits this service so well. The client who gets an answer at night books the appointment in the morning.

## A small start beats a big launch

The smartest way into this market is small. Send one stylist on a curly-cutting course, give her a dedicated slot on the diary twice a week, and let the results build. Track three numbers: how many curly cuts you do each month, how many of those clients rebook within eight weeks, and how many new curly clients arrive by recommendation. If the rebooking rate is high — and for a good curly service it usually is — the case for more training, more slots, and a bigger place on the menu makes itself.

The UAE salon market rewards salons that notice the clients everyone else overlooks. The salon that takes curly hair seriously, learns the craft, and says so clearly will not need to chase these clients. They are already looking.
