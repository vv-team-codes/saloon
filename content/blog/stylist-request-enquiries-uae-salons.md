Every salon owner in the UAE knows this message. It arrives on WhatsApp in the evening, often just after a client has seen a friend's new haircut: "Hi, can I book with Sara on Thursday?" The client is not asking about a service or a price. She is asking for a person — and how you answer in the next few minutes decides whether that booking happens at all.

Stylist requests are fragile. The requested stylist may be fully booked, on leave, or working at another branch that week. A flat "she is not available" sends the client to another salon, and a vague "we will check and let you know" leaves her waiting. In a city where the next salon is one search away, waiting usually means losing her. The salons that handle this well treat a stylist request as a sales conversation, not a diary problem.

## Why clients ask for a person, not a service

The motive behind the request changes the reply. Most clients who name a stylist are returning clients who trust one person, referrals sent by a friend who named Sara, or clients who saw a specific result online and assume only that stylist can reproduce it.

Each group needs a different answer: the returning client needs reassurance, the referral needs a reason to trust the salon, and the online viewer needs honest guidance about whether the result depends on the stylist or the technique. So before offering a slot, ask one short question: "Is there a particular style you had in mind, or is it Sara herself you would like to see?"

## The golden rule: never end on a no

The most common mistake is answering with a dead end. "Sara is fully booked this week" may be true, but it gives the client nothing to say yes to. Every reply should contain an alternative in the same message, ideally two: acknowledge the request warmly, state the reality honestly, then offer a concrete next step.

For example: "Sara is fully booked on Thursday, but she has Saturday at 11am. If Thursday is the only day that works, Layla does the same balayage technique and has Thursday at 6pm — shall I hold either for you?" It protects Sara's diary, introduces a colleague by name and skill rather than as a random substitute, and ends with an easy choice.

This matters even more in the UAE, where clients book around work, school runs and weekend plans. A client who wanted Thursday evening and is offered only Saturday morning may simply disappear, while a same-day alternative with another stylist keeps the visit inside your salon — and a good experience there quietly builds trust in the whole team.

## When the stylist is away for longer

Annual leave and visa renewals are part of salon life here, and clients notice when a familiar face disappears. The worst approach is silence: the client messages, nobody mentions the absence, and she assumes the stylist has left. A short, honest note works far better: "Sara is on leave until the 20th. Her clients are being looked after by Layla, who works closely with her, and Sara's notes on your last colour are on your file."

That last detail matters. A simple record of each client's formula and preferences lets a colleague step in without the client explaining everything from scratch. Clients stay loyal to salons that remember them, not just to individuals.

## Turning a request into a team strength

The smartest salons in Dubai and Abu Dhabi do something counterintuitive: they do not hide their team behind one star stylist. Their replies name several stylists and their specialties: "Sara specialises in blonding, Layla in curly cuts, and Noor in bridal styling." When a client asks for Sara and Sara is busy, the alternative is not a stranger — it is a named expert the client has already heard of.

This also protects the business: a salon built around one popular stylist is one resignation away from a crisis.

## The reply speed problem

Stylist requests are unusually time-sensitive. The client has already decided she wants a booking; she is not browsing. If your reply arrives three hours later, she has often already booked elsewhere, especially during peak evening hours. Many small salons lose bookings they never knew about — an enquiry read at midnight and answered the next morning is usually already lost.

A practical fix is a simple triage rule: stylist requests and same-day booking requests get answered first, before price questions and aftercare queries. Even a short holding reply — "Let me check Sara's diary and confirm within 15 minutes" — keeps the client engaged while you check properly. The key is keeping the promise: if you say 15 minutes, reply in 15 minutes.

## What to say when the answer is genuinely no

Sometimes there is no good alternative: the stylist has left, or the diary is genuinely full for two weeks. Then honesty plus a waiting list beats a polite brush-off. Tell the client plainly, offer to add her to a priority list for cancellations, and give a realistic timeframe rather than a vague "soon." Clients accept bad news gracefully when they feel respected; what they do not forgive is feeling ignored or misled.

Record these requests too: if three clients in a week ask for a stylist who is on leave, that signals a staffing or training gap.

## A simple script your team can use today

You do not need a complicated system. A short script, shared with everyone who answers messages, covers most situations: greet warmly and use the stylist's name, confirm what the client wants, state availability honestly, offer two concrete alternatives, and end with a choice. Add one rule: never let a stylist request sit unanswered for more than 30 minutes during opening hours, or until morning without at least a holding reply.

Handled well, a request for one stylist becomes proof that your salon listens, remembers its clients, and has a team worth trusting — the kind of experience clients recommend to friends.
