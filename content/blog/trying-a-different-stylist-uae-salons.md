She has been coming to the same stylist for two years. She trusts her, she likes her, she books her by name. Then she sees another stylist's work on your salon's Instagram — a balayage, a precision bob, a bridal updo — and she wonders what it would feel like in that chair instead.

Most clients never say this out loud — they feel disloyal just for thinking it. So they quietly book the other stylist and hope nobody notices, try a different salon, or stay put and slowly get bored. Two of those three cost you a client. The salons that keep her make trying someone new feel normal and guilt-free.

## Why clients want to try someone new

It is rarely about unhappiness. Her usual stylist may be fully booked on the Saturday she needs, she may want a service her stylist does not specialise in, or she may simply be curious after eighteen months of the same cut.

There is also a practical Dubai reason: people here travel. A regular flies out for the summer, comes back in September and finds her stylist on leave. If the salon has no graceful way to place her with someone else, that first visit back becomes an awkward negotiation instead of a homecoming. And the guilt is real — asking for someone else can feel like ending a friendship. Your job is to remove that worry before she has to voice it.

## The cost of making it awkward

When a salon treats stylist loyalty as a rule rather than a preference, clients stop experimenting inside your salon. They experiment outside it instead. A client who cannot try your colour specialist without a scene will try the colour specialist down the road, and if that visit goes well, you may never see her again.

A client who knows three stylists in your salon is far more loyal to the salon than one loyal to a single person — if that person leaves, the single-stylist client leaves with her.

## Make the whole team visible

Clients cannot ask for a stylist they do not know exists. Most salons introduce the team only at the booking stage, when the client picks a name from a list. By then she has already decided, and the decision is the name she already knows.

Show the team earlier. A short Reel of each stylist's best work, a "meet the team" highlight on Instagram, a card at reception naming each stylist's speciality — these turn the team from a list of names into people with distinct strengths. When a client sees that Sara does the sharpest bobs and Mariam does the softest lived-in colour, asking for Mariam feels like choosing the right person for the job, not a betrayal.

## The taster: a low-stakes first try

The easiest way to let a client try someone new is to make the first try small. A full colour correction with a new stylist is a big commitment. A fringe trim, a blow-dry or a fifteen-minute consultation is not.

When a regular books, the receptionist can say, "Your stylist is fully booked on Saturday, but our colour specialist has a slot at 11 — would you like a consultation with her first, no charge?" Even if she waits for her usual stylist this time, the new stylist is no longer a stranger next time.

## The two-minute handover

The moment that matters most is the handover, and it should happen before the client sits in the new chair. It has three parts and takes about two minutes.

First, pass on the facts: the formula, the cut, the preferences, the sensitivities — your client notes make this possible. Second, introduce the new stylist with warmth, not apology: "Mariam is brilliant with this colour" sounds like a recommendation, while "Sara is off today, so Mariam will have to do it" sounds like a consolation prize.

Third, the usual stylist follows up. A short message after the visit — "I heard you had a lovely colour with Mariam, it suits you" — tells the client nobody is keeping score.

## What to say — and what never to say

Clients usually ask indirectly. "Is Sara free on Saturday?" is often a test: if the answer is no, will the salon help her or leave her to work it out? The reply that keeps her is specific and warm: "Sara is fully booked Saturday, but Layla has 11 AM and she does beautiful work with your hair type — shall I hold it for you?"

What never to say: "She's my client" or "You'll have to wait for Sara." Train the whole team on one rule: a request to try someone new is always answered with a name, a time and enthusiasm. And when a client returns to her usual stylist afterwards, welcome that just as warmly.

## When the switch becomes permanent

Sometimes the taster turns into a permanent change. A client tries the new stylist once, loves the result, and quietly moves over. This feels like a loss, but it is not. The client stayed in your salon — still paying your prices, still rebooking, still recommending you.

Handle it with grace. The original stylist should be told first, privately, by the owner or manager — never by the client or by gossip. The message is simple: the client is happy, she is still ours, and the salon is bigger than any one chair.

## Start this week

Put each stylist's speciality on your Instagram highlights and at reception. Add one line to your booking replies: "If your stylist is ever unavailable, we will always offer you the best match — just ask." Offer a free fifteen-minute consultation with any stylist a client has not tried before, and brief the team on the handover habit: two minutes, three facts, one warm introduction.

Clients who try a second stylist book more often, because there are now two people they trust instead of one. And when a stylist takes annual leave, her clients do not drift — they simply sit in the next chair, with someone they already know.
