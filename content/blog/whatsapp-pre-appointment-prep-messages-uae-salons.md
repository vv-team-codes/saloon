Most salon days do not fall apart at the reception desk. They fall apart ten minutes into a service, when the stylist discovers the client washed her hair an hour ago and the colour will not take evenly, or the facialist learns the client started a strong retinol serum three days ago and the treatment is now unsafe to perform. The appointment was confirmed and the client arrived on time — yet the diary is running late and the next client is waiting.

The fix costs nothing: a short WhatsApp message sent the day before, telling the client exactly how to arrive. Salons that do this well treat it as part of the service — a quiet habit that protects the diary, the result and the client's experience.

## The cost of an unprepared client

Every service has a version of the same problem. A colour client arrives with freshly washed, conditioner-heavy hair, and the stylist spends twenty minutes working around it. A bride-to-be books a facial the day before her wedding, not knowing some treatments need a week of settling time. A nail client arrives with old gel still on, adding thirty unplanned minutes to a slot priced for a fresh set.

None of these clients did anything wrong. They simply were not told. And when the salon absorbs the delay, the cost lands somewhere: the stylist rushes the finish, the next appointment starts late, or the service is quietly shortened to catch up.

## When to send it — and what to say

The best time is the afternoon or early evening before the appointment. Send it a week ahead and it gets buried; send it on the morning and the client may already be on her way. Around 5 or 6 PM the day before is the sweet spot: she is planning tomorrow and the message still feels like care rather than a nag.

Keep it to four short lines: one confirms the appointment — service, time, stylist's name. One gives the one or two preparation steps that matter for that service. One covers the practical detail clients forget, such as parking or the mall entrance. And one invites a reply: "Reply here if anything has changed." A client who replies "can we make it 7 instead of 6?" is a client who does not become a no-show.

Write it in the client's language — in the UAE that often means English, but many salons keep the same message saved in Arabic, Tagalog and Hindi too. A prep message a client cannot read is the same as no message at all.

## Prep notes for the services that need them most

The aim is one or two instructions per service, never a wall of rules. Colour clients need to arrive with hair that has not been freshly washed that morning, and to know a patch test is required if they have never had colour with you before. That single line saves the awkward moment when a client arrives and the patch test has to be done on the spot.

Facial clients need to know what to stop before they arrive: no strong exfoliants, no retinol and no waxing in the days before, and no new products on the morning itself. A client who hears this for the first time on the treatment bed gets a shorter, gentler version of what she paid for.

Nail clients need to arrive with old polish or gel removed if the booking does not include removal. Keratin clients need to arrive with clean, product-free hair. Bridal clients need the simplest instruction of all: arrive with the hair and skin in the state you discussed at the trial, and bring the accessories and the photo. Pick the one or two things that would genuinely change the appointment if the client got them wrong, and say only those.

## What to do when they do not read it

Some clients will not read the message, and some will read it and forget. Send it anyway: it creates a record the stylist can point to kindly, it still catches the majority who do read it, and a client who received a thoughtful message the day before arrives already feeling looked after.

For clients who never read WhatsApp messages, put a one-line version of the same instruction in the booking confirmation itself. And when a client repeatedly arrives unprepared, note it in her record — next time the front desk can mention it in one sentence while confirming: "Same as last time — please come with unwashed hair."

## A template you can copy today

Write one template per service, save it where the whole team can find it, and send it the day before every appointment. Here is a colour version a salon could use tomorrow:

"Hi Sara, looking forward to seeing you tomorrow at 6 PM with Layla for your balayage. Please come with hair that hasn't been washed today, and bring a photo of the look you love if you have one. We're on the first floor near the food court — parking is free for two hours. Reply here if anything changes. See you tomorrow!"

Notice what it leaves out: no list of rules, no terms and conditions, no lecture about cancellations. The preparation detail sits inside a warm message, not on top of it.

Start with your three most-booked services and write one message for each. Test them for a month and watch two numbers: how often appointments start on time, and how often a service has to be shortened because the client arrived unprepared. Both usually improve within a fortnight, at no cost but consistency.

The salons clients call "so organised" rarely have bigger teams or better software — just small habits, repeated daily, that make the visit feel effortless. A short message the day before is one of the smallest and most effective. It tells the client you have already thought about her visit before she walks through the door, and that feeling is what brings her back.
