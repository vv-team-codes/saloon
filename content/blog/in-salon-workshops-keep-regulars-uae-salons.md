Every salon owner in the UAE knows the same quiet fear. A regular who has sat in your chair for two years starts stretching her visits. Six weeks becomes eight. Eight becomes twelve. She has not complained. She has simply drifted — and by the time you notice, she is someone else's regular.

Most retention ideas try to pull her back after she has already gone: discounts, reminders, win-back messages. An in-salon workshop works the other way around. It gives a regular a reason to stay connected between visits, and it deepens the relationship while she is still happy. Teach her something useful and your salon becomes more than a place she visits. It becomes a place she belongs to.

## Why Teaching Builds Loyalty

A haircut or a facial is a service. A workshop is a relationship. When a stylist shows a client how to recreate her blow-dry at home, or how to do a five-minute evening makeup look, something shifts. The client stops seeing the salon as a transaction and starts seeing it as her team. She trusts the people who taught her, because teaching is generous.

A client who understands her own hair or skin also values the professional result more, not less — and she stops comparing your prices with the salon down the road. She talks about the workshop too, with sisters, colleagues, and friends in her building WhatsApp groups. One workshop of eight regulars can quietly reach eighty people who have never set foot in your salon.

## What to Teach

The best topics are the small frustrations your clients already mention in the chair. Listen for a week and you will have a list.

A blow-dry masterclass is the classic: volume at the roots, smoothing frizz in Dubai humidity, and which brush actually does what. A five-minute makeup session works beautifully before party season — one look, three products, no jargon. A skincare evening suits the winter months: the order of products and what each one does, in plain language. Before wedding season, try a braid and updo session; for a mixed clientele, a men's grooming class. Keep every topic narrow. One skill, taught properly, beats five skills skimmed in an hour.

## The Format That Works

Keep it small and hands-on. Six to eight clients is the sweet spot: enough energy in the room, small enough that everyone gets attention. Forty-five to sixty minutes is right — long enough to learn, short enough that nobody checks the time.

Run workshops in your quiet hours. A Tuesday morning after the school run, or a Sunday late morning, uses chairs that would otherwise sit empty. Set up two or three stations with mirrors, and let clients practise on themselves while your stylists walk around and correct them gently.

Give everyone a one-page card with the steps and the products you used. That card lives on a bathroom mirror, with your salon's name on it every morning. It is the cheapest marketing you will ever do.

## How to Price It

For your regulars, make it free. A free workshop for a regular who visits every month costs you an hour of quiet time and some product. In return you get a client who feels valued and who books her next visit before she leaves.

For non-regulars, charge a small fee — fifty to a hundred dirhams — and make it redeemable against a service or a product. The fee filters out people who were never going to book, and the redemption turns the workshop into a first visit.

If you sell retail products, workshops are the honest way to do it: when a client has just learned to use a product on her own hair, recommending it is finishing the lesson, not selling. Never turn the session into a sales pitch, though. One mention, at the end, with the price — and then stop. The trust you build by not pushing is worth far more than one extra sale.

## Turning a Workshop Into Bookings

End every session by looking at the calendar together. Offer each client a slot for the service you just taught — a blow-dry for the weekend, a facial before an event, a colour refresh. Book it there and then, while the enthusiasm is warm.

Invite with intention. Send a personal WhatsApp message to twenty regulars, not a broadcast to two hundred strangers. Name the topic, the date, and the fact that places are limited to eight. Regulars-first invitations make people feel chosen, and they make the workshop feel like a privilege rather than a promotion.

After the session, send a thank-you message the same evening with the date of the next workshop. That message is also the moment to ask the simplest question in retention: would you like me to book your next visit now?

## Make It a Rhythm, Not a One-Off

One workshop is a nice event. A monthly workshop is a retention system. Put it on the calendar for the same week every month, and let clients start asking when the next one is.

Rotate the topics through the year so regulars keep coming back. January is a skincare reset. February is a Valentine's look. Before Ramadan, a quick evening routine for busy nights. In summer, frizz control and scalp care. In November, party hair and makeup for the December season.

A workshop fails when it becomes a lecture or a sales event, so keep it hands-on and keep the selling to one honest mention at the end. Do not overfill the room, and never run one in peak evening hours. The first session is a rehearsal: ask the eight clients what they liked, change one thing, and run it again.

## The Salon They Belong To

Retention is not really about points, or discounts, or reminders. It is about belonging. A client stays with a salon where she feels known, where she learns something, and where the people remember her. A workshop gives her all three in one hour.

Start small. Pick one topic your clients already ask about, invite eight regulars personally, and run it in a quiet hour next week. You will know it worked when the first client books her next visit before she leaves — and asks, on her way out, when the next one is.
