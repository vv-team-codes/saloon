Every salon owner in the UAE knows the message. It arrives late in the evening, just as you are closing the diary: "Hi, how much for balayage?" You reply with the price range. A minute later comes the follow-up: "Can you do it cheaper?"

It is the most awkward message in salon WhatsApp. Say yes and you have taught that client — and every client she tells — that your prices are negotiable. Say no badly and she books somewhere else. Most discount requests are not really about the money. They are a test of confidence: the way you answer decides whether the client respects your price or keeps pushing it down.

WhatsApp makes haggling easy. Face to face, most people would never ask a stylist to cut her price. In a chat window, the same question costs nothing — the client is sitting at home, comparing three salons at once. Clients here are also used to negotiating on cars, rent and souk prices, and many assume a salon price works the same way. Your reply is often the first time a client hears why a salon service has a fixed price.

## Pause before you answer

The worst replies are the fast ones. A quick "Sorry, no discounts" reads as cold; a quick "Okay, 10% off" reads as desperate. Before you answer, ask what the client is really asking: a lower price, or whether the service is worth it? Most of the time it is the second question wearing the first question's clothes.

If a team member handles your WhatsApp, agree on one rule: nobody offers a discount in a chat without checking with the owner first. A discount given in haste is almost impossible to take back, and regular clients notice very quickly when one person pays less than another for the same service.

## Answer the value, not the number

A strong reply never argues about the price; it shifts the conversation to what the price includes. A balayage is not a tube of colour and an hour in a chair — it is a consultation, a senior colourist, premium products, a toner, a blow-dry, and a stylist who will still be there in six weeks when the colour needs refreshing. Clients cannot see most of that from a price list, so say it plainly.

Keep the reply short and warm: "Our balayage starts at AED 450, and that includes the consultation, toner and blow-dry with a senior colourist. We keep our prices the same for everyone so every client gets the same care." That message restates the price without apologising, explains what the client gets, and gives a fair reason — equal treatment — that most clients accept immediately. Clients here are used to paying for quality; what they dislike is feeling that a price is arbitrary.

## Offer value instead of a cut

Holding the price does not mean offering nothing. The trick is to offer something that costs you little and means a lot to the client, instead of cutting the price itself. A complimentary deep-conditioning treatment with a colour service, a free fringe trim between appointments, or a blow-dry included in a first visit — these gestures feel generous without teaching the client that your prices move.

The gesture must be framed as a one-time welcome, not a new lower price. "For your first visit, we will include the toner and a complimentary conditioning treatment" is a gift. "We can do it for AED 380 this time" is a discount, and the next time she messages, the starting point will be AED 380. The difference matters enormously over a year of regular visits.

Packages work the same way. A package of four facials at AED 1,200 is not a discount on one facial — it is a commitment to a routine, paid in advance, that fills your quiet weekday chairs. Offer the package, not a cheaper single service.

## What never to write

Some replies do more damage than the discount itself. Never write "That is our final price" in the first exchange; it closes a door that was still open. Never blame costs — "products are expensive now" invites the client to suggest cheaper products. Never compare yourself to another salon by name, and never write a long justification: long messages in a negotiation read as nervousness, and nervousness invites another push.

Above all, never argue in the chat. If a client becomes rude or keeps pushing after two polite replies, move the conversation off WhatsApp. "I would love to explain it properly — would you like a quick call?" ends the haggling loop, because most people will not repeat a discount demand out loud. A short call also lets you hear whether the client genuinely cannot afford the service — then suggest a shorter service that fits her budget instead of cutting the price.

## The follow-up that saves the booking

Many discount requests end in silence. The client reads your reply, does not answer, and you assume the booking is lost. It often is not — she may simply be thinking, comparing, or waiting until payday. A single gentle follow-up the next day keeps the door open without chasing: "Just checking in — would you like me to hold a slot for Thursday evening? The 7 pm with Sara is still free."

That message works because it changes the subject from price to availability. Evening slots fill fast, especially Thursday and Friday, and a specific slot with a named stylist is a stronger reason to book than a lower price. One follow-up is helpful; three is pressure.

## The price is part of the experience

Clients who book because the price was cut rarely become loyal: they return when the next discount appears and vanish when it does not. Clients who book because they understood the value — the consultation, the senior stylist, the products, the result — come back for the result, and bring friends who expect the same standard.

Every "can you do it cheaper?" message is a chance to explain what your salon actually sells. Answer it with confidence, offer value instead of a cut, and follow up once with a real slot. Most clients will book — and the chair left by one who does not is better filled by someone who values your work.
