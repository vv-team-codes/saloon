A client may forget the exact shade of her balayage within a week. She will not forget the towel that smelled stale, the comb pulled from a crowded drawer, or the basin that still held someone else's hair. In a UAE salon, where clients compare notes in group chats and read reviews before they book, hygiene is not a backstage chore. It is part of the service — and one of the cheapest ways to make a salon feel premium.

Clients do not expect a hospital. They expect to see care — and the details below are exactly the things clients mention when friends ask, "Is that salon clean?"

## The five-second scan every client does

Every client runs the same silent check the moment she sits down. Her eyes sweep the station: is the counter clear or cluttered with yesterday's tools? Is there a used tissue, a stained towel, a cup from the previous client? That first scan happens fast — and it sets the tone for the whole visit.

The fix is a reset between every single client, not just at the end of the day. Two minutes, every time: wipe the station, clear the tools, lay out fresh towels, check the floor around the chair.

## Make the clean visible

Hygiene only builds trust when clients can see it. A steriliser humming in a back room does no marketing for you. The salons clients rave about do the visible work in front of them: opening a sealed pouch of tools at the start of the appointment, wiping the chair arms while the client watches, laying a fresh neck strip before the cape goes on.

This is not theatre — it is reassurance. A client who watches her stylist take clean tools from a closed pouch stops worrying about the last client's germs and starts thinking about her own hair. Keep surface spray and a clean cloth at every station, and make it a habit nobody is embarrassed about.

## Towels and capes: the things clients actually touch

Nothing tells a client more about your standards than the fabric that touches her skin. A towel that smells of damp laundry, a cape with colour stains from three months ago, a headband that has clearly been used all day — these are the details clients remember and tell their friends about.

Set a simple rule: one client, one set. Fresh towels and a fresh cape for every appointment, no exceptions. In the UAE heat, towels dry slowly and laundry piles up fast, so keep a buffer stock of at least a full day's towels and store clean ones where clients can see them — folded, covered, and clearly separate from the used pile. A warm towel during a long colour service costs almost nothing.

## The basin and the chair: where skin meets the salon

The hair wash is the most intimate part of a salon visit, and it is also where hygiene failures are hardest to hide. A basin that still holds water from the previous client, a neck rest without a cover, a chair that has not been wiped since morning — clients feel all of it, literally.

Build the basin into the reset: rinse it between every client, wipe the neck rest, and use a fresh disposable neck strip or a freshly laundered towel under the client's neck. Check the chair too, because armrests and headrests collect product and dust through a busy evening — and the 7 PM rush is exactly when standards slip. Foot baths need the same treatment between every pedicure client.

## Tools that touch skin need a system, not a memory

Combs, brushes, razors, nail files and tweezers all touch skin, and each one needs a rule that does not depend on anyone remembering. The simplest system is three trays: clean, in use, and to be cleaned. Tools move one way only, and nothing returns to the clean tray until it has been washed and disinfected.

For blades, single-use is the honest answer: a fresh blade for every client, opened in front of them, and the old one straight into a sharps container. For metal tools like tweezers and nail nippers, a disinfectant soak and a UV steriliser cabinet are a visible, affordable investment. Stylists should also wash or sanitise their hands in front of the client before starting — especially after handling money, a phone or a door handle. Keep a short written checklist at each station: what gets washed, what gets sprayed, what gets replaced. New staff learn it on day one, and regulars learn that your standards do not depend on who is on shift.

## When a client asks, "Is this clean?"

Sooner or later a client will ask — about a tool, a towel, a product, or whether the salon is safe for her sensitive skin. This is not a complaint. It is a buying signal from someone who wants to trust you. Answer it directly, show her the routine, and thank her for asking. "Yes — this is a fresh blade, opened for you, and the tools come from the steriliser" is worth more than any poster on the wall.

The same honesty applies to enquiries that arrive after hours. Clients often message late in the evening asking about hygiene, allergies or sensitive skin before they book — and a salon that answers those questions the same night wins the booking. ## A one-week hygiene reset

Start small. On Monday, walk your salon the way a first-time client would and write down everything that looks tired — the stained cape, the dusty shelf, the basin that needs a scrub. On Tuesday, buy the buffer stock: extra towels, fresh capes, single-use blades and neck strips. On Wednesday, put the three-tray system on every station and teach it in ten minutes. On Thursday, add the two-minute reset to the diary as a real step between clients. On Friday, check the air conditioning filter and the steriliser, and put the cleaning checklist where the whole team can see it.

By Saturday, your regulars will have noticed. In a market where a new salon opens every month, hygiene is the quietest, cheapest way to stand out. Clients may come for the cut or the colour, but they come back for the feeling that someone took care of them.
