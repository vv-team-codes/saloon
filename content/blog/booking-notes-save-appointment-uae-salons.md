Most appointment problems do not start in the chair. They start at the booking, when a small detail never gets written down.

A client books a full head of colour over WhatsApp at 9 pm. Nobody notes that she had a reaction to a tint two years ago. She arrives on Saturday at 6 pm, the busiest slot of the week, and the stylist discovers the problem with ten minutes to go. The appointment is cancelled, the chair sits empty, and the client leaves upset. Nothing about the service itself went wrong. The booking note was simply missing.

In a busy UAE salon, the diary is not just a timetable. It is the salon's memory. The notes attached to each appointment are what turn a name and a time into a visit that runs smoothly.

## What a booking note actually is

A booking note is a short line of useful information attached to an appointment, written at the moment the booking is made. It is the handful of facts the stylist and the front desk need on the day.

The test is simple. If the person who took the booking is off sick on the day, could someone else read the diary and run the appointment properly? If the answer is no, the booking notes are not doing their job.

## The five things worth recording

Not everything needs a note. Five things do.

**1. What was actually booked — and what it depends on.** "Balayage" is not enough. Was it a full balayage or a partial one? Did the client send a photo? Is it a first visit or a top-up? A note like "partial balayage, photo received, roots done in September" tells the stylist exactly what to prepare.

**2. Anything that changes the service.** An allergy, a sensitive scalp, a previous reaction, a pregnancy, a recent keratin treatment, hair extensions, or a colour done at another salon last week. These details decide whether a patch test or a longer consultation is needed before the appointment starts.

**3. Time notes.** Some clients need more time than the standard slot. Thick, long hair takes longer to colour. A nervous first-time client needs a slower start. A client who always runs ten minutes late needs a buffer. Write it down at booking, when it is easy, instead of discovering it when the diary is already behind.

**4. The stylist and the price.** If the client asked for a named stylist, record it. If a price was quoted on WhatsApp, record the figure. Few things damage trust faster than a client arriving to find a different stylist and a different price from the one she was promised.

**5. The small preference that makes it personal.** She likes the chair by the window. She prefers no small talk. She brings her toddler on Saturdays. She wants her appointment finished by 4 pm because of school pickup. These details cost nothing to record and everything to forget.

## How to write a note people actually read

A booking note fails when it is too long to read or too vague to help. "Nice client, see notes" helps nobody. "Colour, long thick hair, allow 3 hours, patch test done 12 Nov" helps everyone.

Keep each note to one or two short lines. Use plain words, not shorthand only one person understands. Put the most important fact first — the allergy, the extra time, the deposit — because on a busy Saturday evening nobody reads to the end of a paragraph.

And keep the note where the appointment lives. A detail written in a WhatsApp chat, a paper notebook, or someone's memory is not a booking note. It has to be visible to whoever opens the diary.

## When the note changes the appointment

Good notes change decisions before the client arrives. A patch-test flag means the salon messages the client two days early instead of cancelling on the day. A note that a client needs a ground-floor chair means the right station is kept free. A note that a bride's trial is on Thursday means the senior stylist is not booked for a walk-in that morning.

In a UAE salon this matters even more: clients book across languages, across time zones while travelling, and often late at night. The person who answers the 11 pm WhatsApp message is rarely the person who greets the client on Saturday. Without a note, the detail dies in between. With one, the salon looks organised, and the client feels known.

## Making it a habit

The system only works if it happens every time. Three rules make it stick.

First, the twenty-second rule: whoever takes the booking spends twenty seconds adding the note before moving on. No note, no confirmed booking. Second, one place: every note goes in the diary entry, not in a chat, a notebook, or a head. Third, a weekly tidy: once a week, the owner or manager scans the coming week's diary and checks the notes make sense.

Teach this to new team members on day one.

## What never belongs in a booking note

A note should be factual and useful. It should never contain a judgement about a client, a comment about her appearance, a guess about her finances, or anything you would be embarrassed for her to read. Assume one day she will see it. Write it the way you would want your own appointment note written.

Medical details need the same care. Record what the client told you and what action it requires — "patch test needed" — not a diagnosis. Keep health notes brief, factual, and limited to what the stylist needs to know.

## Start with this week's diary

You do not need new software to start. Open next week's diary and read it the way a stranger would. For every appointment, ask one question: does this entry tell the stylist what she needs to know? Where the answer is no, add the note now, while there is still time to message the client and fill the gap.

Within a month the difference shows: fewer surprises at the chair, fewer cancelled appointments, and fewer clients leaving because the salon forgot something she told you. A diary full of good notes is a salon that remembers — and clients stay where they feel remembered.
