Every salon in Dubai has the same number printed on its door, its Instagram bio, and its Google listing. One number. The trouble starts when the people behind that number multiply. The receptionist answers in the morning, a stylist picks up the phone at lunch, the owner checks messages from home at night — and the client has no idea who she is talking to. She repeats her request three times, gets two different prices, and books somewhere else.

Sharing one WhatsApp number across a team is not a technology problem. It is a habit problem. Salons that get it right treat the shared number like a shared front desk: one voice, one record, one person responsible at any given moment. Here is how to set that up.

## Start with one phone, one owner per shift

The simplest mistake is letting everyone answer whenever they happen to see a message. Two people reply at once with different answers, or nobody replies because everyone assumes someone else will. The fix is a rota, written down and pinned where the team can see it.

Name one person as the WhatsApp owner for each shift — morning, afternoon, and evening. The owner is the only person who sends replies and marks messages handled during that window. Everyone else may read, but nobody else types. If the owner is on a break, duty passes to a named backup, not to whoever is free.

This matters most during the evening rush, when enquiries and walk-ins peak together. One owner keeps every conversation intact while the team focuses on the chairs.

## Give every conversation a note, not just a reply

A shared number fails when the history lives in one person's head. A client messages about a balayage on Tuesday, the receptionist quotes a price, and on Thursday a stylist has no idea the conversation happened. She asks the client to explain everything again, and the client feels like a stranger.

WhatsApp Business labels are the cheapest fix. Create a small set and use them every time: New Enquiry, Booked, Waiting for Reply, Deposit Paid, VIP, Do Not Disturb. Five or six labels are enough. Rule: no conversation is left unlabelled at the end of a shift.

Labels do more than organise. They let the next person on duty see exactly where every conversation stands and pick up without asking the client to repeat herself. For regulars, keep a one-line note — preferred stylist, usual service, any allergy. Clients notice when you remember, and notice even more when you forget.

## One voice means agreed answers, not identical robots

Clients should not be able to tell who is on shift from the tone of a reply. That does not mean everyone writes like a script. It means the team agrees on the answers that matter: prices, deposits, cancellation policy, hours, and how long each service takes.

Write these down in a short shared document — one page, not a manual. When a client asks "How much is a balayage?", everyone gives the same price range and the same next step. When a client asks "Can I come today?", everyone checks the same diary first. Consistency builds trust; a different answer from each person destroys it.

A short library of saved replies helps, but keep it small. Ten to fifteen replies covering the questions you answer every day — prices, location and parking, opening hours, deposit link, aftercare. Each should sound like a person, not a form letter, and end with a concrete next step: a time, a price, or a question.

## Protect the handover, not just the message

The riskiest moment is the handover between shifts. The morning owner knows a bride is waiting for a trial date. The evening owner does not. The bride messages at 7 pm, gets a vague reply, and books with the salon down the road.

Build a two-minute handover habit. Before a shift ends, the outgoing owner sends a short message to the team group chat — three lines at most: who is waiting for a reply, who still owes a deposit, and anything unusual, like an unhappy client or a service that ran long. Two minutes, every shift, no exceptions.

This also protects the salon when someone is sick or on leave.

## Keep personal numbers out of it

The moment a stylist gives a client her personal number, the salon loses the conversation. The booking never reaches the diary, and when the stylist leaves, the client leaves with her. It feels friendly in the moment and costs the salon later.

Make it a clear rule: all client conversations happen on the salon number. Stylists can build personal relationships at the chair, but bookings, prices, and changes go through the shared number. Clients prefer it: their message gets answered even when their stylist is off.

## What about after hours?

A shared number still needs rest. Nobody should answer enquiries at midnight, and clients do not expect it. What they do expect is an acknowledgement. Set a simple away message: the salon's hours, a promise that messages are read first thing in the morning, and one useful link. Many UAE clients message late at night after scrolling Instagram, and a warm away message keeps them from messaging a competitor instead.

In the morning, answer the overnight inbox first, oldest first. A client who messaged at 11 pm and gets a reply at 9 am feels looked after. One who waits until noon feels ignored.

## A simple way to start this week

This week, do five things: pin up the shift rota, create six labels and agree what each means, write the one-page answer sheet for prices and policies, start the two-minute handover message, and agree that every client conversation happens on the salon number.

After a month, check the results: unanswered enquiries, clients who had to repeat themselves, double-bookings from two people answering at once — all three usually fall sharply. Clients notice first — they stop saying "I already told someone" and start saying "you always reply so fast."

A shared WhatsApp number is not about the phone. It is about the promise behind it: whoever answers, the client gets the same salon, the same answer, and the same care. Get that right, and one number becomes one of the strongest assets a UAE salon owns.
