It arrives on WhatsApp at 10:40 on a Tuesday night, between a price question and a booking request: "Do you do Botox?" Maybe "and fillers?" follows in the next message. The client is not testing you. She has seen a friend's results, she has a wedding or a big event coming up, and your salon is the beauty business she trusts. So she asks you first.

It is a tempting question to dodge. Some salons leave it on read, some reply with a vague "we can discuss in the salon", and some hint that something can be arranged. None of those replies help. The honest answer, given well, does something surprising: it usually books the client anyway, for something your salon does.

## Why this question keeps arriving

Dubai and the wider UAE are among the most beauty-conscious markets in the world. Clients here are open about treatments and see results everywhere — at the mall, at work, on Instagram. For many, Botox and fillers sit in the same mental category as a facial or a blow-dry: something you book at a beauty place. The distinction between a beauty service and a medical treatment is not obvious to them, and it is not their job to know it. It is yours.

## What the honest answer actually is

Botox and dermal fillers are medical treatments, not beauty services. They involve injections, carry real medical risks, and must be carried out by a licensed medical professional in a licensed medical facility. A beauty salon should not offer them, or arrange them informally, unless it holds the proper medical licence and employs the right professionals. That is not a limitation to be embarrassed about. It is the reason a client can trust you with everything else.

The honest answer is also simple, and it fits in one WhatsApp message: "We don't offer Botox or fillers in the salon, because those are medical treatments that need a licensed doctor in a clinic. What we do offer is..." The sentence that follows matters as much as the no. A bare no ends the conversation. A no followed by a real alternative keeps it going.

## The reply that keeps the client

A good reply does four things in a few lines: it answers directly, explains why briefly and without lecturing, offers the closest thing your salon does well, and ends with a concrete next step — a named service, a price range and a time.

"Thanks for asking! We don't offer Botox or fillers here — those are medical treatments, so they need a licensed doctor in a clinic. What we can do is a skin consultation and a treatment plan for the same goal: smoother-looking skin, fresher eyes, that rested look before your event. Our hydra-facial with LED is AED 350 and takes about an hour — I have Thursday at 6pm or Saturday at 11am. Shall I hold one for you?"

Notice what happened. The client was not corrected or made to feel silly. She was given a reason she can respect, and offered something real, priced and bookable. Many clients who ask about injectables are really saying "I want a visible result before my event". Sell the result, not the syringe, and the booking often follows.

## What never to say

Some replies cost salons clients, and sometimes much more. Never quote a price for a treatment you do not offer, even as a guess — the client will hold you to it. Never say "we can arrange something" or hint at an off-the-books arrangement. That puts the client at risk and your salon's licence and reputation at risk too. Never dismiss the question with "that's not what we do" and nothing else; the client hears rejection, not honesty. And never promise results the alternative cannot deliver. A facial is not Botox, and a client who books one expecting injectable results will leave disappointed and will not come back.

## Turn the no into a booking

The clients who ask about injectables are some of the most valuable enquiries a salon receives: they have already decided to spend money on looking good. The skill is redirecting that intent toward services you genuinely excel at. Start by asking what result they want, not which treatment. "Is it the forehead lines, or more the tired look around the eyes?" The answer tells you what fits: a targeted facial, a skin-smoothing treatment, a brow and lash combination, or a full pre-event package with hair and makeup.

When the client is unsure, offer a fifteen-minute skin consultation as the next step, booked like a real appointment. Once a plan is agreed, confirm it in writing on WhatsApp the same day — service, price range, time — so she has something concrete to look forward to.

## Train the team on one answer

This enquiry will arrive when you are not there — at 11pm on a Friday, to the junior receptionist on her second week, or to a stylist mid-service who answers the phone. If the team has not agreed on one answer, the client gets a different reply every time, and the weakest reply is the one she remembers.

Write the reply down and keep it in the salon's saved WhatsApp replies, word for word, so anyone on the team can send it in seconds. Run through it once in a team meeting, including what to say if the client pushes back. The test is simple: any member of the team, at any hour, should be able to answer "Do you do Botox?" with warmth, honesty and a booking offer — without needing to ask you first. ## When a referral is the right move

Sometimes the honest answer is that the client genuinely needs a clinic, and the best thing your salon can do is point her to a good one. Keep the name of one trusted licensed clinic you would send your own family to, and offer it without awkwardness. Do not take a commission or dress it up as a partnership.

Then follow up. A short message a week later — "Hope the consultation went well — your facial slot is still open for Saturday if you'd like it" — turns a referral into a relationship. The client who asked about Botox and was treated with honesty becomes the client who trusts your judgement on everything.
