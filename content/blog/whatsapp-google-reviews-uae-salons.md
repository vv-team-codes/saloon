A new resident in Dubai Marina searches "salon near me" on a Thursday evening. Two salons look equally good. One has 214 Google reviews and a 4.8 rating. The other has 11 reviews and a 4.6. She books the first one without reading a single review. The number did the deciding.

That is how most UAE salon bookings are really won: in a city where everyone is new and nobody has a neighbour to ask, Google reviews are the trust. The salon with 11 reviews probably has hundreds of happy clients. It just never asked them.

WhatsApp is where the asking works best. An email lands in a promotions folder; a card handed over at checkout ends up in a handbag. A WhatsApp message arrives on the phone the client is already holding, from the number she already trusts, while she is still happy.

## Why the review never happens on its own

Most salon owners assume a happy client will leave a review. She will not — not because she is ungrateful, but because the moment passes. By the time she remembers the salon, the feeling has cooled. A review needs a small push while the feeling is still warm, and it needs to be effortless: every extra step loses another client.

## The right moment is the same evening

Timing matters more than wording. The best moment is the same evening as the appointment, usually between 6 and 8 pm, when the client is home, relaxed, and still enjoying the result.

The trigger is the mirror moment: when a client loves her cut, colour or facial, the stylist knows. Note it in the client notes, and the evening message writes itself — "You looked so happy with your balayage today."

Ask only the clients who were genuinely pleased. A client who asked for a fix, or who seemed unsure at the mirror, should get a care message instead — "How is the colour settling?" — and no review link at all. Sending a review request to an unhappy client is how a salon collects its only one-star review of the month.

## The message itself: short, personal, one tap

The message has three parts and fits on a phone screen without scrolling. First, a personal line about her actual visit. Second, one sentence explaining that a review helps other clients find the salon. Third, the direct Google review link — not the salon's Google profile, and not a link to the website. One tap should open the review box.

It might read like this: "Hi Sara, thank you for coming in today — your balayage turned out beautifully. If you have a moment, a short Google review would mean a lot to us and helps other clients in Dubai find us. Here is the link: [link]"

That is the whole message. No long thank-you paragraph, no discount offered in exchange, no "please give us five stars" — asking for five stars directly makes clients uncomfortable and can look like pressure. Clients who had a great visit give five stars on their own. Send it once, and do not chase a reply.

## Make the link effortless

The single biggest reason review requests fail is a bad link. Test yours before you send it to anyone. Open it on your own phone, from WhatsApp, and check that it goes straight to the review screen with the stars visible. If it lands on the salon's search result or asks the client to sign in twice, fix it first. Google's own review link generator gives you a short link that opens the review box directly — use that one, and save it in your WhatsApp quick replies so the whole team sends the same working link.

Put the same link on a small QR code at the mirror or reception desk, so clients can scan and review while still in the chair.

The same applies to clients who book late at night. A 24/7 AI receptionist for salons and beauty businesses in the UAE, like ZYNA, can make sure their review request still goes out the next evening.

## What never to do

A few rules protect the reviews you already have. Never offer a discount, a free service or a gift in exchange for a review. Google removes reviews that look bought, and a review that mentions a freebie does more damage than no review at all. Never write reviews for clients, and never ask staff or family to post them. A batch of reviews in similar wording can disappear overnight.

Never argue with a bad review in public, either. A calm, short reply — "We're sorry the visit didn't meet your expectations. Please message us on WhatsApp so we can make it right." — tells every future reader more about the salon than the complaint does. Readers forgive an unhappy review when the owner's reply is graceful; they do not forgive a defensive one. And never buy reviews: a sudden jump from 12 to 200 reviews, all in similar wording, is obvious to Google and to clients. Real reviews arrive steadily, a few each week, in different voices.

## Answer every review, even the short ones

Replying matters as much as asking. A short, personal reply to every review — "Thank you, Aisha! So glad you loved the keratin — see you in six weeks" — tells the client she was heard, which makes her more likely to return, and shows every future reader that a real person runs this salon. Set aside ten minutes each week to answer the week's reviews. Use the client's name, mention the service, and never paste the same reply twice.

## A simple weekly system that keeps it going

Reviews stall when asking depends on someone remembering. Make it a system instead. Each evening, the person closing the salon looks at the day's appointments and picks the clients who left happy. The message goes out the same evening, from the salon's WhatsApp number, with the saved review link. The next morning, someone checks whether any new reviews arrived and replies to them.

That is the entire system: pick, send, reply. Track one number each week — how many reviews arrived. A healthy target for a busy UAE salon is three to five a week; at that pace, a salon with 15 reviews today has over 150 within a year.
