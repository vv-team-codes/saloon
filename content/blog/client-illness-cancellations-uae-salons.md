It is 8:40 on a Thursday morning in December, and your diary is full. The first message is a WhatsApp voice note from a regular: she woke up with a fever, she was meant to be in your chair at 11 for a blow-dry and party makeup, and she is so sorry.

This is the moment your cancellation policy stops being a paragraph on a price list and becomes a real decision. Handle it well and you keep a loyal client — and probably still fill the chair.

## Why illness cancellations deserve their own rule

Most cancellation rules were written for a different problem: the client who forgets, double-books herself, or changes her mind. A deposit or a late fee makes sense there.

Illness is not carelessness. A client who cancels because she is sick has done you a favour, keeping a fever away from your team and your other clients. If your reply makes her feel punished for that, she will book her next party look somewhere kinder.

So illness cancellations need their own rule, written down before the next one arrives.

## The first reply: kind, fast, and free of guilt

Speed matters as much as kindness. A sick client is lying in bed wondering whether she has ruined your day. Aim to answer within fifteen minutes, even before the salon opens.

The reply has three parts. First, genuine concern. Second, reassurance that the cancellation is fine and there is no charge. Third, two or three real slots later in the week, so the rebooking is already in front of her.

Something like: "So sorry to hear that — please rest, and don't worry about today at all. There's no charge when you're unwell. Would Thursday at 6 or Saturday at 11 work for the same blow-dry and makeup?" No mention of the policy, no request for proof, no "as per our terms".

## Waive the deposit — once, and say so clearly

If your salon takes deposits for peak slots, say the deposit moves with the booking, not against it. "Your deposit stays on your account for the new date" is the sentence that keeps the client. "Your deposit is forfeited" is the sentence that loses her — and her friends, because this is the story people share.

The word "once" matters. Your rule can say the first illness cancellation in a season is always free, deposit protected, no questions asked. That gives your team a clear line — not an open-ended right to cancel every Saturday morning with a sore throat.

## Rescue the chair: the 20-minute refill rule

Kindness to the sick client is only half the job. The other half is the empty chair at 11, in the middle of your busiest week. A December blow-dry and makeup slot is sellable — but only if you move fast.

This is where your waitlist earns its keep. Most UAE salons already have one, even if it lives in a notebook: clients who wanted Saturday and could not get it. The moment an illness cancellation lands, the freed slot goes to the top of that list with a timed offer — "a slot opened today at 11, can you make it? I need to know in 20 minutes" — and if the first name says no, it moves to the next.

Offer it to one person at a time, with a short window, then move on. Two rounds and the chair is full again before 9:30.

## Rebook before she feels better

Here is the detail most salons miss: rebook in the same conversation as the cancellation, not a week later when she has recovered and forgotten you. A sick client offered two real slots while she is still in bed will often pick one on the spot — turning a lost booking into a moved booking.

If she is too unwell to choose, do not push. One gentle follow-up the next day — "Hope you're feeling better, those Thursday and Saturday slots are still open" — and then leave it. Two messages is care. Three is pressure.

## When illness becomes a pattern

Now the honest part. A few clients will discover that "I'm sick" is the magic sentence that cancels any booking with no cost. The pattern is easy to spot: always a Saturday, always the evening slot, always the day before, always better by Monday.

Your rule handles this without a confrontation. The first illness cancellation is free. The second in the same season gets the same kind reply, with the deposit held against the new booking rather than refunded. The third gets a calm, private conversation: "We completely understand, and of course your health comes first. For Saturday evenings we do need to hold a deposit, because that chair is our busiest of the week." No accusation — just a boundary, stated once, kindly.

Most clients never reach the third conversation. The ones who do usually sort themselves out: they start keeping their bookings, or they drift to a salon with looser rules. Either way, your Saturday evenings get easier.

## Write it down once, use it all winter

None of this works if it lives only in the owner's head. Write the rule down in one short paragraph, share it with the whole team, and pin it where the front desk can see it: reply within fifteen minutes, no charge the first time, deposit moves to the new date, freed slot goes to the waitlist with a twenty-minute offer window.

Welcoming illness cancellations also protects everyone else in the building: a client with a fever in a busy salon for three hours is a risk to your team and the client in the next chair. A line in your confirmation message — "If you're feeling unwell on the day, please let us know and we'll move your booking, no charge" — gives sick clients permission to stay home, and tells every other client this salon takes their health seriously.

December in the UAE is party season and the busiest fortnight of the salon year. Colds and fevers are part of it. The salons that come out of the season with their regulars intact are not the ones with the strictest rules. They are the ones whose clients know that being unwell costs them nothing — and that their chair will be waiting when they are better.
