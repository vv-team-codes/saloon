If you run a salon in Dubai or Abu Dhabi, you have received this message. It usually arrives late at night, on Instagram or WhatsApp, from an account you have never seen before: "Hi! I love your salon. I would love to visit and try your services. I can post about it to my followers in exchange."

Some come from genuine local creators who could bring you clients. Most come from people collecting free treatments. The difference between a good collaboration and a wasted chair is having a clear way to answer before you say yes or no.

## Why the freebie message arrives every week

The UAE is a creator-heavy market. Beauty content performs well here, salons are photogenic, and a treatment is an easy thing to ask for. For the person sending the message, it costs nothing to ask. For you, it costs a chair, a stylist's hour, and products — often at peak evening hours, in the slot a paying client would have taken.

That is why the answer should never come from whoever happens to be holding the phone. A free service is a business decision, and it deserves the same calm process as any other enquiry.

## Step one: read the request properly

Before replying, spend five minutes checking the account. You are looking for four things.

First, location and audience. Are their followers actually in the UAE? An account with 40,000 followers mostly based elsewhere will not fill chairs in JLT or Deira. Look at who comments, in what language, and whether they tag local places.

Second, engagement, not follower count. A creator with 3,000 followers and 200 genuine comments on every post is worth more to a neighbourhood salon than an account with 80,000 followers and twelve likes per reel. Bought followers are easy to spot: big numbers, empty comments, and a feed full of giveaways.

Third, relevance. Have they posted about salons, beauty, or local life before, or is your salon just the latest in a long line of free dinners, hotel stays, and treatments?

Fourth, tone. A professional request names the service they want, what they will deliver, and when. A vague "let's collaborate" usually means the same text went to fifty businesses.

## The three kinds of request

Once you have looked, almost every freebie enquiry falls into one of three groups.

The genuine local creator has a real UAE audience, posts regularly about beauty or local life, and asks for something specific. These are the only requests worth considering seriously.

The opportunist has big follower numbers, low engagement, and no local connection. They want a free treatment and will post a rushed story that disappears in a day. The polite answer is no.

The established creator has a large, genuinely engaged local audience and a rate card. They are not asking for a freebie — that is a paid conversation for your marketing budget, not your diary.

## If you say yes, set the terms first

A yes without terms is a gift, not a collaboration. Before the appointment is booked, agree in writing — a WhatsApp message is enough — on five points: which service is included, and only that service; what they will post, such as one reel and two stories; when it will go live, ideally within 48 hours; whether you can see it before posting; and that there is no plus-one, no extra treatment, and no promise of future free visits.

Pick a quiet weekday slot for the visit, never a Saturday or a peak evening hour. A creator who genuinely wants to work with you will accept a Tuesday morning.

When the post goes live, check it. Did they tag the salon, mention the location, and link to your booking? Keep a short note of who came, what it cost, what they posted, and whether any bookings followed. After three or four collaborations you will know exactly what a free treatment is worth to you.

## If you say no, say it kindly

Most requests deserve a no, and a no does not have to be awkward. A short, warm reply keeps the door open without giving anything away: thank them, explain that you are not taking on gifted collaborations at the moment, and point them to your booking link with the current price list. Many will book and pay. Some will disappear, which tells you everything you need to know.

Never argue or explain your margins. A salon that gives away its work teaches the market that the work is free — and your regular clients pay full price for the same chair.

## The middle ground: a paid trial

Between a free yes and a flat no sits the option that works best for most salons: a discounted first visit. Offer the creator a real but modest reduction — say twenty per cent off one service — in exchange for an honest post. It costs you far less than a free treatment, and it separates the people who want to try your salon from those who only want something for nothing. Someone who will not pay eighty per cent was never going to send you paying clients.

If the first visit goes well and bookings follow, you can talk about a paid collaboration with clear deliverables and a written agreement. At that point it is marketing, with a budget and a measurable result — which is exactly how it should be treated.

## Keep one decision-maker

The easiest way for freebie requests to leak through is when anyone on the team can say yes. Give the decision to one person — usually the owner or the manager — and make sure everyone else knows the reply: "Thank you, let me pass this to the manager." A request that waits a day is a request answered properly.

Log every request in one place, even the ones you decline. Over a few months the pattern becomes obvious — which accounts ask again, which services they want, and which collaborations actually brought bookings.

Freebie enquiries are a sign that people notice your salon — and a chance to show that your work has value. Answer them with the same care you give a paying client, protect your chair, and save your generosity for the people who already trust you.
