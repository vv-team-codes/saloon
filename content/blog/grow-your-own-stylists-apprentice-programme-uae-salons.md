Every salon owner in the UAE knows the feeling. You lose a stylist, you post the vacancy, and the same small pool of experienced candidates circulates between the same salons — each one a little more expensive than the last.

There is a slower answer, and it is the one the strongest salons in Dubai and Abu Dhabi quietly rely on: grow your own stylists. A structured apprentice programme turns keen beginners into loyal team members who know your standards — because they learned them in your salon, not someone else's.

## Why the experienced-hire market keeps letting you down

Hiring an experienced stylist feels fast, but it is often the most expensive way to fill a chair. A good stylist with a following knows their value, and the salary you offer is only the start — there is the higher commission expectation, and the quiet risk that they leave in six months and take their regulars with them.

Worse, an experienced hire arrives with someone else's habits: their own way of consulting and their own idea of what a finish should look like. Retraining that takes weeks. An apprentice, by contrast, learns your way from day one.

## What a good apprentice programme actually looks like

An apprentice programme is a written path with clear stages, so the apprentice always knows what comes next and you always know what they can be trusted with.

The first three months are the foundation. The apprentice shadows your senior stylists, masters the basin and the blow-dry, learns colour preparation, and studies how your salon consults a client before a single strand is cut.

Months four to six are the supervised floor. The apprentice takes model clients at junior prices, with a senior stylist checking every consultation and every finish. They build speed on simple cuts, root colour and blow-dries, and they learn how long a service really takes.

From month seven, the apprentice earns a junior column of their own — a small diary of real paying clients, at junior pricing, with targets that rise gently. By the end of the first year, you have a stylist who can hold a busy evening shift and knows your regulars by name.

## Who to hire as an apprentice

Hire for attitude, not experience. The best apprentices are rarely the ones with the longest CV. They are the ones who arrive on time, ask good questions, handle a difficult client with warmth, and genuinely want to learn the craft. Look for reliability and curiosity before technique — technique can be taught, but showing up and caring cannot.

Finding them is easier than most owners think. A post on your salon's Instagram, a notice in the window, and a word to your team will usually surface candidates. Give every serious candidate a paid trial day: you will learn more in eight hours of watching them work with real clients than in any interview.

## What it costs — and when it pays back

Be honest about the cost, because there is one. An apprentice earns a training wage, and your senior stylists spend real hours mentoring instead of earning. There are model costs and training products, plus the occasional redo. Budget for all of it openly, and protect the training time in the diary.

The payback starts earlier than most owners expect. By month four, an apprentice is already earning: blow-dries, fringe trims, root colour and junior cuts at junior prices, filling chairs that would otherwise stand empty in quiet weekday hours. By month eight, most apprentices cover their own cost — and unlike an experienced hire, they are unlikely to leave with your regulars.

## Training around a busy diary

Never schedule training in the Saturday rush or the 6 to 9 pm evening wave, when every chair is earning and every minute counts. Use the quiet hours instead: Tuesday and Wednesday mornings, and one model evening a week.

December is your party season, and it is the wrong month to start intensive training — but it is a wonderful month for an apprentice to support the team and learn how a busy salon stays calm. January, the quietest month of the year, is the perfect time for a training block, while the diary has room to breathe.

## Keeping them once they are good

Growing a stylist is only half the job. Keeping them is the other half, and this is where many salons quietly fail. An apprentice who has been trained well, paid fairly and treated with respect will usually stay — but only if they can see a future. Give them a written pay ladder tied to clear milestones: junior stylist, stylist, senior stylist, with the pay rise arriving when the milestone is met, not months later.

Add commission on retail and rebooking, and review their progress every month. Apprentices stay in the salon that invested in them and trusted them with real clients.

## The mistakes that kill apprentice programmes

A few traps catch almost every owner the first time. The first is using the apprentice as a cleaner and runner, with no teaching at all — they leave within weeks, and rightly so. The second is having no written plan, so training depends on whoever is free that day. The third is giving them five mentors instead of one, so nobody owns their progress.

Promoting too fast is just as damaging. An apprentice pushed onto a full-price column before they are ready will produce unhappy clients. And never freeze their pay: if their skills grow but their wage does not, the salon down the road will happily pay them what they are worth.

## Your first 30 days

Start small and start now. In week one, write the programme down: the three stages, the mentor, the pay ladder and the training hours you will protect each week. In week two, put the word out — Instagram, the window, your team. In week three, run trial days and choose one apprentice, not three. In week four, your first apprentice starts, with a timetable, a mentor and a first milestone already on the wall.

One apprentice, done properly, teaches you how to run the programme. By this time next year, that apprentice could be holding a chair through your busiest evenings, keeping clients your salon would otherwise have turned away.
