Walk past any row of salons in Dubai or Abu Dhabi and you will see the same hours on nearly every door: ten in the morning until ten at night, seven days a week. Most owners chose those hours once, years ago, and never questioned them again. Yet hours are one of the most expensive decisions a salon makes — every hour open costs wages, cooling and attention, and every hour closed is a booking you might have won.

The right hours depend on who your clients are, where you sit, and what your diary shows. Owners who get this right often find growth without spending a dirham on marketing — they stop paying for empty hours and open when clients are ready to book.

## Your diary already knows the answer

Before changing anything, look at your booking data for the last eight weeks — the diary itself, not your memory of it.

Check three things. First, which hours are genuinely full — booked at least three weeks out of four? Second, which hours are quiet? A 10am to 12pm block that is empty more often than full is a pattern, not a slow patch. Third, when do enquiries arrive that you cannot serve? Clients messaging at 8am for a 9am slot, or at 9.30pm for tomorrow morning, are telling you where demand sits.

Repeat the exercise by day of the week. In the UAE, Saturday is often the busiest day, Friday has become a working day for many, and Sunday can be surprisingly strong in residential areas. A salon near offices runs on a different rhythm from one in a villa community.

## Morning clients, evening clients, and the hours in between

Most UAE salons earn the bulk of their money in two windows: late morning to early afternoon, and the evening rush from around 5pm. The hours between them are where the money quietly leaks away.

Early mornings are the most misunderstood slot. A few clients want a 9am cut before work, but that demand is thin: opening at 9am for two or three bookings means paying a full team to stand ready for a handful of chairs. Some salons open with a skeleton team — one stylist and one receptionist — and add staff from 11am. Others stay closed until 10am and lose nothing, because early callers simply book later in the day.

Evenings are the opposite problem: demand is real and concentrated. If your 7pm to 9pm slots are full every day, that is a signal worth more than any marketing campaign — protect those hours, add a stylist for the evening shift, or extend closing by an hour on Thursdays and Fridays.

## Mall salons and high-street salons play by different rules

A salon inside a mall is tied to mall hours and footfall. Your busiest moments follow the mall's rhythm — weekend afternoons, school holidays, the evening crowd after work. Opening before the mall gets busy is usually wasted money, but staying open until it closes matters: the last hour of mall traffic often brings walk-ins fresh from shopping.

A high-street salon has more freedom, and that freedom is worth using: your hours should match your clients' lives, not a landlord's schedule. A salon in a family neighbourhood might do its best business on Saturday mornings; one near offices might find a lunch-hour trade in quick cuts. Test it — don't copy the mall salon down the road.

## The true cost of one extra hour

Owners often think of an extra hour as free if a stylist is already there. It is not. Count the full cost: wages for everyone on the floor, cooling and lighting a large space in UAE heat, and the wear on your team. A stylist who has worked nine hours does slower, less careful work in the tenth — and a mistake costs far more than an hour's revenue.

For each hour you are open, take the average revenue earned in that hour over the last month and subtract the staff and running cost. Negative week after week? That hour is a charity, not a business decision. Keeping a quiet hour is fine if it serves a purpose — a slot for regulars, say — but make that choice deliberately, not by default.

The same maths applies in reverse. If the last hour of your day regularly turns clients away, that hour is probably your most profitable — turning away three bookings a night, five nights a week, is fifteen lost clients a week. One extra hour on your two busiest evenings can be worth more than a whole extra day of quiet morning trade.

## Test a change for four weeks before you commit

Never change your hours on a hunch, or permanently in one step. Run a four-week test: pick one change — opening an hour later, adding a late Thursday, closing an hour earlier on Sundays — and tell your clients clearly on WhatsApp and at the desk.

Then measure three things: bookings in the changed hours, revenue per hour, and complaints. If the new hours fill up within two weeks, keep them; if they stay empty, the test has paid for itself. Tell regulars in advance and give them a reason — "from next month we open at 10am instead of 9am, so the team is fresh for your appointment" lands very differently from a door sign that simply changed.

## Sometimes shorter hours grow the business

Cutting hours can be a growth move. A salon open twelve hours a day with a tired team, half-empty mornings and a chaotic evening serves no one well. A salon open ten hours with a rested team and a full diary delivers better work, earns better reviews and keeps its staff.

The question is not how many hours you are open, but how many earn their keep. A salon that fills ten hours a day is stronger than one that fills eight out of twelve.
