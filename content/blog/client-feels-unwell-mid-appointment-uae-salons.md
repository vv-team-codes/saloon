It happens more often than salon owners admit. A client is halfway through a colour service, or twenty minutes into a facial, when she goes quiet. Her colour drains. She says she feels a little dizzy, or a little sick, and suddenly the whole salon is holding its breath.

Most of the time it passes in a few minutes — a glass of water, a few deep breaths, a pause in the service. But the way your team handles those few minutes decides whether that client ever comes back, and what she tells her friends about your salon.

A client who is looked after well in a worrying moment becomes one of your most loyal regulars. A client who is rushed or embarrassed in the same moment does not return, and she does not stay quiet about why.

## Why it happens in UAE salons

The UAE makes this more likely than most places. A client walks in from 40-degree heat into strong air conditioning, and her body is already adjusting. She may have skipped breakfast, or she may be fasting. She may be sitting still for two or three hours during a long colour or treatment. Add in dehydration, low blood sugar or a pregnancy she has not mentioned, and a faint spell is not a mystery. It is a normal human moment in a hot climate.

It means your team needs a plan. The salons that handle it well stay calm and treat the client like a person, not a problem.

## The first thirty seconds matter most

The moment a client says she feels unwell, or a stylist notices she has gone pale and quiet, the service stops. Not after this section of hair. Not once the colour is rinsed. Now.

The stylist stays with her and speaks calmly, in a low voice, without fuss: "Let's pause here. You're all right, we're going to take a break."

Recline her chair, or move her from the basin to a chair where she can lean back. Loosen anything tight around her neck. Offer water right away, and keep it within reach. In many cases, a few sips of water and a few minutes of sitting still are all it takes.

## Small comforts that help, and what to avoid

A cool, damp towel on the back of the neck helps. A sweet drink or a biscuit helps if she feels shaky and has not eaten, but only once she is sitting comfortably. Never offer food or drink to someone who is struggling to stay awake or who seems confused.


There are a few things to avoid. Do not give her any medicine, not even a painkiller, unless it is her own and she asks for it. Do not try to diagnose what is wrong, and do not insist she finish the service. And do not leave her alone while she is still unwell. Someone from the team stays with her until she is clearly herself again.

## Knowing when to call for help

Most faint spells pass quickly. A client who is alert and feeling better after a few minutes of rest and water is usually fine to continue, or to reschedule if she prefers. But call for medical help straight away, on 999, if she faints and does not come round quickly, has chest pain or trouble breathing, cannot speak clearly, one side of her face droops, or has a seizure. Call too if she vomits repeatedly, or if she says she has a serious medical condition and feels something is badly wrong.

Do not drive her to a clinic yourself — keep her comfortable, keep someone with her, and let the professionals take over. Keep the emergency number and the nearest clinic's name on a card at reception.

## After she feels better, let her choose

Once she is feeling like herself again, the next question is hers, not yours. Some clients want to carry on; others want to go home, and that is a perfectly good outcome. Never push a client to finish a service she no longer wants. A half-finished colour can be completed another day. Trust cannot.

Handle the money gracefully too. If the service is unfinished, do not charge for the full service — charge only for what was completed, or charge nothing and invite her back to finish it. That story is worth far more than one appointment fee.

Before she leaves, make sure she is steady on her feet, and offer to book her a taxi.

## The follow-up that turns a scare into loyalty

The same evening, send her a short personal message. Not a template, and not a sales message. Something simple: "I hope you're feeling better this evening. We were thinking of you. Whenever you're ready, we'll finish your colour — no rush at all."

That one message changes the whole memory of the day. Without it, she remembers feeling ill in a salon. With it, she remembers a salon that cared.

Record a short private note on her client record too, so the team knows next time: "Felt faint during colour — prefers a break halfway, likes water nearby." ## Preventing it next time

You cannot prevent every faint spell, but you can make them rarer. Offer water the moment a client sits down, not twenty minutes in. For long services, build in a short break halfway. Keep a few biscuits or dates at reception for clients who skipped a meal. And watch for the quiet clients — one who suddenly stops talking is often one who is starting to feel unwell.

During Ramadan, be extra mindful: fasting clients may feel weaker in the late afternoon, so shorter services suit them better.

Train the whole team on this, not just the senior stylists. The person who notices a client going pale is often the junior at the basin, and she needs to know she is allowed to stop a service and call for help. Three steps for everyone: stop the service, stay with her, and call for help if anything seems serious.

A salon is not a clinic, and nobody expects it to be one. Clients expect something simpler: calm, kindness and common sense when their body lets them down. Get that right, and a frightening moment becomes the reason a client trusts you for years.
