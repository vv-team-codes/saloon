For years the pedicure sat at the bottom of the salon menu. It was the quick add-on, the thirty-minute filler between a cut and a colour, the service nobody talked about and nobody charged properly for. That is changing fast across the UAE. Today, busy salons list pedicures as rituals, not chores — ninety minutes long, with a named therapist and a waiting list on weekends.

The shift is not an accident. Sandals are worn year-round here, feet are visible in every mall, beach club and poolside photo, and clients who once booked a pedicure before a holiday now want one every three weeks as part of their routine. Salons that treat it seriously are finding it one of the most reliable earners on the menu: repeatable, hard to do well at home, and impossible to rush without the client noticing.

## Why the basic pedicure stopped working

The old model was simple and it was broken. A client booked a pedicure, a therapist worked through a fixed list of steps as fast as possible, and the salon charged the lowest price in the area to stay competitive. Margins were thin, therapists rushed, and clients treated the appointment as interchangeable — they would go wherever was cheapest or closest that week.

Worse, the basic pedicure trained clients to expect very little. When nothing about the visit felt special, there was no reason to rebook with the same salon, no reason to pay more, and nothing worth recommending to a friend. The service filled a chair for half an hour and added almost nothing to the client's loyalty.

Smart owners started asking what would make a client look forward to this appointment the way she looks forward to her hair appointment. The answer became a full ritual, and the ritual changed the economics completely.

## What a luxury pedicure ritual actually looks like

A proper pedicure ritual is not a basic pedicure with a scented candle added. It is a different service, built around time, attention and small details the client can feel.

It starts with a proper soak, not a quick dip: warm water, salts and a few unhurried minutes while the therapist talks through what the client wants. Then real exfoliation, a gentle callus treatment, and a foot and lower-leg massage long enough to relax rather than a token rub. Nails are shaped with care, cuticles tidied, and the polish — or a clean, buffed natural finish — applied with the same attention a colourist gives to hair.

The small details matter as much as the service itself: a comfortable chair, a warm towel, a drink offered without being asked, and a therapist who remembers this client prefers no polish. None of these are expensive, yet together they make the visit feel like a treat rather than an errand.

## The pricing lesson: longer service, better margins

The fear that stops most owners is price: a ninety-minute ritual costs nearly double a basic pedicure. Surely clients will walk away.

In practice, the opposite tends to happen. A client who pays more for a longer, better service values it more, rebooks more reliably and complains less — and spends more while she is in the chair: a gel finish, a take-home foot cream, a booking for her next visit made before she leaves. The salon earns more per hour and the diary fills with appointments clients actually keep.

The trick is to keep the basic option on the menu. Some clients genuinely want a quick tidy-up, and turning them away helps nobody. The ritual sits above it as the premium choice, clearly described and honestly priced. Clients self-select, and the salon stops competing on the lowest price for the service that deserves better.

It also fits the UAE evening diary well: ninety minutes, one therapist, one chair, no processing time — ideal for the after-work peak, when every chair is fought over, and a calm midday escape when the salon is otherwise quiet. Summer brings sandals and beach trips; the cooler months bring weddings and open-toe shoes for evening occasions. There is no dead season for a service people can see every time they look down.

## Training the team to sell a ritual, not a chore

A luxury pedicure cannot be delivered by a therapist who sees it as the least skilled job in the salon. Owners who have made the shift say the same thing: the service is only as good as the person delivering it, and the team has to believe in it first.

That means proper training in foot care, not just nail painting — recognising when a cracked heel needs a different product, and when something should be referred to a podiatrist rather than treated in the salon. It means consulting before starting, explaining each step in simple language, and showing the client the result rather than rushing her to the till.

It also means paying for the skill: therapists who deliver a premium service should earn a premium share, or they will leave for a salon that values them. Treat pedicure specialists as experts, give them regular clients of their own, and let them build a following — the same way a good colourist builds one.

## Start small, then build the ritual

If the pedicure ritual is not yet on your menu, you do not need a renovation to begin. Start with one therapist, one chair and a clear ninety-minute service description. Train that therapist properly, price it honestly, and offer it to your ten most regular pedicure clients first. Watch what happens to rebooking rates over six weeks.

Then refine: ask clients what they loved, adjust the products and timing, and add a second therapist when the first is booked out. Put the service on your WhatsApp menu with a photo and a price, so enquiries can be answered in one message instead of five.

The pedicure will never be the most glamorous service in a salon. But a service clients book every three weeks, pay properly for and recommend to their friends is worth far more than its place at the bottom of the menu suggests. Treat it like a ritual, and it will start behaving like one — for your clients, your team and your bottom line.
