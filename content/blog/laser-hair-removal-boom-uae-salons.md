For years, laser hair removal sat on the edge of the salon menu. It was something clients asked about and owners referred elsewhere, usually to a clinic down the road. That is changing fast. Across Dubai and the wider UAE, salons are bringing laser in-house, and for many it is quietly becoming one of the steadiest revenue lines in the business.

The demand has been there for a long time. Smooth skin is a year-round priority in the UAE, where the climate, the beach culture and the dress code for a night out all push hair removal to the top of the beauty list. What has changed is the economics. Machines have become more affordable, training is widely available, and clients increasingly prefer to get everything done in one place they already trust. When a regular who comes in every fortnight for nails and facials asks whether you do laser too, every referral you give away is a client you are handing to someone else's diary. November through February is the natural season for it, when cooler weather makes it easy for clients to stay out of the sun after a session.

## What it actually takes to offer it

The first decision is the machine. Diode and Alexandrite systems are the common choices for the UAE market, and the difference matters because your client base is genuinely diverse. A machine that works well on light skin may not be the right one for deeper skin tones, and in a market as mixed as Dubai or Abu Dhabi, limiting yourself to one skin type limits your market. Before signing anything, ask the supplier for the full range of Fitzpatrick skin types the device is cleared for.

The second decision is people. Laser is not a service you can hand to a new hire on day one. The operator needs proper certification, supervised practice hours and a clear protocol for patch tests, settings and aftercare. Clients will ask direct questions — how many sessions, will it hurt, will the hair grow back — and the person answering them needs to be confident and honest, not reading from a leaflet. Many salons start with one trained operator and one machine, then add a second operator once the bookings justify it.

The third decision is space. You do not need a new building, but you do need a private room with a door that locks, proper cooling and a place to store the machine safely. Clients will not relax in a curtained corner. Privacy is part of the service here, and it is also a selling point: a calm, enclosed room signals professionalism in a way a shared floor never will.

## Pricing it so it pays

The biggest pricing mistake salons make with laser is treating it like a one-off service. Nobody buys one session and walks away smooth. The real product is a course, typically six to eight sessions, and the pricing should reflect that. Packages sold upfront do three things at once: they commit the client, they bring cash into the business in the quiet months, and they make the per-session price look reasonable when compared with a single-session walk-in rate.

A simple structure works best. Price a single session for the walk-in who wants to try it once. Price a package of six at a genuine saving, not a token one. And offer a smaller area, like underarms or upper lip, as a low-priced entry point for clients who are curious but nervous. Once they have had one comfortable session, the full package becomes an easy yes.

Be careful with discounting. Laser is an expensive service to run, and the machine, the maintenance and the operator's time all cost money whether the chair is full or not. A deep discount that fills the diary with one-session bargain hunters will not pay the machine off. Protect your margins and let the results do the selling.

## The honest conversation clients remember

Laser is also a service where honesty builds loyalty faster than any promotion. It does not work the same for everyone. Hormonal changes, hair colour and skin type all affect results, and a client who is told to expect eighty percent reduction over six sessions will be delighted when it happens. A client who was promised permanent, total removal will be disappointed even by a good result.

Build the honesty into the first consultation. Ask about medications, recent sun exposure and previous treatments. Do a patch test and explain what it is for. Give the aftercare in writing, in simple language, so the client does not have to remember it from a conversation. And set the expectation that a top-up session once or twice a year is normal, not a failure.

This matters doubly in the UAE, where word of mouth travels through WhatsApp groups and family chats at remarkable speed. One client who feels misled will tell ten people. One client who feels properly looked after will send her sister, her colleague and her mother.

## Fitting laser into a salon, not a clinic

The salons that do laser best do not try to become clinics. They keep the warm, welcoming feel of a salon and add the discipline of a medical-adjacent service. The front desk books it like any other appointment. The reminders go out on WhatsApp like any other appointment. And the aftercare follow-up, a short message the next day asking how the skin feels, turns a clinical treatment into a personal one.

That follow-up is also where rebooking happens naturally. A message that says "your next session is due in four weeks — shall I hold your usual Tuesday evening?" books the next appointment before the client has time to drift. It also catches the small number of clients who had a reaction and need advice, before a small irritation becomes a complaint.

If your salon already has the clients, the room and the reputation, laser is not a leap. It is the next logical service for the regulars who already trust you with their skin. Start with one machine, one trained operator and one clear package. Let the results build the demand. In a market where clients value convenience as much as quality, the salon that can do it all under one roof is the salon they stop shopping around for.
