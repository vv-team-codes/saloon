It arrives on WhatsApp at 9.40 on a Tuesday night: "Hi! I have a small dog — can I bring her with me to my appointment on Thursday?" The client is not testing you. She has a blow-dry booked, a dog that cannot be left alone for three hours, and no one else at home. She would rather ask an awkward question than cancel.

The answer you give in the next few minutes decides whether she shows up, whether she ever books again, and what she tells her friends about your salon. A flat no loses the booking. A careless yes can cause a problem you spend the rest of the week fixing. The salons that handle this well do neither. They answer honestly, explain the real reason, and offer something that works.

## Why this question is a UAE question

The UAE has more pet owners than it did five years ago, and many of them live in apartments with a single dog as their only company. Mall salons and high-street salons see the question most, because the client is already out with the dog — walking the dog, then popping in for a treatment — or because she has driven across town and cannot go home first.

Summer makes it sharper. From May to September, a dog cannot wait in a parked car, even for ten minutes, and many owners will not leave a small dog tied outside a shop in that heat. When a client asks to bring her dog inside, she is often solving a welfare problem, not asking for a favour.

## The honest answer starts with your real rules

Before you reply, the salon needs one agreed answer. Not three stylists giving three different answers depending on who picks up the phone, but a single policy the whole team knows.

For most UAE salons the honest answer is no, and the reason is not the salon's preference — it is the building. Mall leases, hotel locations and many tower lobbies prohibit animals outright, and the salon cannot override the landlord. Saying so is not passing the blame; it is giving the client a reason she can accept without feeling rejected.

If you are a standalone villa salon and the decision really is yours, decide it properly. A small dog sitting quietly in a carrier at reception is a different proposition from a large dog loose among colour bowls and hot tools. If you allow it, set the terms: carrier or lead, stays at reception, the client brings water and a blanket. A policy with terms sounds professional. A vague "bring her, it'll be fine" is a problem waiting for a busy Saturday.

## The four-part reply that keeps the booking

The best replies follow the same shape, on WhatsApp, on the phone, and at the door.

First, warmth before the boundary. "We love that you asked — and we'd love to see her, but we can't have dogs inside the salon." Leading with warmth costs nothing and changes everything. A reply that opens with the rule sounds like a refusal. A reply that opens with the person sounds like care.

Second, the real reason, in one sentence. "Our mall doesn't allow pets in the building, and we also have clients with allergies who sit right next to reception." One sentence is enough. Two or three reasons start to sound like excuses, and the client stops listening.

Third, offer the alternative you actually have. This is the part most salons skip, and it is the part that saves the booking. "If you come at 10 in the morning, your appointment is only 45 minutes — the quietest time." Or: "Our stylist can do a 30-minute express blow-dry, so she's only on her own for half an hour." Or, for a longer colour appointment: "Would a Saturday morning work instead, when your husband is home?" The alternative proves you were listening to the problem, not just enforcing the rule.

Fourth, close with the booking, not the dog. "Shall I keep your Thursday 6.30 slot, or move it to the 10 o'clock?" The question was never really about the dog. It was about whether Thursday still works. End there.

## What never to write

Do not promise to check with the manager and then never reply. A client who has to chase the answer twice will assume the answer is no and book somewhere else. If the policy is yours to state, state it in the first message.

Do not blame the client, even gently. "You should really arrange someone to look after her" is a lecture, and lectures do not book appointments. She already knows the dog is her responsibility. She asked because she has run out of options.

Do not make an exception you cannot repeat. Letting one regular's dog in on a quiet Tuesday, then refusing another client's dog on a packed Saturday, creates exactly the kind of inconsistency clients talk about in reviews. If you make an exception, it becomes the policy — so make it deliberately, or not at all.

## Turn the enquiry into a system

Once you have the answer, write it down. Put the exact wording into your WhatsApp quick replies so every team member sends the same message, with the same warmth and the same alternative. A client who messages at midnight deserves the same answer as a client who walks in at noon, and the only way that happens is if the answer is written, not remembered.

Log these enquiries too. If you are getting two or three a month, they are worth a line in your notes — and worth a look at your booking page. A short note on your website or booking link, "we love pets, but our building doesn't allow them inside," answers the question before it is asked and saves the client an awkward message.

There is a quieter win here as well. The client who asked about her dog has told you something useful: she is a dog owner with a busy life and limited free time. A note in her record — "small dog, prefers morning slots" — lets you offer the right appointment next time without her having to explain again. That is the kind of remembering that turns a one-time visitor into a regular.
