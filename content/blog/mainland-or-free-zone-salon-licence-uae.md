Every salon owner in the UAE reaches the same fork in the road sooner or later: mainland licence or free zone licence? It looks like a paperwork decision, but it is one of the biggest growth decisions you will make. The licence you choose decides where you can trade, who you can serve, how many visas you can hold and how easily you can open a second branch later. Get it right and the business grows without friction. Get it wrong and you spend years working around a structure that was never built for what you actually do.

The good news is that the choice is not as complicated as it looks once you ask the right questions. The bad news is that most owners ask the wrong one first: which is cheaper? Price matters, but it is the last question, not the first.

## What the two licences actually mean

A mainland licence is issued by the Department of Economic Development in the emirate where you trade — DED in Dubai, its equivalent elsewhere. It lets your salon trade anywhere in the UAE, serve walk-in clients off the street, and take premises in a mall, on a high street or in a residential community. For most salon owners, that freedom is the whole point of the business.

A free zone licence is issued by a free zone authority, and the salon operates inside that zone's jurisdiction. Free zones are famous for simple setup, packaged costs and quick processing, which is why they attract so many new businesses. But a free zone salon is generally limited to trading within the zone itself unless it appoints a local distributor or opens a mainland branch. For a business that lives on walk-ins and neighbourhood regulars, that limitation matters enormously.

## The three questions that decide it

Start with where your clients are. If your salon depends on footfall — a mall unit, a high-street shopfront, a community salon where mums pop in after the school run — you need a mainland licence. Clients will not drive into a free zone office park for a blow-dry. If, on the other hand, your model is a home-visit beauty service, a small studio inside a free zone business centre, or a specialist service sold mainly online to clients across the Emirates, a free zone setup can work well, provided you understand its limits.

Second, ask how you plan to grow. A mainland licence makes a second branch straightforward: same structure, another location, another lease. A free zone business that wants a high-street branch will need a separate mainland entity or branch anyway, which means paying for two structures. Owners who dream of a second salon in another neighbourhood often find their free zone licence becomes a ceiling rather than a foundation.

Third, consider your team. Mainland licences typically offer more flexibility on visa numbers, which matters if you plan to hire several stylists, a receptionist and a manager. Some free zone packages come with a fixed, small visa allocation, and adding visas later can be slow or expensive. If your growth plan involves building a team of six or eight people, check the visa maths before you sign anything.

## The cost picture, honestly

Free zone packages look cheaper on the first quote, and sometimes they are. A one-visa package with a flexi-desk can cost less than a mainland licence with a physical shop. But compare like with like. A salon needs a real shop, and once you add the cost of a physical premises, a mainland licence often closes the gap. Both have ongoing renewal costs; neither is a one-off payment.

The expensive mistake is choosing a licence on the headline price and then discovering the hidden costs of working around it — distributor fees, a second licence, or a forced move when the business outgrows its structure. Owners who have been through it usually say the same thing: the cheapest licence is the one you only buy once.

## The traps owners fall into

The most common trap is picking the wrong activity code. A salon licence covers hair and beauty services, but if you later add spa treatments, nail services or retail sales, your activity list needs to match. Adding activities later is possible, but it costs time and money, and some additions need extra approvals. Think about what you will offer in two years, not just what you offer on opening day.

A third trap is signing a lease before the licence is sorted. Some landlords and mall operators need to see your licence type before they will sign, and some locations only accept mainland licences. Sort the licence first, then the lease, not the other way round.

## If you already have one, can you switch?

The process takes weeks rather than days, and it usually involves new approvals, a new lease and updated visa arrangements, so it is not something to do casually. But it is not a life sentence either. If you chose wrong at the start, the fix is a project, not a disaster.

The smarter move is to treat the licence as a growth tool from day one. Before you sign, write down where you want the business to be in three years: how many chairs, how many staff, one location or two, walk-ins or appointments only. Then ask your business setup adviser to show you which licence structure gets you there without a rebuild halfway.
