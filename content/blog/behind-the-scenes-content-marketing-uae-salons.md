Open Instagram and search for a salon in Dubai, and you will see the same thing ten times over: a perfect blow-dry, a flawless balayage, a smiling client in a bright chair. The photos are beautiful, and they tell a new client almost nothing. She cannot tell which salon mixed that colour carefully or listened during the consultation. When every feed looks the same, the salon that shows its real work wins the booking.

That is what behind-the-scenes content does. It is the work itself: the colour being mixed, the consultation before the first snip, the chairs being set up in the morning. A client who has watched your stylist work before she ever walks in arrives already trusting you.

## What behind the scenes actually means

Behind-the-scenes content is your salon doing its ordinary work, filmed honestly. A phone on a tripod and thirty seconds of something real will do more than a studio shoot that looks like everyone else's.

The strongest material is the process. Film the colour being mixed and brushed on, the foils going in, the cut taking shape. People love watching skilled hands at work, and a client who has seen that care understands the price.

The consultation is the second goldmine. Film the first minute of a real consultation: the stylist asking what the client wants, checking the hair, giving an honest opinion about what will and will not work. New clients in the UAE are not looking for the cheapest chair. They are looking for someone who will listen.

The third kind of content is the least glamorous and often the most persuasive: the morning setup. Towels folded, tools laid out, stations wiped down, the team gathered for a two-minute briefing. It takes twenty seconds to film and answers the question every cautious client is asking: is this place careful?

## Why it works so well in the UAE

Dubai and Abu Dhabi are cities of newcomers. Every month, people arrive who need a new salon and have no recommendations to go on. They search, they scroll, and they judge on what they can see. A feed full of finished results gives them nothing to choose between. A feed that shows the people, the process and the standards gives them a reason to pick you.

It works just as well for clients who have been burned elsewhere. A woman who left her last salon with brassy colour is not shopping for a prettier photo. She is shopping for proof that this time will be different.

It is also cheap to make and hard to copy. A competitor can copy your offer, your price and your hashtag. They cannot copy your team, your morning routine, or the way your senior stylist talks a nervous client through a big change.

## Five clips that bring bookings

Start with the consultation, because it is the moment that decides everything. Once a week, ask a willing client if you can film the first minute of hers. Keep her face out of it unless she is happy to be shown. Name the service and a price range in the caption, and end with a booking prompt.

Next, film the process. A time-lapse of a full colour service, a close-up of a scalp massage, the careful sectioning before a keratin treatment. These clips get saved and shared, because people enjoy watching good work.

Then show the team as people. A stylist's birthday, a training afternoon, the junior learning a new technique from the senior. Clients book people as much as services.

Fourth, show the honest fix. A colour correction, a client who arrived unhappy with work done elsewhere, a restyle after a home dye went wrong. These are your most powerful posts, because they show skill under pressure. Always get clear permission and never name the other salon.

Finally, show the small rituals that make your salon yours. The karak offered at the door, the receptionist greeting a regular by name, the playlist that goes on at 5pm. These details are what clients describe when they recommend you to a friend.

## The rules that keep it honest

Behind-the-scenes content only works if it is real, so guard that. Always ask before filming a client, even from behind, and take no for an answer gracefully. Never use a filter that changes the result: a colour that looks different on screen than in the mirror is a complaint waiting to happen. Never film another salon's work and present it as your own, and keep other clients out of the background unless they have agreed.

One more rule: show the work, not the mess. A busy station mid-service is interesting. A dirty basin or a cluttered back room is not behind the scenes; it is just untidy.

## A simple weekly rhythm

One behind-the-scenes post a week is enough to change how your salon looks online within a month. Film in one twenty-minute batch: Monday morning, film the setup before the first client; Wednesday, film thirty seconds of a real service; Friday, film a team moment or a finished result with the stylist talking through what she did.

Write the caption like a booking message, not a diary entry. Name the service, say how long it takes, give a price range, and tell the reader what to do next: message us on WhatsApp and we will find you a slot. End every post with a next step.

The enquiries this content brings will not all arrive during opening hours. A client who watches your video at 11pm will message at 11pm, and if the reply comes the next morning she may already have booked elsewhere. ## How to know it is working

Give it six weeks before you judge it. Watch for messages that start with "I saw your video", clients asking for a service by name after seeing it done, and saves and shares on process clips. Track how many of those enquiries become bookings, and which kind of clip brings the best ones.

The polished photo will always have a place in your marketing. But the photo shows what the result looks like, and the behind-the-scenes clip shows what the experience feels like. In a city where every salon can post a beautiful result, the salon that shows its real work is the one a new client chooses. Start with one honest clip this week.
