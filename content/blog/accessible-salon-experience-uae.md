Every salon owner in the UAE knows the regulars who matter most are not always the ones who book the most expensive service. They are the ones who come back every three weeks without fail, bring their daughters and granddaughters with them, and tell their neighbours where to go. Many of them are older. Some use a walking frame. Some arrive in a wheelchair. How your salon treats those clients says more about your business than any mirror, menu or marketing campaign ever will.

For a salon, accessibility is not a building regulation. It is the client experience. A client who struggles at your door, cannot reach the basin comfortably, or feels rushed through a service because her body needs more time will not complain. She will quietly stop coming.

## Start at the door, not at the chair

The experience begins before a client reaches your reception desk. In a mall salon, that means knowing exactly which entrance is nearest, which lift to take, and where the accessible parking bays are. In a high-street salon, it might mean a flat entry or a small portable ramp rather than a single step that looks harmless to everyone except the person who cannot climb it.

Ask yourself one question: could a client in a wheelchair get from the car to your chair without asking a stranger for help? If the answer is no, that is the first thing to fix. Doors need to be wide enough for a standard wheelchair, and the path to the chair should be clear of product displays and waiting-room chairs pushed out at angles. Small changes, but they are the difference between a client arriving relaxed and a client arriving already tired.

When a client asks on WhatsApp, "Is your salon wheelchair friendly?" give a real answer, not a polite one — name the entrance, the parking and the step-free route.

## Inside the salon, comfort is in the details

A standard styling chair is too high for some clients and too low for others. A chair that adjusts smoothly, with a stable footrest, makes a real difference. So does a basin with proper neck support, because a client who cannot hold her head back comfortably will dread every wash, however good the shampoo is.

Think about mirrors as well. A mirror hung for a standing stylist is useless to a client seated in a wheelchair. A lower mirror, or a chair that can be turned so the client can see herself properly, turns a service from something done to her into something she can enjoy. The same goes for the waiting area: one chair with arms is easier to get up from than three armless ones. None of this needs a renovation.

## Give the service the time it actually takes

Elderly and less-mobile clients often need a little more time, and not because the service itself is slower. They need a few extra minutes to settle into the chair, to stand up again, and to walk to the basin without being hurried. When the diary is packed with back-to-back appointments, that time has to come from somewhere — and it usually comes out of the client's comfort.

The fix is simple: when you know a client needs more time, book it. A ten-minute buffer before and after the appointment protects both the client and the stylist. Note the need in the client's record, the same way you would note a colour formula, so she never has to explain herself again.

## Train the team, not just the front desk

Good intentions are not enough if only one person on the team knows how to help. Every stylist and assistant should know how to steady a chair, how to help a client to the basin without making a fuss of it, and how to speak to an elderly client directly rather than talking over her to the daughter who brought her. Nothing makes a client feel invisible faster than being discussed as though she is not in the room. Agree on a few simple habits as a team: greet every client at the door, offer an arm rather than grabbing one, never rush someone who is moving carefully, and check in during long services instead of assuming silence means comfort.

## The business case is stronger than you think

Some owners worry that thinking about accessibility means spending money on a small number of clients. The reality is the opposite. Older clients are often the most loyal clients a salon has: they book regularly, they keep their appointments, and they recommend you to people who trust their judgement. A grandmother who loves your salon brings her daughter, and her daughter brings her friends. In a market as competitive as Dubai's, that kind of word of mouth is worth more than any advertisement.

The changes that help a less-mobile client — a clear path, a comfortable chair, an unhurried service, a team that pays attention — make the experience better for every client. A mother with a pram, a client on crutches after a sports injury, a heavily pregnant regular: they all benefit from the same thoughtfulness. Accessibility is not a niche. It is hospitality done properly.

It starts before the visit, too. Many of the first questions about access arrive late at night, when a daughter is booking for her mother. ## Start with one walk-through

You do not need a project plan. Do one thing this week: arrive at your salon as if you were a client who moves slowly, and walk the whole journey — from the car park or the mall entrance, through the door, to the chair, to the basin, to the washroom, and back out again. Note every step, every narrow gap, every place where a client would have to ask for help. Fix the easiest three things first, and tell your team why.

Then, the next time an elderly client or a client in a wheelchair books in, notice the difference. A relaxed client is a returning client. And a salon where every client is genuinely welcome stands out — not because it spent the most, but because it paid the most attention.
