A client asks for a treatment you do not offer yet — a scalp ritual, a new colour technique — and you wonder if adding it would pay. A new service can grow your salon, but it can also lose money for months if priced on a guess and launched to everyone at once. The owners who get it right follow a simple discipline: cost it properly, price it with confidence, test it small, and only then roll it out.

## Start with the real cost, not a guess

Most pricing mistakes start here. An owner looks at what a competitor charges, knocks off a little, and calls it a price. That is how a service that looks profitable on paper turns into a loss once the month's numbers arrive.

Work out the full cost of delivering the service once. Add the products you actually use — weigh a few treatments and you will be surprised how much product a long hair service consumes. Add the stylist's time at their real hourly cost, including the quiet minutes while colour develops. Add the extras people forget: laundry, disposable tools, and the training you paid for.

Then add the cost of the chair. A two-hour treatment on a Saturday evening costs far more than the same treatment on a Tuesday morning, because that chair could have earned three quick services instead. Many owners find their new signature treatment is their least profitable service once they count chair time.

## Price from value, not from the salon down the road

Your neighbour's price is not your price — their rent, team, products and clients are all different. Pricing by copying them means inheriting their mistakes.

Price from the result the client walks out with. A treatment that solves a real problem — frizz that survives the UAE humidity, hair that stays healthy between colours — is worth more than a generic service with a similar name. Clients here will pay for expertise when they can see the difference. A vague service at a low price attracts bargain hunters. A clearly explained service at a fair price attracts clients who stay.

Be honest about where the service sits on your menu. If it is premium, price it like one and say why: the products, the training, the time, the result. If it is an affordable add-on, price it as one and keep the full version as the upgrade. The worst position is the middle — too expensive to feel like a treat, too cheap to feel special.

## Test it small before it goes on the menu

Never launch to your whole client list on day one. Pick twenty or thirty regulars who already trust you and fit the service — those who already ask about it, or whose hair or skin would benefit. Offer them the first appointments at the real price, with one honest condition: you want their feedback afterwards.

This test tells you things no spreadsheet can. How long does the service really take? Do clients like the result a week later? What holds them back? You will also find out whether your team is ready. A service your stylists enjoy delivering will sell itself. One they find fiddly or stressful will quietly disappear from the diary. Keep the test to two or three weeks. If it works, you have real before-and-after photos, real client words and a team that knows the service — far better marketing than any launch poster.

## Launch it to the right clients first

Launch in layers. Start with the clients who already showed interest, then the regulars most likely to try it, and only then the wider list. A launch to everyone at once creates a rush you cannot handle, then silence when the novelty fades.

Give the launch a clear story: what it does and who it is for. "For clients whose colour fades fast in the Dubai sun" is a story. "New treatment, 20 percent off this week" is not — and a launch discount trains clients to wait for the next one. If you want an opening offer, make it a package: the treatment plus a home-care product, or a follow-up check.

Time the launch well. A treatment that needs several sessions should appear a few weeks before the season it serves — the winter wedding season, Ramadan gatherings, the summer humidity peak. And protect the diary: if the service takes ninety minutes, hold those evening slots before regular bookings fill them.

## Watch the numbers for the first month

For the first four weeks, track five numbers: how many book it, how many rebook it, how long it really takes, what it costs per client, and what clients say afterwards. Strong first bookings with no rebookings means a problem with the result, not the marketing. Everyone rebooking but nobody new trying it means a problem with how it is explained at the chair.

Listen to the enquiries too — clients' questions reveal what your menu does not explain. If the same three questions keep arriving — how long it takes, whether it suits their hair type, what it costs — those answers belong in your service description. ## When to keep it, fix it or drop it

After a month, decide. Keep it if it rebooks well and earns its chair time. Fix it if clients love the idea but the delivery is off — maybe the appointment is too long, or the aftercare confusing. A shorter version, a clearer name or a better explanation can rescue a service that is nearly working.

And be willing to drop it. A service that fills the diary but loses money, or one your team dreads and clients forget, is not a failure — it is an experiment that gave you an answer. The costliest mistake is not a failed launch. It is keeping a service alive for a year because nobody wanted to admit it never worked.

Launching a new service is one of the cheapest ways a UAE salon can grow. No new rent, no new chairs, no new hire — just a better use of the team and the clients you already have. Cost it honestly, price it with confidence, test it on the people who trust you most, and let the first month's numbers decide.
