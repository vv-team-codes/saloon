It happens in almost every UAE salon, several times a week. A husband messages on WhatsApp to book a blow-dry for his wife. A mother books a facial for her daughter. A friend books a bridal trial as a surprise gift. The booking goes into the diary, the slot looks full, and everyone is happy — until the day arrives and the person in the chair is not the person you have been talking to.

A normal booking has one person at both ends: the person who books is the person who arrives. A third-party booking splits that in two, and every system in the salon quietly assumes the two are the same person. The reminder goes to the booker, who may forget to pass it on. The consultation notes record what the booker told you, which is often a guess. The deposit is in the booker's name, and on the day the client has no idea it was paid. It is a booking type most salons never plan for, so the team improvises every time — and improvisation is where chairs get lost.

In the UAE this is more common than in most markets. Husbands book treatments for wives, mothers book for daughters around weddings and Eid, and friends club together for a gift — often over WhatsApp late at night, in a mix of languages. No wonder a booking that looked simple on Monday becomes a confused phone call on Saturday morning.

## Capture four facts before you confirm

The fix starts at the first message, not on the day. Before you confirm a booking made for someone else, collect four facts and write them in the diary note. First, the client's name — the person who will actually sit in the chair. Second, the client's own phone number, if the booker is willing to share it. Third, the booker's name and number, so you know who to call if anything changes. Fourth, who is paying, and how: is the booker paying in advance, will the client pay on the day, or is it a gift where the price should never be mentioned in front of the client?

Ask these four questions in the same message, politely and once. Most bookers answer immediately, because you have made it sound like good service rather than an interrogation.

## Whose phone gets the reminder?

This is the single most common failure, and the cheapest to fix. If the reminder goes only to the booker, it depends on them remembering to forward it. If it goes only to the client, she may have no idea the booking exists and think the message is a mistake. Send the confirmation to both numbers when you have them, and keep the two messages different.

The booker gets the confirmation and the thanks, plus a note that the client will get a reminder too. The client gets the details and a warm welcome, with an invitation to reply if anything needs to change.

If you only have the booker's number, say so honestly: "I will send all reminders to you, so please pass them on — and share her number later if you can." That one sentence removes the most common no-show excuse in this category: "I did not know it was today."

## Deposits, gifts and the awkward price question

Money is where third-party bookings get genuinely awkward. A husband who has paid a deposit does not want his wife told the price. A friend who has booked a gift does not want the recipient asked for payment at the till. A mother who said she would pay may not be there on the day, and the daughter is left standing at reception with no wallet.

Decide the money at booking time, and write the decision in the diary where the front desk can see it. If the booker is paying, take the deposit from the booker and mark the booking clearly: gift, paid in full, or deposit paid with the balance due from the booker, not the client. If the client will pay on the day, say that to the booker in writing so there is no surprise for anyone.

The rule that saves the most embarrassment is simple: never discuss price with the client when someone else has paid. The receptionist should be able to see at a glance that a booking is a gift, so nobody asks the client for a card at checkout. One careless question at the till can undo a whole thoughtful gift. The same goes for privacy: the booker can know the time, the service and the price they agreed to pay — not what the client told you in the consultation. And if the client arrives wanting a different service than the one booked, listen to her; she is the one in the chair.

## The no-show risk is doubled

A booking with two people involved has two chances to fail. The booker forgets to tell the client. The client never saw the reminder. This is why third-party bookings no-show more often than ordinary ones, and why a deposit matters more here than anywhere else in the diary. A small deposit taken from the booker turns a casual favour into a real commitment, and gives you a fair reason to hold the slot through the busy Saturday evening rush. If the booker will not pay one, treat the booking as soft: confirm it with the client directly before the day, and be ready to release the slot if neither of them answers.

## One rule your team can follow

Third-party bookings do not need a complicated policy. They need one shared habit: every booking made for someone else gets four things in the diary before it is confirmed — the client's name, the client's number, the booker's number, and who is paying. If any of those four is missing, the booking is not confirmed yet; it is a pencil hold with a message to send.

Teach that habit once, and the whole category of problems shrinks. Reminders reach the right phone. Gifts stay surprises. Nobody is asked for money they did not agree to pay. A salon that handles this smoothly tells the booker they chose well, and tells the client she is welcome. Both of them remember that — and both of them are the kind of people who book again.
