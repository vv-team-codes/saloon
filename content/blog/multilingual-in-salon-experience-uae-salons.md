Walk into any busy salon in Dubai on a Saturday evening and you will hear English, Arabic, Hindi, Tagalog, Russian, French and half a dozen other languages. That is the ordinary reality of a UAE salon.

The trouble is that a salon visit is built on conversation. A colour consultation is a negotiation about shade, tone and upkeep. A first haircut with a new stylist is a small act of trust. When the two people in that conversation do not share a language, the whole visit wobbles. The client nods when she does not understand. The stylist guesses. The result is a surprise nobody wanted — and a client who quietly never comes back.

Most salons never notice the pattern. They see a one-time client and assume she did not like the result, when the real problem was that she never managed to say what she wanted.

## The consultation is where it matters most

The riskiest five minutes of any visit are the first five. That is when the stylist learns what the client wants. If that conversation fails, everything after it is built on a guess.

Slow it down. A rushed consultation in broken English is where mistakes are born. Keep your sentences short, and check understanding as you go — not with a vague "okay?", but with a specific question she can answer by pointing or nodding. "Shorter here?" with a hand at the jawline is clearer than a paragraph of explanation.

Watch for the polite nod. In many cultures, nodding is a courtesy, not agreement. If a client nods at everything, she probably understood very little. Repeat the key point once, slowly, in different words — and then confirm it another way.

## Show, don't just tell

The most reliable language in a salon is not spoken at all. Photos, mirrors and hands close the gap faster than any phrasebook.

Keep a small set of visual consultation cards at every station: printed photos of the salon's most-requested results — a soft balayage, a blunt bob, a natural brow — each labelled with the service name and a rough price range in English and Arabic. When a client points at a photo, you both know exactly what is being discussed. Add a few shade swatches for colour work and a simple length chart, and you have removed most of the guesswork from the busiest services.

Use the mirror as a second language. Hold up the back of her hair in the hand mirror before you start, and again halfway through. A client who cannot describe "two centimetres shorter" in English can still show you with her fingers — and that gesture is more accurate than any sentence.

## Build a team that covers the room

No single stylist needs to speak five languages. But a team that, between them, covers the languages of the neighbourhood is a genuine competitive advantage in the UAE — and clients notice it.

Map it honestly. Write down the languages your team speaks, at what level, and make that list visible at the front desk. When a new client books, match her where possible with a stylist who speaks her language. A client who discovers her stylist speaks her language relaxes in a way no discount ever achieves.

And train the whole team, not just the multilingual few. Every stylist should know the most useful phrases in the salon's three most common client languages — greetings, "how short?", "does this hurt?", "tea or water?" A little effort in a client's own language is remembered far longer than fluent English ever is.

## The small tools that close the gap

A few cheap habits remove most of the remaining friction. Keep a one-page bilingual service menu — English and Arabic, with prices — at reception and on WhatsApp, so a client can read what she wants before she has to ask for it.

Use translation apps openly, not secretly. Holding up a phone with a translated sentence is not unprofessional — it is considerate. The trick is to translate short, simple sentences, one at a time, and to read the client's face while she reads the screen.

Keep a shared note on each regular client: her preferred language, the stylist she liked, and the exact words she uses for her usual service. Next time, anyone on the team can start from where the last visit ended.

The same care should extend to the messages clients send before they ever walk in. A client who writes in her own language at ten in the evening still deserves a reply that understands her. ## Confirm the result before she leaves the chair

The last two minutes of a visit are the cheapest insurance a salon can buy. Before the cape comes off, show her the result from every angle and ask one real question — not "is it okay?" but "is this what you wanted?" If she hesitates, even slightly, treat it as information, not an insult. A small fix now costs five minutes; a client who leaves unhappy costs you her next ten visits.

Write down what was agreed, in the client's own words where possible, and keep it with her notes. When she returns in six weeks and you remember exactly what "a little lighter at the front, but not blonde" meant to her, the language barrier has effectively disappeared.

## When words fail, warmth still works

No system covers everything. There will be days when the right stylist is off and a client arrives speaking a language nobody on shift knows. On those days, attitude is the whole service.

Slow down, smile, and give the client your full attention. Never raise your voice, never laugh at a misunderstanding, and never discuss the client in front of her as if she were not there. Offer water, offer a seat, and take the extra five minutes. Clients forgive a clumsy sentence. They do not forgive feeling invisible.

A salon in the UAE is a small United Nations with hair dryers. The salons that win in this market are not the ones with the most languages on the wall — they are the ones where every client, whatever language she arrived in, leaves feeling that she was understood. Build the visit so it works without perfect words.
