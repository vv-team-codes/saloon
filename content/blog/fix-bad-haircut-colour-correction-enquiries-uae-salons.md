The message usually arrives with a photo attached and very little text. "Can you fix this?" Sometimes it comes at 11 pm, sometimes just before a wedding. The photo shows orange tones where blonde should be, a fringe cut too short, or a colour gone patchy after a home dye kit.

This is one of the most valuable enquiries a UAE salon can receive — and one of the easiest to mishandle. The client is upset, embarrassed and in a hurry. She has already had one bad experience, and she is deciding in a single conversation whether your salon is the rescue or just the second disappointment.

## Why correction enquiries are different

A normal enquiry is about a service she wants. A correction enquiry is about a problem she already has. She is not comparing prices; she is looking for someone who understands what went wrong and can be trusted to put it right.

That changes the tone of the conversation. A breezy "yes, come in anytime" feels careless. What she needs is calm, specific and honest: you have seen this before, and you will tell her the truth about what is possible before she sits in your chair.

Speed matters too. These enquiries come with urgency. A client with a colour disaster before a family event does not want a reply tomorrow. If your salon answers within the hour, you are already ahead of most of the market.

## The first reply: reassure, then ask, never promise

The worst thing a salon can do with a correction enquiry is promise a perfect result sight unseen. Colour correction is genuinely unpredictable. A stylist cannot know from one photo how many layers of old colour are in the hair, or how it will react to lightener.

So the first reply should do three things. First, reassure her: this happens, it can usually be improved a lot, and she has come to the right place. Second, ask the right questions. Third, book a consultation, not a full service.

The questions matter more than the reassurance. Ask when the colour or cut was done, what was used — salon colour, a home box dye, henna — whether the hair has been lightened before, and for a photo in natural light. Henna is the one to ask about directly, because it changes everything a stylist can safely do afterwards.

## The consultation is the service

Here is the part many salons get wrong: they book the correction straight into the diary like a normal colour appointment. That is how a two-hour job turns into a five-hour one, and how the stylist ends up rushing the most delicate work of the day.

A correction should always start with a proper consultation — in person, with a strand test where needed. Fifteen to twenty minutes, a named senior stylist, and a clear conversation about what is realistic. Can the orange be neutralised in one visit, or will it take two? Can the uneven cut be reshaped now, or does it need six weeks of growth first? Is the hair strong enough for another round of lightener?

That honesty builds the trust. A client told "we can fix about 80% of this today, and the rest in a second visit in three weeks" will trust you far more than one promised a miracle who leaves disappointed.

## Pricing a correction without an awkward conversation

Correction work is the hardest conversation in salon pricing, because the client is already unhappy about money spent elsewhere, and the fix often costs more than the original service. The trick is to talk about it before the appointment, not in the chair.

Give a range, not a single number, and explain what the range depends on. "Correction colour usually runs between AED 450 and AED 750, depending on how much colour needs removing and how many visits it takes — your stylist will confirm the exact price at the consultation before we start anything." That sets an honest expectation and removes the fear of an open-ended bill.

Never quote a correction price from a photo alone — the photo hides too much. And never discount a correction to win the booking. It signals the work is simple, and trains the client to expect cheap fixes.

## When the honest answer is "not yet"

Sometimes the hair cannot be fixed today. It might need rest, a conditioning programme, or simply time to grow. Tell her plainly, with a plan attached. "Your hair needs two weeks of conditioning first — come in for a treatment this week, and we will book the colour for the 25th." She may be disappointed in the moment, but she will remember that you protected her hair instead of taking her money — and clients who are turned away honestly come back, and bring friends.

A client you rescue becomes the most loyal client you will ever have. She arrived upset and vulnerable, and if you handle it well, she will never forget who fixed it. Her next five bookings, her mother's bookings, her friends' bookings — they often trace back to that one rescued afternoon.

## A simple correction enquiry routine

If your salon handles these enquiries often, write the routine down and train the whole team on it. It only needs five steps.

First, reply fast — within the hour, or the same evening after hours. Second, reassure her and ask the five questions: when was it done, what was used, has it been lightened before, any henna, and a photo in natural light. Third, book a consultation with a named senior stylist, never a correction straight into the diary. Fourth, give a price range and confirm the exact price at the consultation. Fifth, follow up the next day, and record everything in her client notes.

Five steps, practised by everyone who answers messages, turn the most stressful enquiry a salon receives into a reliable source of loyal clients. The client who arrives upset leaves relieved — and relieved clients are the ones who stay, who spend, and who tell everyone they know exactly where to go when things go wrong.
