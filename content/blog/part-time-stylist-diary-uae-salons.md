Every busy salon in the UAE has one. She is the stylist clients ask for by name, the one whose regulars plan their month around her. And she works three days a week.

Maybe she is a mother who keeps school hours, or a senior stylist easing back after years of six-day weeks. The reason hardly matters. The diary problem is the same: three days of frantic demand, four days of clients who will not book with anyone else, and a waiting list that keeps growing.

They squeeze her three days until she burns out, or they let her regulars drift away because "she's not in today" starts to sound like a rejection. A part-time star stylist is not a scheduling problem to solve. She is a diary to design around.

## Start with her real hours, not her ideal ones

The first mistake is building her diary around the hours you wish she worked. Sit down with her and agree her real, fixed days for the next three months, and write them into the diary as unchangeable facts. If she works Sunday, Tuesday and Thursday from 11 to 7, those are her days. Not "usually", not "unless it's busy". Fixed.

Fixed days let her regulars plan: a client who knows her stylist is in on Tuesdays can book her next visit before she leaves the chair. They also protect the stylist — a part-time arrangement only works if the part-time hours are respected, and one "just this once" Friday is how you end up with a full-time stylist quietly looking for another salon.

Agree the exceptions in advance too: how many Saturdays before the festive season, and what happens during Eid week, when every salon in Dubai is booked solid. Decide it now, while everyone is calm.

## Make her days the easiest days to book

Here is the counterintuitive part: the stylist with the least availability should have the most bookable diary. Her three days should be the easiest to fill, the quickest to confirm, and the hardest to waste.

Start by opening her diary further ahead than anyone else's. If your other stylists open four weeks out, open hers six or eight weeks out. A client who books her next colour six weeks ahead is a client who never drifts to the salon down the road.

Then protect her diary from the appointments that waste it: 15-minute fringe trims any junior could do, or consultations that could happen on a quieter day. Keep her diary for the services only she does well: the full colour, the bridal trial, the correction, the regular who has trusted her for two years. Everything else goes to the team, and you say so kindly at booking time.

## The waitlist that actually works

Every part-time stylist needs a waitlist, and most salon waitlists do not work. They are a notebook page, or a name in someone's head, and by the time a cancellation appears, nobody remembers who was waiting or what they wanted.

Run hers properly. When her day is full, offer the waitlist in the same message, and take three details: the service, the day, and the time window they can manage. A client who can come "any time Thursday" is far more useful than one who can only come at 6 PM. Keep the list in order, in one place, and work through it in order when a gap opens. Once clients realise the waitlist is fair and actually moves, the morning phone calls stop.

One rule matters most: a waitlist slot is offered once, with a short deadline. Message the first person, give them an hour or two to reply, and if they do not, move on. A waitlist that waits for people who never answer is just a slower way of leaving chairs empty.

## What to say when her day is full

The hardest moment is telling a loyal client her stylist has nothing available for three weeks. How you say it decides whether she books ahead, books with someone else, or books nowhere.

Never end on the no. "She's fully booked until the 12th" is a dead end. "She's fully booked until the 12th, and I can hold you a slot on the 13th at 5 PM — shall I?" is a booking. If the client cannot wait, offer a named colleague, not a faceless alternative: "Sara trained with her and does the same balayage technique. She has Thursday at 4." Clients accept a named person with a specific time. They do not accept "anyone else is free".

Keep one or two hold-back slots on her days too, released a few days before, so you have something real to offer the client who rings in a panic.

## Protect the rest of the diary too

A part-time star can quietly damage the rest of the team. When every client wants her, the other stylists' diaries thin out, their confidence drops, and the salon becomes dependent on one person who is only there half the week.

Guard against that. Pair her regulars with a second stylist for the small in-between visits — the blow-dry, the root touch-up — so the client builds a relationship with someone else too. Book her regular's follow-up gloss with a colleague, and over time the client trusts the salon, not just the stylist — so a sick day or a holiday stops being a crisis. She should be able to take a week off without the salon falling apart, and that only happens if the team has been meeting her clients all along.

## A diary that bends without breaking

A part-time star stylist is one of the best problems a salon can have: someone clients love enough to plan around, and a diary with genuine demand. The salons that handle it well treat her schedule as the fixed point and build everything else around it.

Agree her days and hold them. Open her book further ahead than anyone else's. Keep a fair, fast-moving waitlist. Never end a conversation on a no. Grow her hours slowly, with her agreement. And keep building the rest of the team, so the salon never depends on three days a week.

Do that, and her three days become the most profitable three days in your week.
