It happens on the worst possible morning. A Saturday in a busy Dubai mall salon, the diary full from 10 AM, and the basin in chair three starts leaking. Or the hydraulic chair will not go up. One station is out of service, and suddenly a third of the day looks impossible.

The instinct is to start cancelling. Resist it. A station out of service is a capacity problem, not a lost day. With ten calm minutes of re-planning, most salons can save nearly every booking. Here is how, in the order that works.

## The first ten minutes: triage, not panic

Before anyone messages a client, get the facts on one sheet of paper. Which station is down, and exactly what is wrong. Which bookings actually need that station, and which were only assigned to it out of habit. How long the repair will realistically take — a plumber on a Saturday in Dubai is not a ten-minute job, so plan around the worst case.

Then sort the affected bookings into three groups. First, the ones that genuinely cannot happen without that station, such as a full-head colour that needs the basin every forty minutes. Second, the ones that can move to another chair with a small adjustment. Third, the ones that do not need the station at all. In most salons, the third group is bigger than owners expect. A cut, a blow-dry, threading or a manicure rarely needs the broken basin — it just needs a chair, a mirror and a stylist.

## Move the service, not the client

The golden rule is to move the service around the client, never the client around the problem. Clients care about being seen on time, by the stylist they booked — not which chair they sit in.

Look at what each affected service actually needs, minute by minute. Colour processing time is dead time for the chair — the client can sit anywhere comfortable while the colour develops, and only needs the basin for the rinse. So a colour client can start in a spare chair, move to the basin when it is free, and finish without anyone noticing the shuffle. A cut booked into the broken chair can simply move to the next chair along. If a client is having colour and nails, the nail technician can work while the colour processes, and the diary suddenly has room to breathe.

Do not quietly downgrade anyone: if a client booked the premium chair and you move her to a standard one, say so honestly and offer something small in return.

## Borrow hours from the quiet parts of the day

When a station is down, the diary loses chairs, not hours. A UAE salon's day has a shape everyone knows: quiet late mornings, then the long climb from 4 PM into the 7 PM peak. The morning hours are your spare capacity.

Offer the affected clients the quiet hours first. A regular booked for 6 PM will often happily take 12:30 PM if you ask nicely and explain why — especially if you frame it as getting the stylist's undivided attention rather than the rushed evening slot. The clients who cannot move keep first claim on the evening chairs, and the ones who can move free up the peak.

If the diary is still full, reach for the waitlist: a quick message — "a slot has opened at 2 PM, would you like it?" — fills a gap in minutes. If a client agrees to move to tomorrow, book her into tomorrow's diary before she leaves the conversation — a moved booking is a kept booking.

## Tell clients before they arrive

Nothing damages trust faster than a client who drives to the mall, parks, walks to your salon, and only then discovers her appointment cannot happen as planned. The message must go out before she sets off, not when she is standing at reception.

Keep it short, honest and concrete. Name the problem in one line, give the solution in the next, and end with a choice. "Hi Sara, the basin at your usual chair is being repaired this morning, so we have moved your 11 AM colour to chair two with Aisha at the same time — nothing else changes. If you would prefer 1 PM instead, just reply and we will switch it." That message tells the truth, shows the work is already done, and gives her control.

Send it on WhatsApp, because that is where UAE clients actually read messages, and send it as early as you can. If a client does not reply, do not assume silence means agreement — call her.

## Use the downtime well

A station out of service still leaves a stylist with an empty chair for part of the day. That is not wasted time — it is the rarest thing in a salon: an unscheduled hour.

Use it for the jobs that never get done: a deep clean of the station next door, a stock check of the colour bar, a ten-minute training moment, or updating client notes.

## A small plan for next time

Every salon will lose a station eventually. The salons that handle it well are the ones that decided in advance what to do. Write a one-page plan and pin it where the team can see it. It needs four things: the name and number of the technician or plumber you trust, the supplier who can deliver a spare part the same day, the list of services that can move chairs and the ones that cannot, and the message template for telling clients.

Then do a five-minute station check once a month: test every chair, run every basin, check every light.

A broken basin is not a crisis. It is a test of how well the diary is really run — and the salons that pass it are the ones whose clients never even notice anything went wrong.
