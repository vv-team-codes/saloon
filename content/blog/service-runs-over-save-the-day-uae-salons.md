Every salon owner in Dubai knows the feeling. It is 4 PM, the diary is full until closing, and the colour in chair three — booked for two hours — is nowhere near finished. The lift did not take, the toner needs another round, and the client asked for a little more blending. Forty minutes later, the next client is waiting at reception and the whole evening is quietly unravelling.

An overrunning service is not the same as a late client. A late client arrives behind schedule; an overrun means the work itself took longer than the slot allowed, even though the diary was planned well. In a UAE salon — where the evening hours from 5 PM carry most of the day's revenue — one overrun can cost you three appointments.

An overrun is recoverable — but most salons recover badly, rushing the client in the chair, keeping the waiting client in the dark, or quietly cancelling the rest of the day. Here is how to handle it properly.

## Why services run over

Colour is the usual suspect. Hair coloured before, lightened at home, or longer and thicker than the booking notes said will process more slowly than the standard time. A client who books a "root touch-up" and arrives with six months of regrowth is not a root touch-up — and a client who mentions halfway through a facial that she has a wedding on Saturday has just changed the service entirely.

Then there is the cause nobody logs: the stylist who cannot say no. A client asks for "just a quick trim while the colour processes", the stylist agrees, and the next client's time pays for it.

Most salons never find out which services overrun, because nobody writes it down. Note it in the diary whenever a service runs more than fifteen minutes over, with the reason. Within a month, a pattern appears — and patterns can be planned around.

## The first five minutes decide the day

The moment you realise a service will overrun, the communication clock starts. Every minute a waiting client sits uninformed, she is deciding whether your salon respects her time.

The stylist should not break the news — she is mid-service and will either minimise the delay or promise a time she cannot keep. The front desk owns the message: the service before yours is running about thirty minutes over, here is the new time, and here is what we can offer while you wait.

A waiting client offered a drink, a comfortable seat, and a realistic time will usually wait happily. One ignored for twenty minutes and told "just five more minutes" three times will not come back. If the delay is more than thirty minutes, offer a real choice: wait with a small gesture — a complimentary express treatment or a blow-dry upgrade — or rebook with priority. Giving her the choice turns a delay into a decision she made herself.

## Triage the rest of the diary

Once the waiting client is looked after, look at the rest of the day. The instinct is to squeeze — shorten every remaining service by ten minutes and hope nobody notices. That is how one delayed client becomes five unhappy ones.

Instead, triage. Split the remaining diary into three groups. First, services that can move: clients you can call now and shift by thirty minutes. Most would rather shift with notice than sit in reception without it. Second, services that can be handed over: a blow-dry another stylist can finish, a manicure that runs in parallel while the colour processes. Third, services that cannot move — a bride, a client with a flight, a colour already started. Protect those, and build the recovery around them.

This is where a second pair of hands earns its keep. A junior stylist who takes over a rinse or a straightforward cut frees the senior stylist to finish the overrunning service properly. One floater on every busy shift is how salons recover in minutes.

## Never rush the client in the chair

The most tempting shortcut is also the worst: hurry the client whose service is overrunning. Rinse the colour early, skip the toner, cut the blow-dry short. The client can feel it, the result shows it, and her review will mention the rushed colour — not the diary you saved.

The client in the chair paid for a result, and she is watching the clock too. Tell her honestly — the colour needs one more round, twenty minutes — and keep her comfortable. A client who feels looked after during a delay becomes a loyal client; one who feels rushed becomes a lost one.

If the overrun is extreme — an hour or more — split the service. Finish what can be finished well today, and book the client back within a day or two to complete it, at no extra charge. Better than a bad result and a ruined evening.

## Build a diary that can absorb a bad day

Recovery is a skill, but prevention is cheaper. Three small habits help. First, stop booking colour and chemical services back to back with no gap. A fifteen-minute buffer after a long service is the cheapest insurance a salon can buy, and on quiet days it becomes a breather or a walk-in slot.

Second, quote honest durations. If a balayage on long hair takes three hours, the diary should say three hours — not the two and a half the client hoped for. Underquoting to win the booking is how a salon overruns every day.

Third, keep the overrun log. Once a month, look at it with the team for twenty minutes. If root touch-ups overrun every Thursday, the Thursday diary needs longer slots. The log turns a frustrating day into a decision.

None of this needs new software or a bigger team — just a front desk that speaks up early, a team that knows who does what when a service runs long, and a diary with enough air to bend without breaking. What changes is what happens next: a calm message, a triaged diary, a client who leaves feeling looked after.

That is the difference between a salon that loses an evening to one long colour and a salon that absorbs it and carries on — and your clients will notice it.
