Every salon owner in the UAE knows the client who books, then never quite arrives comfortably. She is the grandmother who needs a hand up the step at the door. He is the regular who uses a wheelchair and quietly checks whether the washbasin chair will work for him. She is the client who finds the noise, the dryers and the bright lights overwhelming, and leaves exhausted rather than relaxed. None of them complain. They simply come less often, or stop coming at all — and the diary never tells you why.

Accessible appointments are not a charity project. They are good diary management. A client who needs extra time or a particular chair is a client who will book again, bring family, and tell friends — if the first visit works.

## The booking question nobody asks

Most appointment books record three things: name, service, time. Accessible scheduling adds a fourth: "Is there anything that would make your visit more comfortable?" Asked once, warmly, at booking — never as a form, never as a medical interrogation — it gives the client permission to say what she needs. A ramp. A chair near the entrance. A quieter hour.

The exact words matter. "Do you have any accessibility needs?" sounds like a hospital form. "Anything we should know so we can set up properly for you?" sounds like hospitality. Train whoever answers the phone and WhatsApp to ask it the same way every time, and to record the answer in the client's notes the same day.

## Longer slots, booked honestly

The single biggest mistake salons make with accessible appointments is squeezing them into standard slots. A client who transfers from a wheelchair needs a few extra minutes at the start and the end. An elderly client may simply move at her own pace and dislike being rushed.

Book the extra time in the diary, visibly. Add ten or fifteen minutes around the service and label it — "extra time, Mrs A" — so the next stylist does not see a gap and fill it with a walk-in. The cost is one shorter slot; the return is a client who arrives calm, finishes unhurried, and rebooks on the spot. These often become a salon's most reliable bookings: same day, same time, week after week.

## The chair matters as much as the stylist

Not every chair in a salon suits every client. The chair nearest the entrance, with space beside it for a wheelchair or a walking frame, is worth more than the one with the best mirror light. The washbasin with a step is a problem; the one at floor level, or the chair that reclines gently, is the answer.

Do a walk-through of your own floor the way a client in a wheelchair would experience it: from the mall corridor or the street, through the door, to the chair, to the basin, to the payment counter. Note every step, every narrow gap, every heavy door. You do not need a renovation to fix most of it — you need to know which chair to book and which route to keep clear.

## Quiet hours are an accessibility feature

For clients with sensory sensitivities — including some autistic clients, clients recovering from illness, and older clients who find a busy salon overwhelming — the busiest hours are the barrier. The answer is not a separate service. It is a quieter slot.

Most UAE salons have one: the late morning on a weekday, the first hour after opening, or the slow Sunday afternoon. Offer it by name. "Tuesday at 11 is our quietest time — fewer dryers, softer music, no rush. Would that suit you?" It also fills a slot that would otherwise sit empty.

Keep the promise when they arrive. Lower the music in that corner, hold the small talk unless the client starts it, and let the stylist know in advance that this appointment runs at the client's pace. A quiet hour that is not actually quiet is worse than no offer at all.

## Record it once, remember it always

The difference between a salon that is accessible and a salon that feels accessible is memory. If Mrs A told you three months ago that she needs the ground-floor chair and a hand getting up, and she has to explain it again at every visit, she will conclude you were not listening.

Build the habit into the booking flow. When the appointment is made, the note goes in: "Wheelchair — chair 2, extra 15 min, quiet hour preferred." When the appointment is confirmed, the note is read. When the client arrives, the chair is already set. The receptionist, the stylist and the assistant all see the same note, so nobody has to ask the client twice.

## The team needs one script, not a policy document

Staff want to help; they are mostly afraid of saying the wrong thing. Give them a short, shared script for the moments that matter: greeting a client who uses a wheelchair ("Shall I move this chair so you have room?"), offering help without assuming it is wanted ("Would you like a hand, or are you comfortable?"), and handling the situation where the salon genuinely cannot meet a need.

That last one matters most. If your washroom is not step-free, or your chairs cannot accommodate a particular transfer, say so plainly and early — at booking, not at the door — and offer the closest honest alternative. A client who is told in advance can plan. A client who discovers it on arrival will not come back.
