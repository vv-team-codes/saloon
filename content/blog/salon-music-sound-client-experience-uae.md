A client walks into your salon after a long day, looking forward to an hour of calm. The door closes, and the first thing that reaches her is not the smell of shampoo or the sight of the chairs. It is the sound: a playlist turned up too loud, a dryer screaming three chairs away, a phone ringing at reception.

Most salon owners spend serious money on chairs, mirrors and lighting, and almost no time thinking about what their salon sounds like. That is a mistake. Sound is the one part of the experience a client cannot look away from. She can ignore a scuffed wall. She cannot ignore a song she hates at full volume while she is trying to relax.

## The first thing they hear is the door

Stand outside your salon for two minutes and listen. What does a client hear as she walks in? In many UAE salons the answer is a wall of noise: music competing with the air conditioning, a television nobody is watching, the buzz of clippers and the roar of dryers, all layered over each other.

You do not need silence. You need one clear sound at a time. Turn the television off unless someone is genuinely watching it. Keep the music at a level where a client at the next chair can hold a normal conversation without raising her voice. A good test: stand at the far end of the salon and try to talk to a colleague at normal volume. If you have to shout, the music is too loud. A warm greeting from reception in the first few seconds sets the tone better than any playlist.

## Match the music to the hour

A salon in Dubai does not sound the same at 10 in the morning as it does at 7 in the evening, and it should not. The morning client is often a mum with an hour before school pick-up, or someone fitting in a quick appointment before work. She wants calm and order, so slow, gentle music — acoustic, soft instrumentals, light piano — fits that hour.

By the evening, the salon fills up. Peak hours in most UAE salons run from around 5 to 9 in the evening, when clients arrive after work and before dinner plans. The energy is higher, the chairs are full, and the room can handle something a little more upbeat. The mistake is playing the same high-energy playlist all day, or letting whoever is nearest the speaker choose whatever they like.

Build three simple playlists and name them by the hour: morning calm, afternoon steady, evening energy. Keep each one at least three hours long so nobody hears the same songs twice in one visit.

## Volume is a service decision

Volume is not a personal preference; it is a service decision, and it changes by zone. The waiting area can be a touch livelier. The treatment rooms should be quieter — a client lying down for a facial with her eyes closed does not want to hear the reception playlist. If your salon has separate rooms, give them their own speaker and their own volume. If it does not, keep the whole room at treatment-room volume.

Volume also decides whether a consultation works. A client describing what she wants from a colour service needs to be heard properly, and she needs to hear the stylist's questions. If the music is loud enough that people lean in and repeat themselves, consultations get shorter and vaguer — and vague consultations are how colour goes wrong. Agree on a volume rule with the team and write it down: a number on the speaker, or a mark on the dial.

## The noisy tools problem

Some salon noise cannot be removed. Dryers, clippers and extraction fans are part of the job. But the worst of it can be managed. Old dryers get louder as they age; a dryer that has started to rattle or whine is telling you it needs servicing or replacing, and your clients hear it before you do. Quiet dryers cost more, but they are one of the best investments a salon can make, because a blow-dry is often the longest part of a visit.

Stagger the noisy work where you can. If two blow-dries are running at the same time next to a client having a quiet facial, move one of them. And watch the front desk: a phone ringing unanswered, or a team member taking a personal call in the middle of the salon, are sounds the client did not come for.

## The clients who want silence

Not every client wants conversation, and not every client wants music either. Some arrive exhausted and want to sit in peace. Others are on a call, or reading, or simply recharging. The best salons give these clients a way to have quiet without having to ask for it.

If you have the space, keep one corner of the salon a little calmer: the chairs furthest from the speakers, a softer light. If you do not have the space, a simple habit works just as well — ask at the start of the appointment. "Would you like music, or would you prefer it quieter today?" It takes five seconds, and clients remember being asked. A client who puts in her earbuds is telling you she wants her own world for a while. Respect it, keep the chat short, and she will leave feeling looked after, not ignored.

## A simple weekly sound check

Sound is easy to forget because it is invisible, so put it on a small weekly routine. Once a week, walk the salon the way a client does: from the door, to the waiting chair, to the wash basin, to the treatment room. Listen at each stop. Is the music clear or muddy? Can you hear the reception phone over it? Is there a dryer that needs attention, or a speaker that crackles?

The salons clients return to are rarely the loudest or the flashiest. They are the ones where every detail feels considered, including the ones you cannot see. Get the sound right and the whole visit feels calmer, more professional and more worth coming back for.
