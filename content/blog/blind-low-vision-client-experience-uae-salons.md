Most salon owners think about the client experience in pictures: the mirror reveal, the before-and-after photo, the colour swatch held up to the light. But some of your clients cannot see any of that. A blind client, or one with low vision, judges your salon entirely by how it sounds, how it feels, and how the people in it treat her. Get that right and you win one of the most loyal clients a salon can have.

## Start before she arrives

It begins on WhatsApp, long before the appointment. A client with low vision may use a screen reader, so your messages need to work when read aloud. Write addresses as plain text rather than sending only a location pin, and describe directions in words: "We are on the ground floor of the mall, next to the pharmacy, two minutes from the main entrance." A pin is useful, but a sentence she can hear is what gets her to your door.

When she books, ask one simple question and record the answer in her client notes: "Is there anything that would make your visit more comfortable?" Ask once, remember the answer, and never make her repeat it on every visit.

## The greeting matters more than the decor

A sighted client forms her first impression from your interior. A blind client forms hers from your voice. Walk up, introduce yourself by name, and say your role: "Hi, I'm Mariam, I'll be looking after you today."

Offer your arm rather than taking hers. Offer your elbow and let her hold it, then walk slightly ahead so she can follow your movement. Tell her what is happening as you move: "We're going up two steps," or "The chair is right in front of you, the back is on your left." Small warnings prevent awkward bumps on a first visit.

## Describe the result you cannot show

The mirror moment is the heart of a salon visit, and it is the moment most likely to fail a client who cannot see. The fix is simple: replace showing with describing. Instead of turning the chair and waiting for a reaction, describe what you did in concrete, sensory terms. "I've taken about two centimetres off the ends, the layers start at your chin, and the ends swing when you move your head." Let her touch her hair. Hands are how she "sees" a haircut, so invite it: "Feel the shape at the back — it tapers in here."

The same applies to colour and facials. "The colour is a warm chestnut, two shades lighter than your natural hair" gives her something real to take home and tell her friends. Honest, specific description is the whole service here — vague praise like "it looks amazing" means nothing if she cannot check it herself.

## Touch, sound and smell do the heavy lifting

Your salon is full of other senses to lean on. The wash basin is often the part blind clients remember most fondly, because warm water, a scalp massage and a calm voice need no eyesight at all. Slow it down slightly. Explain each step before it happens: "I'm going to rinse now, the water will be warm," and "I'm putting the conditioner in, it smells like coconut."

Sound matters too. A salon full of clattering tools, shouting across the room and a television at full volume is tiring for anyone, and far more so for a client who is listening for her cues. Keep the music low enough to talk over, and keep the conversation with her, not about her to a colleague across the room.

Smell greets her at the door. The chemical smell of colour and bleach can be strong and unsettling when you cannot see what is happening. A quick, honest warning — "You'll smell the colour now, it's normal and it fades in a few minutes" — turns something worrying into something expected.

## Safety is part of the service

A few practical habits make a real difference. Keep walkways clear of bags, cables and product trolleys, because a clear floor is a safe floor for everyone, and essential for a client using a cane or a guide dog. If she arrives with a guide dog, welcome it: offer water and a quiet spot beside her chair, and never distract or feed the animal while it is working.

At the basin, guide her hand to the chair arm and the basin edge before she sits, and tell her the water temperature before it touches her. At checkout, read the bill aloud and confirm the amount as you take the card. These are tiny courtesies, but they are the difference between a client who feels looked after and one who feels like a problem to manage.

## The booking she makes next

Here is the part owners miss: a blind client who has a good experience becomes a regular faster than almost anyone else. Finding a salon that treats her well takes effort, so once she finds one, she stays — and she tells the people who helped her find it. A recommendation from one trusted person carries enormous weight in the UAE's close-knit communities.

So make the rebooking easy. Offer her preferred time before she leaves, send the confirmation as a plain-text WhatsApp message her screen reader can read, and remind her the day before with the same clear details: date, time, stylist name and the address in words. ## A small checklist to start this week

You do not need a renovation or a training course to get this right. Start with five habits: record access needs in client notes, introduce yourself by name every time, describe rather than show, warn before water, heat or strong smells, and read the bill aloud at checkout. Train the whole team on those five, not just one stylist, because she may be looked after by anyone on a busy evening.

The salons that win are not always the biggest or most expensive. They are the ones where every client feels the visit was designed for her — including the client who will never see the mirror. She will feel the difference, and she will come back for it.
