It usually arrives late in the evening, as the salon is closing: "Hi, I have a job interview tomorrow at 10. Can you fit me in?" Sometimes it comes at 7 in the morning, with the interview at noon. Either way, the sender is nervous, short on time and about to make a first impression that counts.

Interview-prep enquiries are a steady stream for UAE salons. Dubai and Abu Dhabi are cities where people change jobs, arrive on visit visas and face hiring managers every week. A neat haircut, a tidy blow-dry or natural makeup can be the difference between walking in confident and walking in distracted by your own reflection. They are motivated, quick to book, and likely to return once the job is theirs. But a badly handled enquiry — a vague reply, an unkept promise, a drastic change the night before — costs you the booking and the client.

## Why interview enquiries are different

Most salon bookings are about a look the client has chosen for herself. An interview booking is about a look someone else will judge. She does not want drama — she wants the most polished version of herself, lasting through handshakes, a video call or a full day of meetings.

There is no second chance: a colour can be corrected next week, but an interview look cannot be redone before a 10 o'clock meeting. So the first job is not selling the biggest service on the menu, but understanding what the client needs, what time she has, and what can honestly be done in that window.

## The four questions to ask before you offer a slot

A good reply asks four things: when is the interview, and what time must you be ready? What kind of role — corporate office, school, hotel, creative studio? The right look for a bank interview is not the right look for a design agency. What do you want done — cut, blow-dry, brows, makeup, nails? And how much time do you actually have, including travel?

Those answers tell you what the booking should be. Two hours means a cut and a blow-dry; forty-five minutes in a lunch break means a blow-dry and a brow tidy, and nothing more. Asking shows you take her interview seriously. She is not just another booking — she is someone with a deadline, and you are treating it like one.

## The honest answer: what you can and cannot do overnight

The most valuable sentence in an interview enquiry is sometimes a no. A client who asks for a full colour change the night before should hear, kindly, that it is a bad idea. Colour needs a day to settle, a new fringe needs a day of living with it, and a dramatic new cut gambles with her confidence — the whole point of the visit.

That honest no is not lost business; it is why she trusts you. Offer the alternative in the same message: a gloss or toner to freshen her colour, a tidy-up of her existing cut, a blow-dry that makes her style look its best. All three fit the time she has, without risk. Clients remember the salon that protected them from a bad decision far longer than one that simply said yes.

## The interview-ready menu: small services, big effect

Once you know the timing, the menu almost writes itself. A blow-dry is the workhorse: fast, it photographs well and holds up through a long morning in an air-conditioned office. For men, a sharp cut and a clean finish do the same job in thirty minutes. Shaped brows frame the face on a video call, where the camera catches everything. Natural makeup — even skin, groomed brows, a little definition around the eyes — reads as polished without reading as made-up. Clean, neat nails matter too: hands are visible in every handshake and every gesture across a desk.

Price them clearly in the reply. Interview clients decide in minutes, and a price range with a time attached is what lets them say yes: "Blow-dry, 45 minutes, AED 120 to 150 depending on length, and we have 8:30 tomorrow." That one fast message books more interviews than a week of social media posts.

## Fitting them in: early mornings and lunch-break slots

Interview enquiries cluster in two windows: the evening before, and the early morning of the day itself. A salon that opens at 10 will miss the client whose interview is at 10. If one stylist starts at 8 or 8:30 on weekday mornings, you will catch bookings your competitors cannot. The same applies to lunch: a 12:30 to 1:30 express slot — blow-dry, brows, a quick tidy — serves the client interviewing at 2, and fills a chair that would otherwise sit empty.

When the diary is genuinely full, say so and offer the next best thing: "Tomorrow morning is full, but we can do a blow-dry and brows at 6 this evening — it will hold overnight if you sleep with it loosely tied." A client offered a workable alternative books. A client told "we're full, sorry" messages the next salon on her list.

## After the interview: the follow-up that wins a regular

It does not end when the client leaves the chair. A short message the next day — "Hope the interview went well" — costs nothing and is remembered. If she got the job, she has a salary, a routine and a reason to book every three or four weeks. If she did not, she will try again, and she will come back to the salon that treated her well when it mattered.

Keep a note of what you did, so her next visit feels like a continuation, not a restart. That is how a one-off nervous booking becomes a standing appointment, and how a salon builds a base of working clients who book early mornings and lunch hours.

Interview-prep enquiries are not a salon's biggest bookings, and they will never be the most dramatic transformations on your Instagram. But they are some of the most honest work a salon does: a person walks in anxious and walks out ready. Answer fast, ask the right questions, be honest about what the time allows, and she will remember who helped her feel ready. No discount buys that.
