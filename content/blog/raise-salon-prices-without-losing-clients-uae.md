Most salon owners in the UAE know the moment well. Rent has gone up, product costs have crept higher, and good stylists quite rightly expect better pay. Yet the price list on the wall has not moved in two years, because raising it feels like inviting clients to walk out. So nothing changes, margins quietly shrink, and the salon works harder for less.

A pricing reset does not have to be a dramatic event. Done calmly, with clear reasoning and honest communication, it is simply part of running a healthy business. Clients will accept a fair increase far more readily than owners expect — provided they understand what they are paying for and do not feel ambushed at the till.

## Why salons put off raising prices

The fear is usually bigger than the reality. Owners imagine a wave of complaints and empty chairs, but most clients judge a salon on the whole experience, not on a single number. If the cut is good, the appointment starts on time and the stylist remembers how they like their hair, a modest increase rarely breaks that relationship.

What does damage trust is surprise. A client who books a balayage at one price and is charged noticeably more at checkout feels misled, even if the new price is entirely fair. The same increase, explained a few weeks in advance, feels like a normal business decision. The difference is almost never the amount. It is the manner.

## Start with your real costs

Before choosing any new number, work out what each service actually costs to deliver. That means more than the tube of colour or the bottle of shampoo. Add the stylist's time, the chair time, rent for the hour, utilities, laundry, product waste, and the front-desk time spent booking and confirming the appointment. Many owners are surprised to find that a popular service they assumed was profitable barely breaks even once all of this is counted.

Look at your services one by one rather than raising everything by the same percentage. A quick blow-dry that turns over fast may need only a small adjustment, while a three-hour colour correction that ties up your senior stylist may be seriously underpriced. A flat increase across the board feels simple, but it can make your best-value services look expensive while leaving your most demanding work still underpaid.

## Decide what changes — and what does not

A good pricing reset is selective. Raise the services that are underpriced or most time-intensive, and leave a few everyday anchors steady. When a regular's usual cut or root touch-up stays the same while a specialist service moves up, the change feels measured rather than sweeping.

Think about how you present the new prices, too. Clients understand ranges better than single figures for work that varies, such as colour, extensions or bridal styling. A range with a clear explanation — hair length, thickness, or the complexity of the work — prevents the awkward conversation where a quote given on WhatsApp does not match the final bill. It also protects your stylists, who should never have to negotiate a price mid-service.

## Tell clients before the till tells them

Give clients notice, ideally two to four weeks. A short message at booking, a note at reception, and a brief mention at the end of an appointment are enough. Keep the wording simple and honest: costs have risen, you are adjusting prices from a specific date, and you remain committed to the quality they expect. There is no need for a long apology or a detailed breakdown of your expenses.

Train your team on one consistent explanation, so a client does not hear three different stories from three different stylists. The front desk should be able to answer the obvious question — why now, and by how much for my usual service — without hesitation.

## Timing matters in the UAE calendar

Timing a price change well makes it easier for everyone. The start of a new season, the return from the summer holidays, or the run-up to a busy festive period are natural moments when clients already expect salons to be busy and to refresh their menus. Changing prices in your quietest week, when clients are already price-sensitive, adds unnecessary friction.

Also consider your own diary. A price rise that lands the same week as a new stylist starting or a refurbishment can feel like too much change at once. Space changes out, and let clients settle into one improvement before you ask them to accept another.

## Protect your regulars

Your regulars are the foundation of the business, and a pricing reset is the moment to show them they matter. That does not mean discounting the increase away for everyone, which defeats the purpose. It means recognising loyalty in ways that cost little but feel personal: first notice of the change, a conversation with their own stylist rather than a generic message, and a booking kept at the old price if they rebook before the change takes effect.

Watch the clients who go quiet after the change. A regular who suddenly stops booking may not be angry; they may simply have drifted, or assumed the salon is now out of their budget. A short, personal follow-up message a few weeks later — asking how they are, not offering a discount — often brings them back. Losing a regular silently is far more expensive than the conversation that keeps them.

## What to do in the first month after the change

The first month is when you learn whether the reset worked. Track three things: rebooking rates for regulars, the average spend per client, and the number of price questions at the front desk. If rebooking holds steady and the average spend rises modestly, the change has landed well.

Above all, keep delivering the experience that justifies the price. A pricing reset buys you room to invest — in better products, in training, in keeping good stylists. Clients will forgive a higher price if the visit feels worth it. They will not forgive a higher price for the same rushed, unchanged experience.
