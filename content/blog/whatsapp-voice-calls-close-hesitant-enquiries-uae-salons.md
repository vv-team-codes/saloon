She has read every message, asked three questions, and still has not booked. Two days of messages — a question at lunch, an answer that evening, another next morning. She likes your salon and price — and is still not booked. A three-minute voice call would have closed it on day one.

This is the client WhatsApp typing quietly loses — she decides by talking: a busy professional between meetings, a mum typing one-handed, a client writing in a second language, a first-timer who needs a friendly voice before trusting a salon with her hair. The salons that win these clients know when to stop typing and start talking.

## The clients who will never book by typing

Some clients love messaging — price, slot, confirmation, done. Others exchange twenty messages and never decide, because every message gives them one more thing to think about.

Spot them early: the hesitant first-timer asks the same question twice — not confused, just wanting reassurance. The busy professional replies in fragments and never lands on a time; the client writing in a second language keeps messages short, and a long typed explanation only makes her more cautious. The bridal or colour-correction enquirer is not asking about price — she is asking if she can trust you.

For all of them, a voice does what text cannot — it carries warmth no emoji can, and lets you hear the hesitation and answer it in the moment.

## The five moments a call beats a message

Not every enquiry needs a call — offer one when you recognise these moments.

First, she has asked the same question twice — a signal, not a misunderstanding; she needs to hear the answer, not read it again.

Second, the price is the sticking point. Quoted in text it is a number on a screen; spoken aloud with the service, time and stylist attached, it sounds like a plan instead of a cost.

Third, the booking is complicated — a bridal party, a group of friends, two services back to back, a deadline before a flight. Moving parts settle faster in two minutes of talk than in fifteen messages.

Fourth, she has gone quiet after a quote — rarely a rejection; often she cannot say the price is more than she expected, or she needs a smaller first step.

Fifth, something has gone wrong — a late arrival, a changed plan, a service that ran long. Bad news by text reads colder than it is; a short, honest call protects the relationship.

## The three-minute call that closes

A closing call is short on purpose — clients agree because it is quick, and will say yes again if you keep it that way.

Always ask first — never call out of the blue. One message: "Would a quick call be easier? Two minutes, and I can hold a slot for you." Most say yes; a no tells you something useful — they prefer writing, so respect it.

Follow a simple shape. First thirty seconds: greeting and confirmation — use her name, confirm what she wants. Next minute: one question, not five — "What matters most, the colour, the timing, or the price?" Next minute: one recommendation, not three options — a named service, a named stylist, a time and a price range. Options delay; a clear recommendation decides. Last thirty seconds: the close — a specific slot, "Thursday at 6 PM with Sara, 90 minutes, AED 280 to 340," promised in writing.

Three minutes, one recommendation, one slot — that is the whole call.

## What to say — and what never to say

Say the price again, even if you already typed it, plus the stylist's name and the duration. If there is a deposit, say it plainly and explain why it protects her slot. If you are unsure, say so and promise the answer by a time — "I will check with Sara and message you by 4 PM" — and then do it.

Never pressure a client — "This slot will go" turns a warm call into a sales call, and she will not pick up next time. Never quote a different price from your messages, and never discuss another client's booking. Keep your voice unhurried and your language simple — many clients are choosing their words in a second language, so short sentences beat polished ones.

## After the call: the message that locks it in

A call without a written confirmation is a conversation, not a booking. Within five minutes, send one message with everything agreed — service, stylist, date and time, price, deposit, location pin — ending with: "Reply YES to confirm and your chair is booked."

That message gives her something to show her family, and your salon a record. If she does not reply, tomorrow's reminder is easy — you are confirming what she already agreed to, not starting over.

## When not to call

A call is a tool, not a habit. Do not call after 9 PM unless she asked you to, or twice about the same enquiry without a reply — one unanswered call is a message she has read; two is pressure. Do not call for a simple fact either: opening hours, a price list or a location are faster by text.

And never call a client who has said she prefers messages — some people choose WhatsApp precisely because they do not want to talk on the phone, and respecting that is itself a reason she will come back.

## Make it a team habit

Decide who calls: one person per shift owns the call list — enquiries going for more than a day, quotes that went quiet, big bookings that need a voice. Two or three calls a day is plenty, and each takes less time than the message thread it replaces. Note after every call what she booked and worried about — that note turns a good call into a loyal client, because next time your team already knows her.

The salons that fill their chairs are not always the fastest to reply — they are the ones that know which clients need a voice instead of a message, and pick up the phone for three minutes.
