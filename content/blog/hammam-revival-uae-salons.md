Walk through a salon in JBR or Sharjah on a Thursday evening and you may spot something unexpected on the menu board: the Moroccan bath. The hammam, a ritual far older than the malls around it, is quietly becoming one of the UAE's most requested salon services. For owners, the question is whether you can offer it well, price it honestly, and fit it into a diary already full by the weekend.

## What a modern hammam service actually looks like

A traditional hammam follows a simple sequence: steam warms the client up, black beldi soap softens the skin, a kessa glove exfoliates it, rhassoul clay draws out the build-up, and argan oil finishes with moisture. Sixty to ninety minutes later, the client leaves with smoother, softer, visibly brighter skin.

Most UAE salons have no full hammam house, and they do not need one. The salon version adapts the ritual to a private room: a steam cabinet, a treatment bed with drainage, and the same sequence of soap, glove, clay and oil. What matters is that the steps happen in the right order, with the right products, and with enough time to work. A rushed forty-minute version is not a hammam.

## Why it is working in the UAE right now

First, clients are tired of fast beauty. After years of express facials and thirty-minute fixes, more women in the UAE want to slow down. The hammam asks the client to lie still for an hour, and that stillness is part of what she is paying for.

Second, the climate. Heat, dust, sand and constant air conditioning leave skin dry and dull in a way a single facial cannot fix. Deep exfoliation followed by real hydration makes visible sense here, and the immediate result turns a first try into a monthly habit.

Third, cultural familiarity. Many UAE residents grew up with the hammam or a version of it — Moroccan, Turkish, Levantine or South Asian. For them it is a home ritual they have missed, not a trend. Meanwhile newcomers discover it through friends and social media. Nostalgia for some, discovery for others — that is what makes a trend durable.

Finally, the occasion calendar. Brides book it before a wedding; clients book it before Ramadan evenings, Eid, and holidays — a service tied to real occasions has a built-in reason to be rebooked.

## What it takes to offer it well

The hammam looks simple, which is why salons get it wrong. Start with the room: it must handle steam and water, with drainage, ventilation, and surfaces that can be cleaned between clients. A curtained corner of a facial room will not do; a steam cabinet in a ventilated private room is the honest minimum.

Training matters more than the room. The kessa glove is the heart of the service: firm enough to lift dead skin, gentle enough never to hurt. Scrub too hard and the client leaves sore; too tentative and she wonders what she paid for. Train with someone who knows the ritual properly, not from a short video.

Then come products and hygiene. Real beldi soap, rhassoul clay and argan oil are not expensive, but they are specific, and knowledgeable clients will ask what you use. A fresh kessa glove for every client, fresh linen, and a room reset between sessions are non-negotiable — the client's confidence in your cleanliness is part of the experience.

Be honest about what it is not: a skin ritual, not a medical treatment. Do not promise detox miracles, weight loss or cures for skin conditions. Softer, brighter skin and an hour of calm is already a strong promise.

## Pricing it honestly

The temptation is to price a new service low to fill the diary. Resist it. A hammam occupies a room and a therapist for sixty to ninety minutes, plus products, laundry, and fifteen minutes of turnaround. Work out the true cost first: therapist time, room time, products, linen, and the quiet hour you could have sold instead.

Then price it alongside your existing menu: if a signature facial costs a certain amount for an hour of a therapist's time, a hammam of the same length should sit in a similar range, often slightly above. Clients who value the service will not be put off by a fair price; a price that is too low signals the service itself is not worth much.

Keep the structure simple: one standard session, one extended session with a hair mask or longer oil massage, and a package of four for clients who come monthly. Packages work well here — the hammam is a service people repeat once they have tried it.

## Fitting it into the diary

A ninety-minute service with a fifteen-minute turnaround is a diary challenge in a salon busy on Saturdays and evenings. Place hammam sessions where they do the most good: quiet weekday mornings and early afternoons.

Pairing the hammam with a finishing service makes the slot pay: the client is already relaxed and in the building, often happy to stay for a blow-dry or manicure. Protect your peak hours, though — two hammam sessions on a Saturday evening can block the room for three hours that could have served six shorter appointments.

## Marketing it without overpromising

The hammam markets itself best through the ritual, not stock photography. Show your real room, your real products and your therapist's hands at work. A short video of the steam, the soap and the glow afterwards will do more than any poster.

Explain what is included before the client asks: how long it takes, what to bring, what happens step by step. A clear explanation on your WhatsApp or booking page calms nervous first-timers better than any discount. Then let reviews do the persuading: ask a happy client what her skin felt like afterwards and use her words, with her permission.

The hammam revival is not a gimmick. It is an old ritual finding new life where the climate, the culture and the calendar all point the same way. Salons that offer it properly — real room, trained therapist, honest products, fair price — are giving clients something they already wanted, and giving themselves a service that fills quiet hours and brings people back month after month.
