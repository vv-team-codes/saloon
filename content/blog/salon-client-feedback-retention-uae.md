Most salon clients never complain. They smile at the mirror, pay at the desk, say "see you soon" — and then quietly book somewhere else. In a city like Dubai, where a new salon opens every week and a client can find five alternatives within a ten-minute drive, that silence is the most expensive thing in your business. You never get the chance to fix what you never heard about.

The salons that keep their chairs full are not the ones with the fewest mistakes. They are the ones that find out about problems while the client is still theirs to keep. And the tool they use is almost embarrassingly simple: they ask.

## Why feedback is a retention tool, not a survey

Feedback in most salons arrives too late or not at all. A long online survey sent three days after the visit gets ignored. A comment card left by the till gets filled in by almost nobody. Meanwhile, the real information — the colour was slightly warmer than she wanted, the wait was twenty minutes longer than promised — walks out of the door with the client and never comes back.

The point of asking is not to collect data. It is to catch the small disappointments before they become decisions. A client who tells you something was not quite right is a client who is still giving you a chance.

## Ask one question, at the right moment

The salons that get real answers keep it to one question, asked at the right moment. Not a form, not a rating scale, just one open question while the visit is still fresh.

The best moment is the last two minutes of the appointment, when the stylist steps back and the client looks at the finished result. A simple "Is there anything you'd change about today?" works far better than "Are you happy?" — because "are you happy?" invites a polite yes, while "what would you change?" gives permission to be honest. The second-best moment is a short WhatsApp message the same evening, when the client is home and has lived with the result for a few hours.

What does not work is asking a week later, when the visit is forgotten, or asking in a way that feels like a performance review. Keep it human, keep it short, and keep it in the client's own words. One genuine answer is worth more than fifty five-star ratings.

## Listen for the quiet complaints

The answers that matter most are rarely dramatic. Nobody says "this was terrible." They say "it was nice, just a bit shorter than I expected" or "the colour is lovely, maybe a touch darker next time" or simply "all good" in a tone that does not quite match the words. These are the quiet complaints, and they are the ones that decide whether a client returns.

Train your team to hear them. When a client hesitates before answering, that hesitation is the answer. When a regular who used to chat all the way through a blow-dry suddenly goes quiet, something has changed. Teach stylists to note these moments — a one-line note on the client card, a quick message to the front desk — so the salon remembers what the client never said outright.

## Close the loop — fast, and personally

Asking and then doing nothing is worse than never asking at all. A client who took the trouble to tell you something and hears nothing back learns that her opinion does not matter, and she will not offer it a second time.

Closing the loop does not mean a grand gesture. It means a personal reply within a day: a short WhatsApp message from the salon, not a template, thanking her and saying what will change. "Thank you for telling me the wait was too long — I've adjusted Thursday's diary so your next visit starts on time." That one message does three things: it proves the feedback was heard, it shows a concrete fix, and it gives the client a reason to book the next appointment instead of trying the salon down the road.

For feedback that points to a real service issue, the follow-up matters even more. A complimentary fix, offered without being asked for, turns a disappointed client into a loyal one. But the fix alone is not enough — the client needs to know the salon took her seriously. A short, honest message from the owner or the senior stylist carries weight that a free treatment on its own never will.

## What to do with what you hear

Feedback is only useful if it changes something. Once a week, spend fifteen minutes looking at what clients actually said. You are not looking for a score; you are looking for patterns. Three clients mentioning long waits in the same week is a diary problem. Two clients asking about a service you do not offer is a menu opportunity.

Write the patterns down, act on one of them each week, and tell the team what changed and why. When stylists see that client feedback leads to real decisions — a new quiet hour, a different shampoo, a longer consultation for colour — they start listening harder during appointments. The whole salon gets better at hearing what clients are really saying, and the clients feel it.

## Make it a habit, not a campaign

The salons that lose clients to silence are usually the ones that only ask for feedback when something has gone wrong, or when a review site sends a reminder. By then it is too late. The ones that keep clients ask every time, as part of the visit, the way they offer water or confirm the next appointment.

Build it into the routine: the stylist asks the question at the chair, the front desk sends the same-evening message, and the owner reviews the answers every Monday morning. No software required, no survey platform, no extra cost — just a habit, repeated until it is part of how the salon works.
