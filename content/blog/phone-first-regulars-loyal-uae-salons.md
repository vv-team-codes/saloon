In most UAE salons today, WhatsApp does the heavy lifting. But the phone still rings. And when it does, it is often the client who matters most: the woman who has been coming every three weeks for four years, or the professional who wants a quick word with a human being before she commits her Saturday evening.

She does not want a booking link. She wants a voice, a name she recognises, and an answer in thirty seconds. Salons that treat that call as an interruption quietly push their most loyal clients towards the salon down the road. Salons that treat it as the relationship it is keep those clients for years.

## The clients who will never tap a link

Phone-first clients are not a small, fading group. In a typical Dubai or Abu Dhabi salon they include long-standing regulars who booked by phone long before WhatsApp existed, older clients who find typing slow, and busy professionals who would rather speak than write.

They are also, very often, your best clients. A phone-first regular usually books the same slot at the same time of week, spends steadily rather than chasing offers, and brings family members with her. She does not shop around on price, because she is not booking through an app that shows her five competitors at once.

## Never make the phone feel like the slow lane

The fastest way to lose a phone-first regular is to make her feel like a nuisance. A sigh when the phone rings, a hurried "can you just WhatsApp us?", or a request to repeat details she has given a dozen times all send the same message: your way of booking is wrong.

Train the whole team on this. Whoever answers the phone uses the client's name, confirms the slot before ending the call, and never asks her to switch channels to finish the booking. If she calls, she books by phone. That is the whole policy, and it should be written down where every new starter can see it.

## Answer like the booking depends on it, because it does

The hardest hours for phone answering in a UAE salon are the peak evening hours, roughly five to nine, when every chair is full. That is exactly when your regulars are calling, often from the car on the way home from work, trying to secure a slot before the weekend.

Give one person ownership of the phone on every shift. The rule is simple: answer within three rings, or call back within ten minutes. Check the missed-call list twice a day and return every missed call the same day. If a client has called twice without reaching you, she goes to the top of the callback list. A missed call that is never returned is a client halfway out the door, and she will not complain. She will simply book somewhere else next time.

After hours, the phone still matters. Many regulars try to book late at night, after the children are asleep or after a long day. A warm recorded message promising a reply first thing in the morning is far better than silence, and a 24/7 AI receptionist like ZYNA can make sure the enquiry is answered and the booking captured even when the salon phone is unattended.

## Write down how she likes to be reached

Client notes are the quiet engine of retention, and the phone preference belongs at the top of them. Next to her colour formula and her usual stylist, note the simple facts: prefers calls, not WhatsApp. Call after eleven in the morning. She likes a reminder call the day before, not a message.

## Rebook her before she has to call

The best way to keep a phone-first regular is to make sure she rarely needs to chase you. At checkout, book the next visit together before she leaves the chair. For a phone-first client, add one more step: offer to call her.

"Shall I ring you on Tuesday to confirm your usual Thursday slot?" is a small sentence that does a lot of work. It tells her the salon is looking after her diary, and it removes the one moment where a busy week could break the habit. Pair a standing slot with a short confirmation call and the rhythm holds itself. She does not need to remember to book. The salon remembers for her.

## The call is the relationship

A phone-first regular does not only call to book. She calls to talk, briefly, to a place that knows her. Two minutes of real conversation — asking about the trip to Kerala, the daughter's exams, whether the new colour is still holding up — is worth more than any loyalty card. It is the reason she stays when a competitor offers her twenty percent off.

Do not rush her off the line. If she mentions a change — a new job with different hours, a move to a new community — note it, and use it next time. Clients stay loyal to salons that remember their lives, not just their appointments.

## Invite her online, never push her

There is nothing wrong with offering a phone-first client the convenience of WhatsApp as well. Some will take it up gladly once they trust you. The mistake is forcing it. "I can send your appointment details by message too, so you have them in writing — would that help?" is an invitation. "We only take bookings on WhatsApp now" is a door closing.

Keep the phone line open even as the salon modernises. A salon that offers both a fast message channel and a warm human voice serves two kinds of clients well.

## Count your phone-first regulars

Once a month, take ten minutes to count them. How many of your regulars book mainly by phone? How often do they visit? And who among them have you not heard from in six weeks? A quiet phone-first regular is a warning sign, because she will not send a message to explain herself. She will simply stop calling.

When you spot one, call her. Not with an offer, and not with a script. Just a warm check-in from the person she knows: "We have missed you — would you like your usual Thursday?" That single call has brought back more regulars than any promotion, because it proves the salon noticed she was gone.

Your phone-first clients chose your salon years ago, and they have stayed because a human voice answered. Keep answering, keep remembering, and keep calling back.
