She has just finished her sixth laser session, or the last facial in the course she bought in January. The results are exactly what was promised, and she walks out smiling. Then nothing. No booking, no message, no sign of her for three months. She is not unhappy. She has simply finished what she came for, and nobody gave her a reason to come back.

This is one of the quietest leaks in a UAE salon. Courses sell well here — laser packages, microneedling series, facial programmes — because clients like the certainty of a plan and a price. But in a client's mind, the last session is an ending, and unless the salon builds a next step before it arrives, she drifts away by default, not by choice.

## Start the after-course conversation at session four

The most common mistake is waiting until the final appointment to talk about what comes next. By then the client is already in goodbye mode, thinking about what she has spent rather than what she needs next.

The right moment is two sessions before the end, when she is seeing results and feeling good about the decision. That is when the therapist can say, honestly and simply, what maintenance looks like: a top-up laser session every few months, a maintenance facial every eight to ten weeks after microneedling, a seasonal refresh after a facial programme.

Frame it as care, not sales. "Your skin is responding really well. To hold this result through the summer, most clients come back for one maintenance session in about eight weeks." She hears a plan, not a pitch.

## Book the maintenance visit before she leaves the chair

A plan that lives only in a conversation dies in the car park. The maintenance visit needs to be in the diary before the client leaves her final session.

While she is still in the chair, offer one specific date — not "come back in a couple of months" but "Thursday the 15th at 6:30, the same time you usually come." If that date does not suit her, offer one alternative, then stop. Two options, one decision, done in thirty seconds.

A booked date turns an intention into an appointment, and appointments get kept. In Dubai, where a client may travel for three weeks in December and return to a full January diary, a date already held beats a promise to message later.

Confirm the booking on WhatsApp before she leaves the building, with the date, the time, the service and the price. If she needs to move it, she will say so — and a moved booking is still a booking.

## Give her a maintenance rhythm she can see

A client who finishes a course and hears nothing will assume the work is done. A client who leaves with a short maintenance plan understands that her result is something to look after, not something that happened once.

Three lines are enough: what to do at home between visits, when to come back, and what to expect over the next three months. For a laser client, that might be sunscreen discipline, a top-up in three months, and a note that finer hairs may need one more pass. For a facial course client, a simple home routine, a maintenance facial in eight weeks, and a reminder that the glow fades gradually, not overnight.

Send it on WhatsApp the same evening as her last session. A written plan feels like a continuation of professional care, and it gives her a reason to reply.

## The check-in that is not a sales call

Three weeks after the final session, send one personal message. Not an offer, not a discount, not a broadcast. A short note from the therapist who treated her: "How is the skin settling after your last session? Let me know if anything feels different."

It shows the salon is still thinking about her result, not her wallet, and it catches problems early — if the skin reacted, or the result is fading, the salon hears about it while it can still be fixed. It also reopens the conversation naturally, so that when the maintenance slot comes up, she is already talking to the salon again.

Send it in the early evening, around 7 or 8 PM, when she is home and likely to read it. If she replies at 10 PM, answer then — that is the moment she is engaged. One message is enough. A second chase turns care into pressure, and a client who feels pressured does not come back.

## Make the next step small, not another course

The fastest way to lose a finished-course client is to pitch her another six sessions on the day her first course ends. She has just completed a commitment. Asking her to sign up for another one immediately feels like the salon never intends to let her go.

The next step should be small, specific and easy to say yes to: one maintenance session, one honest price, one date. A client who enjoys one maintenance visit will book the next herself. A client pushed into a second course will quietly disappear.

Honesty earns its keep here too. If her results are holding well, say so — and stretch the maintenance gap a little longer. The client who trusts the salon's advice about waiting is the client who comes back when the time is right, and who recommends the salon to her friends because she was never oversold.

## Track the one number that tells the truth

Most salons know how many courses they sell. Far fewer know how many course clients come back within ninety days of finishing. That second number decides whether courses build loyalty or just one-off revenue.

Count it once a month: of the clients who finished a course in the last ninety days, how many have a future booking? If it is under half, the problem is not the treatment or the price — it is the gap after the last session.

A course is not the end of a client's journey with your salon. It is the middle. The salons that keep their course clients treat the last session as the start of the next chapter, and give the client a clear, calm reason to turn the page.
