Ask any salon owner in Dubai or Abu Dhabi what keeps them up at night and rent will come up fast — usually the biggest fixed bill on the books. Yet many owners choose a location the way they choose a paint colour — because it feels right, or because a unit happened to be available. The location you pick shapes who walks in, what you can charge, and how hard you have to work to fill a chair.

There is no single best location. A mall unit, a community salon and a high-street shopfront are three different businesses wearing the same name, and the right choice matches your clients, your services and your prices.

## The mall salon: footfall you pay for

A mall location buys you something no other spot can: people already there. A well-placed salon near a food court or a fashion anchor can pick up walk-ins without spending a dirham on ads. For express services — blow-dries, brows, quick nails — that passing trade is gold. A client who was not planning a visit can still be persuaded by a visible chair and a thirty-minute service.

The price is steep: mall rents are among the highest in the UAE, and the bill rarely stops at rent. Service charges, a share of turnover in some leases, fit-out rules and opening hours set by the mall all add up. You also trade freedom for footfall: the mall decides when you open and what your signage looks like. And the crowd is fickle — traffic dips in the hot months and surges around Eid and National Day. So a mall suits quick services with a high average bill, and punishes slow, mid-priced ones.

## The community salon: slower to start, harder to lose

Community salons, tucked into residential areas like JVC, Al Barsha or Mirdif, run on a different engine: repeat clients. Nobody stumbles into one by accident. Clients come because they live nearby, a neighbour recommended you, or your WhatsApp replies were fast. The first months are slow, and you will spend more on local marketing than a mall salon.

What you get in return is loyalty: a client who trusts her stylist books the same slot every three weeks for years, and brings her sister, her mother and her friends. Rent is usually lower, the hours are yours, and you can offer the services that need time — colour corrections, bridal trials, long treatments — without a queue at the door.

The risk is a small catchment: if your area already has three salons and the buildings around you are half-empty, growth has a ceiling. Before signing, walk the streets at your planned busy hours and count competing salons within a five-minute drive. A community salon lives or dies on its neighbourhood, so study the neighbourhood first.

## The high street: the middle ground with its own rules

A high-street shopfront on a busy road in Karama or Deira sits between the two. You get passing trade like a mall, but at a rent closer to a community salon. Clients can find you on Google Maps and park nearby without paying mall parking. For many mid-range salons, this is the sweet spot.

Parking matters more than owners expect: a client who circles the block twice looking for a space will think twice about coming back. Visibility matters too — a salon set back from the road gets none of the passing trade it is paying for. And a high-street unit can be noisy and dusty, a real problem for a business that sells calm. Spend an afternoon outside the unit and watch who walks past.

## Start with your client, not the rent

The mistake most owners make is choosing a location and hoping the right clients show up. Flip the order: decide who your salon is for, then find where those people already are.

A luxury salon selling premium colour and bridal work needs clients who will pay premium prices, and those clients live or shop in particular areas. A value salon selling fast, affordable services needs volume — busy streets, dense buildings, passing trade. A salon serving office workers needs to be near offices, with hours that match the after-work rush.

Write down your ideal client in one sentence: who she is, how often she visits, and what she will pay. Then ask whether the location is full of her — if not, no amount of marketing will fix it.

## What the numbers have to say

Before you sign anything, run three numbers. First, rent as a share of expected monthly revenue — if rent alone would take more than a fifth of what you expect to earn, the location is too expensive for your price list. Second, how many clients a day you need to cover rent, salaries and products — if that is more than the location could plausibly deliver, walk away. Third, whether the business survives its worst month, not just its best: summer is slow across the UAE.

Remember the costs that do not appear in the rent figure: service charges, cooling, fit-out approvals, signage fees and the deposit. Get the full monthly cost in writing from the landlord before you negotiate — many owners discover the real number only after signing.

## Try before you sign

Test a location before committing to a long lease: a short pop-up inside a mall, a weekend stall at a community market, or home visits to clients in the area tell you more about demand than any brochure. Talk to the salon owners nearby — not to copy them, but to learn what they wish they had known before signing.

## A simple way to decide

When choosing between two or three units, score each on five things: the clients nearby, the rent against your prices, visibility and parking, the hours you can trade, and the freedom to run the salon your way. The highest score is rarely the cheapest or the flashiest — it is the one where your kind of client is already passing by and the numbers hold up in a slow month.

Location is the one decision you cannot easily undo. Services can change, prices can move, staff can be hired — but the address on the door decides who finds you, every single day. Choose it like the business decision it is, and the chairs will fill a lot faster.
