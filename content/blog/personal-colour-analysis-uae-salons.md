She walks in with a screenshot saved on her phone. A stranger on the internet, draped in coloured fabric, suddenly glowing in one shade and washed out in the next. "Will this colour suit me?" she asks. "Can you tell me what my season is?"

If you run a salon in the UAE, you have probably heard a version of this question in the last few months. Personal colour analysis — matching skin tone, eye colour and natural hair to a palette of flattering shades — has travelled from social media feeds into real appointment books. It started as a viral curiosity. It is becoming a bookable service — and one of the cheapest new revenue lines a salon can add this year.

## The question clients are already asking

Colour analysis is not new. Stylists have used seasonal palettes — Spring, Summer, Autumn, Winter — for decades. What changed is the audience. Millions of short videos show the same dramatic before-and-after: a woman holds a warm gold cloth under her chin and looks radiant, then a cool silver one and looks tired. Viewers go looking for their own answer — and the first place they look is the salon they already trust.

The question is no longer just about clothes. Clients want to know which hair colour will suit them, which lipstick shade will not wash them out, which nail colour will flatter their hands. Every one of those questions is a service your salon already sells. Colour analysis simply gives the client a reason to ask you first — and a reason to trust your answer.

In the UAE the timing is especially good. Your client base is one of the most diverse in the world: Emirati, Arab, South Asian, East Asian, African and European clients walk through the same door in a single day. There is no single "right" blonde for a market like this. A service that treats each client's colouring as individual fits a Dubai diary far better than a trend imported wholesale.

## What a session actually looks like

A session takes about 45 minutes and needs almost no equipment: a chair by a window or under neutral daylight bulbs, a set of draping cloths in warm and cool tones, and a stylist who knows what to look for.

The session starts with the client bare-faced, hair pulled back, wearing a neutral top. The stylist drapes fabrics under the chin one at a time — warm gold against cool silver, soft muted tones against bright clear ones — and watches what happens to the skin. The right shades make the skin look even and bright, the eyes clearer. The wrong ones cast grey or yellow onto the face and make tiredness more visible. Most clients can see the difference themselves within seconds.

The stylist then names the client's season and translates it into decisions. Which blonde: ash, honey or copper? Which red: cherry or warm auburn? Which lipstick, which blush, which nail colour? The client leaves with a palette card on her phone. That card is the whole product — it travels home with her, gets shown to friends, and brings her back.

## How salons price and package it

The simplest model is a standalone consultation, priced between AED 150 and AED 300 in most Dubai salons. It costs the salon almost nothing to deliver — no products consumed, no chair tied up for long — so the margin is unusually high.

The smarter model is to treat the consultation as a doorway rather than a destination. Offer it free or at a reduced price alongside a colour service, and it becomes the reason a hesitant client finally books the balayage she has been considering. She is no longer gambling on a shade she saw online; she has a professional answer about what will suit her.

Bundle it, too. A "colour discovery" package — analysis plus a gloss, a blow-dry and a makeup shade trial — fills a quiet weekday morning. December is the obvious season to launch it: party-season clients want to look their best, and a palette card is something they will show at every gathering they attend.

## Training your team without a long course

Several colour-analysis training programmes run as two-day intensive courses, some online with a practical kit posted to the salon. Train one person first — your senior colourist, or the stylist clients already ask for by name — and let her become the salon's colour specialist.

Give her a month of practice sessions on staff and willing regulars first. Colour analysis is a judgement skill, and judgement improves with repetition.

Do not let every stylist offer it on day one — a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer at all. A client told she is a Winter when she is plainly a warm Autumn will not trust your colour advice again. Keep the service with your trained specialist until the rest of the team has been through the same training.

## The honest limits

Colour analysis does not change a client's skin, and it does not make an unsuitable hair colour safe for damaged hair. A palette is guidance, not a rulebook — a client who loves a shade outside her palette should still be allowed to love it.

Skin tone also shifts in the UAE sun: weekends at the beach or by the pool change how colours sit on the skin. A palette chosen in November may need a small adjustment by April. Build that into the service: a short seasonal check-in keeps the palette current — and gives you a natural reason to see them again.

## Start small, this week

Train one stylist. Buy one set of drapes. Offer ten trial sessions to your most loyal clients at a reduced price, and ask each one afterwards: did this change what you will book next? Their answers will tell you within a fortnight whether colour analysis belongs on your menu.

The salons that win with a new trend are the ones that notice what their clients are already asking for — and answer it first, in their own chair. Your clients are already asking what colours suit them. The only question is whether the answer comes from you, or from a stranger on their phone.
