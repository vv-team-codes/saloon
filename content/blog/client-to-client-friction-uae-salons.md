A regular leans in mid-blow-dry and whispers, "Can you ask her to keep it down?" The woman in the next chair is on a loud video call. Neither client is really at fault — yet suddenly your salon is in the middle of a problem nobody booked.

It happens more in the UAE than owners expect. Salons here are open-plan, chairs sit close, and the six-to-nine after-work rush packs strangers shoulder to shoulder. Two good clients can spoil each other's visit without meaning to. How you handle it decides whether you keep one, both, or neither.

## Why it happens so often in UAE salons

Most friction comes from a few ordinary things, and it is rarely about rudeness. First, noise: a long phone call, a video call on speaker, a child playing a game with the sound on. In a quiet salon it is background; in a packed evening diary it fills the room.

Second, scent. Perfume, oud and hair products are part of daily life here, and a fragrance one client loves can sit heavily on the client beside her — especially during a long colour appointment with nowhere to move to.

Third, space. Mall and high-street salons in Dubai and Abu Dhabi work with compact floors: chairs close, basins shared, the waiting area doubling as the walkway. When the salon is full, clients are nearer to each other than they would choose to be. None of this is a client behaving badly. It is two normal people sharing a small room, and that mindset is what makes the fix work.

## What not to do in the moment

The worst response is also the most tempting: walk over and ask the noisy client to be quiet. It feels like action, but it creates a new problem in front of a full salon. The client corrected feels shamed, the one who complained feels responsible, and every other client wonders whether they are next.

Do not take sides out loud, even softly — it turns a private frustration into a public judgement. And do not ignore it either. A client who speaks up and gets a shrug learns her comfort does not matter here. She may finish politely, pay, and never come back. The quiet goodbye is the most expensive outcome, because you never get the chance to fix it.

## The quiet fix: change the room, not the person

The best move is almost always a practical one that nobody has to feel awkward about. You are not disciplining anyone — you are rearranging the room.

Move the complaining client, not the other one. Offer her the chair by the window or the quieter corner, framed as an upgrade: "Let me move you somewhere more comfortable while your colour sets." She gets relief, and the other client never knows she was the subject of a conversation.

For scent, the fix is air and distance: open the door, switch on the extraction fan, or move the sensitive client to the chair nearest the ventilation, with a warm drink while the room clears.

## The words that work

Sometimes a practical fix is not enough, and the complaining client needs to hear that she has been heard. Follow a simple shape: acknowledge, do not agree with a judgement, offer a choice. "I can hear it too — let me sort you a quieter spot" works. "You're right, she's being so rude" does not.

If the other client genuinely needs a word — a phone call that has run twenty minutes at full volume — speak privately and warmly, never across the room. Walk over with a smile: "Would you mind popping to the waiting area for your call? The sound carries a bit in here." Most people say yes immediately. And never explain that someone complained — the moment you do, you have made two strangers into opponents.

## After she leaves: close the loop

The visit is not finished when the friction ends. What you do afterwards decides whether the complaining client comes back. Send a short personal WhatsApp message the same evening, in your own words: "Thank you for mentioning the noise today — I hope the rest of your visit was relaxing. I've noted your preference for a quieter chair, and I'll make sure you have it next time." No discount, no over-apology, just proof that you listened and acted.

Do the same, quietly, for the other client. If one client is regularly loud on calls, seat her near the waiting area or the door next time, where her calls bother nobody. You are not punishing her — you are designing around a real habit. And if a message about an awkward visit arrives late at night, answer it promptly. ## Design the room so it happens less

The long-term fix is not better scripts — it is a room that makes friction unlikely. Look at your floor with fresh eyes: are the chairs so close that two strangers can hear each other's phone calls word for word? A gap of even half a metre, a tall plant, or a low divider between two stations changes the sound of a room completely.

Create one genuinely quiet chair — not a VIP chair, just the station furthest from the door, the phone and the front desk, kept for clients who want calm. When a regular asks for it, she feels the salon knows her.

Set a gentle house habit around calls. A small sign at reception — "We love a quiet salon, so please take long calls in our waiting lounge" — gives your team something to point to without making it personal.

## The salon is the host, not the referee

Clients do not come to a salon to manage each other. They come to be looked after, and when two of them collide, they are watching to see whether the salon is in charge of the room. A salon that moves a chair, offers a quieter spot and follows up the same evening is a salon that feels safe. A salon that takes sides, or does nothing, is a salon clients quietly leave.

The goal is simple: both clients should leave happy — one knowing her comfort mattered, the other never knowing there was a problem.
