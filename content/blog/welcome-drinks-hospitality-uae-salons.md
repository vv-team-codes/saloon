Most salon owners spend their energy on the service. The cut, the colour, the facial — that is the product, and rightly so. But clients do not compare salons on technique alone. They compare how a place made them feel from the moment they walked in. In the UAE, where hospitality is a point of cultural pride and where a client can choose between three salons on the same street, the small welcome rituals are what turn a good salon into a favourite one.

## The welcome drink is not a nice extra

Walk into a good hotel lobby anywhere in the UAE and someone offers you Arabic coffee or a cold drink before you have even asked. Nobody thinks of that as an indulgence. It is the baseline. Clients carry the same expectation, consciously or not, into a salon.

The welcome drink works because it does three things at once. First, it gives a new client something to do with her hands while the consultation begins, which takes the awkwardness out of the first minutes. Second, it signals that she is a guest, not a transaction. Third, it buys your team a calm minute to check the booking and greet her properly instead of rushing her straight to the chair.

A word of caution: always ask before serving anything hot, sweet or caffeinated, and keep a plain cold water option visible. Some clients are fasting, some are watching their sugar, and some simply do not drink tea or coffee. A quiet question — "Would you like water, karak or Arabic coffee?" — costs nothing and avoids the awkwardness of a drink left untouched on the side table.

## Small rituals that cost almost nothing

The welcome drink is the most visible ritual, but the best salons layer several small ones around it. None of them cost more than a few dirhams a client. What they cost is attention.

Remember how she takes her drink. A client who was offered karak with less sugar on her second visit and finds it made exactly that way on her third visit feels known. That is a stronger loyalty tool than any points card, because it cannot be copied by the salon next door.

Keep a warm towel ready for evening appointments. Clients arrive at your busiest hours — after work, after school runs, after a long day in the heat — and a warm towel on the hands or the neck while the stylist prepares is a small reset. It takes thirty seconds and it changes the mood of the whole visit.

Greet regulars by name before they reach the desk. A simple habit — the receptionist glancing up and saying the name with a smile — makes a client feel recognised rather than processed. If your team struggles to remember names, a one-line note on the booking does the job. No client will ever know you checked.

## Hospitality during the service

The welcome does not end once the service begins. A client sitting under a dryer or waiting for colour to process is still your guest, and the middle of a long appointment is where hospitality is most often forgotten.

Offer a second drink without being asked. Colour appointments in the UAE often run ninety minutes or more, and a client who has to ask for water will remember asking. A stylist who notices an empty glass and refills it without a fuss earns more trust than any discount.

Watch the small comforts. Is the air conditioning blowing straight onto her? Is the cape pulling at her neck? Is she holding her phone because there is nowhere safe to put it? These are two-second fixes, and they are the difference between a client who relaxes and a client who counts the minutes.

## When hospitality goes wrong

Small rituals only work when they are genuine. A client can tell the difference between a team that enjoys looking after people and a team following a checklist with tired eyes. Forced cheerfulness is worse than a quiet, professional welcome.

The other trap is inconsistency. If the welcome drink appears on a quiet Tuesday morning but disappears on a packed Saturday evening — exactly when clients are most stressed — the ritual becomes a reminder that the salon only cares when it has time. Hospitality has to survive your busiest hours. That means preparing for it: drinks made in advance, towels warmed before the evening rush, the front desk briefed on who is arriving and when.

## Making it a system, not a mood

The salons that do this well do not leave it to whoever happens to be on reception that day. They write it down. A one-page hospitality standard, kept at the front desk, that says exactly what happens when a client arrives: who greets her, what is offered, what happens if she declines, and what happens during a long service. New staff learn it on day one. Existing staff are reminded when it slips.

Review it the way you review anything else. Once a month, ask three questions. Did every client get offered a drink this week? Did anyone mention the welcome in a review or a WhatsApp message? Did any regular get their usual without asking? If the answer to all three is yes, the system is working.

One practical note: clients ask about these things before they visit. A short message on your WhatsApp — what to expect when you arrive, whether there is parking, whether you serve karak — answers the question before it becomes a hesitation.
