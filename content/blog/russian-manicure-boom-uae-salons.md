If you have scrolled through Dubai nail content lately, you will have seen the same close-up: a cuticle line so clean it looks drawn on, polish that starts millimetres from the skin, and a finish that stays flawless for three weeks. That is the Russian manicure, and it has moved from a niche technique to one of the most requested nail services in UAE salons.

It is not a style or a colour — it is a technique, and it changes the whole appointment. Instead of soaking the hands and pushing cuticles back, the technician works dry, using an electric file with a fine diamond bit to lift and remove dead cuticle skin with millimetre precision. The result is a manicure that looks cleaner and grows out far more gracefully than a standard one.

## Why UAE clients are asking for it by name

The UAE is a good market for this service for a few practical reasons. Many clients have seen the technique on social media long before they ever asked for it in a salon. Expat clients from Eastern Europe and Central Asia arrived already knowing the service and expecting it to be available. Local clients tried it once, noticed the difference in how long it lasted, and started asking for it by name.

It also fits the climate. In Dubai's heat, hands are on display constantly, and a manicure that chips or lifts at the cuticle within a week feels like money wasted. A well-done Russian manicure regularly lasts three to four weeks, which is a genuine selling point for clients who book gel and want their money to stretch.

And it photographs well. In a market where most nail bookings start on Instagram, a service that looks dramatically better in close-up photos has a built-in marketing advantage.

## What it takes to offer it well

The e-file is the whole point of the technique, and in untrained hands it is also the whole risk. A technician who presses too hard can nick the cuticle or thin the nail plate. One bad experience costs more than the appointment — it costs the client's trust, and in a small market, her friends hear about it too.

Good salons start with training, not marketing. A proper Russian manicure course takes days, not hours, and the technician needs supervised practice on models before she touches a paying client. Many salon owners send one senior technician for training first, let her build a small regular client base, and only then train a second person. That slower start protects the salon's reputation and keeps the diary honest.

Equipment matters as well. A quality e-file with adjustable speed, sterilised diamond bits in different grits, and a proper dust extractor are the minimum — fine nail dust in a small room is a health issue for the technician and an unpleasant experience for the client.

## Pricing it honestly

Russian manicures cost more than a standard manicure, and they should. In Dubai, salons typically charge 30 to 60 percent more than a classic manicure, with gel on top. The appointment is also longer — usually 60 to 90 minutes against 45 for a standard service. That extra time is where the quality lives, and it is also where inexperienced salons get into trouble by trying to squeeze the service into a standard slot.

Price it by explaining what the client is paying for: time, skill and longevity — a longer appointment, a trained technician, and a result that should last three weeks or more. Clients accept a premium when they understand it.

One pricing mistake to avoid is discounting the service to fill the diary. A Russian manicure done cheaply and quickly is the worst version of the service — all the risk of the technique with none of the quality that justifies the price. If the diary is quiet, offer it to existing gel clients as an upgrade on their next visit instead of advertising a cut-price introductory offer.

## The diary problem nobody warns you about

The biggest operational challenge is not demand. It is time. A 90-minute appointment in a busy nail diary is a different creature from a 45-minute one, and salons that add the service without reworking their booking template end up running late by mid-afternoon, every day.

The salons that make it work do three things. They give the service its own diary length, with no exceptions. They book it in the quieter midday hours when the nail chairs are underused, and keep the fast evening slots for express services. And they cap how many Russian manicures each technician does in a day — usually four or five — because the concentration the technique demands does not survive an eight-hour run of back-to-back appointments.

That diary discipline is also what makes the service profitable: a longer appointment at a higher price, booked into hours that would otherwise sit empty, earns more than a cheap express manicure. The maths only works if the salon protects the time.

## How to add it to your menu this month

If you are considering the service, start small and start honest. Send one technician for proper training. Buy the right equipment before you take a single booking. Add the service to your menu with a clear description — dry e-file technique, 60 to 90 minutes, three to four weeks of wear — and a price that reflects the time involved. Then let your first clients do the marketing: ask permission to photograph their results and post the honest close-ups.

Expect the first month to be slow. A new technique needs its own reputation, and word of mouth in the UAE nail market travels through WhatsApp groups and Instagram stories, not through a single launch post.

Trends come and go in the beauty industry, and not every one deserves a place on your menu. But when a technique genuinely lasts longer, photographs better, and gives clients something they cannot get from a standard service, it stops being a trend and starts being a reason clients choose your salon. The question is not whether your clients will ask for it — it is whether you will be ready, trained and honest, when they do.
