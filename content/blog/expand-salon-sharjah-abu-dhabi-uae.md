---Every Dubai salon owner who is doing well eventually asks the same question. The diary is full, the team is stretched, and the next step looks like a second branch — but Dubai rents keep climbing and the good locations are already taken. So the eye drifts up the coast to Sharjah, or down the highway to Abu Dhabi, where rents are lower and new communities are filling up fast.

## Why owners are looking beyond Dubai

The pull is real. A three-chair salon in a good Dubai neighbourhood can pay rent that would cover a six-chair space in Sharjah. Sharjah's new communities are filling with families who want a good salon close to home, and Abu Dhabi's suburbs hold residents with money to spend and fewer premium salons per head than Dubai Marina or Jumeirah.

Many Dubai salons already serve clients who live in Sharjah or Abu Dhabi and travel in for appointments. If a noticeable share of your booking book comes from one of those cities, the demand is already proven — a branch near them turns a long trip into a local visit, and monthly clients start coming every fortnight.

## Sharjah and Abu Dhabi are two different moves

Sharjah is a family market: mothers booking for themselves and their daughters, groups of sisters coming together, and brides preparing for large family weddings. Price sensitivity is higher, loyalty runs deep once earned, and word of mouth travels fast through schools and neighbourhoods. A Sharjah branch wins on warmth, value and consistency.

Abu Dhabi is a different client altogether: government and corporate professionals who value privacy, punctuality and a calm, unhurried visit. Evening appointments after work are gold. The client base is smaller but steadier, and a salon that earns trust there tends to keep it for years. The trade-off is that Abu Dhabi takes longer to build, so a new branch needs more patience and marketing before the diary fills.

## The maths before the move

Before looking at a single shop, run the numbers honestly. Add up the true cost: rent, fit-out, licence, visas, stock, and six months of salaries. Then work out what the branch must earn monthly just to break even, and how many clients a week that means at your real average bill — the one your books show, not the one you hope for.

Then compare that with what the branch can realistically earn in its first six months. A new salon in a new city does not open full — even with your name behind it, expect the diary to build over three to six months. If it cannot survive six quiet months on the cash you have set aside, the timing is wrong.

One test helps more than any spreadsheet: how many of your current clients live in the city you are considering? Twenty or thirty regulars already travelling from Sharjah or Abu Dhabi means a foundation. Five means a cold start — budget for one.

## Licence, location and the details that catch owners out

A licence in Sharjah or Abu Dhabi is a separate process from your Dubai licence, with its own approvals, costs and timeline — and the approvals do not move at Dubai speed. Start the paperwork before you sign the lease, and budget an extra month beyond what anyone promises.

In Sharjah, a salon inside a residential community often beats a high-street shop, because clients want easy parking and to walk in with their children. In Abu Dhabi, visibility on a main road or inside a popular mall can matter more, because clients are spread across the city. A street that looks busy at noon can be empty at 7pm, when your chairs need to be full.

## Staffing the new branch without emptying the first

The most common mistake is moving your best stylist to the new branch. It feels safe, because she brings clients with her — but it leaves your first salon weaker, and the clients she leaves behind notice. Send one experienced person to lead the new branch, someone who knows your standards and can train others, and keep your core team where it is.

Hire locally for the rest. Sharjah and Abu Dhabi both have stylists and therapists looking for a good salon to join, and local hires know the neighbourhood and what local clients expect. Have the new team train for a week or two in your Dubai salon before the branch opens, so the service feels like yours from day one.

## Test demand before you sign anything

The cheapest way to test a new city is not a lease. It is a pop-up: a weekend of bridal trials, a consultation day hosted with a local boutique or gym, or a small presence at a community event. Watch what happens — do people book, ask about prices, follow your WhatsApp number, and come back?

A softer test works too. Spend a month asking the clients who already travel from that city what they would book more often if the salon were closer. If the response is warm, open a pre-opening WhatsApp list with a small launch offer. A branch that opens with fifty people already waiting is a very different business from one that opens hoping someone walks in.

## When staying put is the smarter move

Not every full diary means you need a second branch. Sometimes the smarter growth is inside the four walls you already have: longer hours on your busiest days, a second shift, or better use of quiet weekday mornings. A second branch doubles your rent, your payroll and your management load. If the first salon still has empty chairs on weekday mornings, fill those first.

The right time to expand is when the first salon is genuinely full, a clear group of your clients already lives in the new city, and you have the cash to carry the new branch through its first six months. Growth is not about having more shops. It is about having more clients who trust you, in more places. If you can build that trust twice, the second branch pays for itself.
