Most UAE salon owners celebrate the wrong moment. A new client walks in, loves the cut, pays, and leaves smiling — and the whole team treats that as a win. It is not, not yet. The win comes three months later, when that same client books her fourth visit without being chased, asks for her stylist by name, and brings her sister along. Between the first visit and that moment sits a stretch of about 90 days, and it is where most salons quietly lose people they thought they had kept.

In a city where residents move often and a message from a competitor is one tap away, a great first visit buys you a chance — not a client. The salons that keep growing treat the 90 days after a first visit as a plan, not a hope.

## Why the first 90 days matter

Ninety days is not a random number. It matches how beauty actually works. Hair grows back in four to six weeks, nails need attention in two to three, and a facial routine settles into a monthly rhythm, so a new client has two or three natural reasons to return in 90 days — if the salon gives her one.

It is also the window in which a habit forms. After one visit, a client has tried you. After two, she has a stylist she likes. After three, she has a routine: her chair, her parking spot, her Saturday slot. Customers who visit three times in their first few months stay far longer than those who visit once and drift. In Dubai and Abu Dhabi, where many residents are still choosing "their" salon in the first months after moving here, those early weeks decide years of bookings.

## Days 0–7: the welcome that actually lands

Retention starts before the client reaches the door. The same evening as her first visit, send a short personal WhatsApp message: thank her by name, remind her of the one aftercare tip that matters for her service, and name the stylist who looked after her. End with two specific dates, not an open question. "Shall we book you in on Thursday the 19th at 6pm, or Saturday the 21st at 11am?" is a decision. "Let us know when you'd like to come back" is homework.

Before she leaves, write the client note. Hair type, colour formula, sensitivities, how she takes her karak, whether she likes to chat or sit quietly — all of it, written down while it is fresh. The note is the cheapest retention tool a salon owns. A client who returns to find the team already knows her does not need a discount to come back.

Do not offer a discount on the second visit. It trains the client to wait for offers, and it tells her the first visit was overpriced. Give her a reason to return instead — "your colour will need a gloss in four weeks" is worth more than 20 percent off.

## Days 8–30: the first rebooking nudge

Two or three weeks after the first visit, the service itself starts asking for a return: roots are showing, nails have grown out, the blow-dry has lost its shape. That is the moment for one well-timed message, tied to what she actually had done — a gloss and a trim for a colour client, the next step in her skin routine for a facial client. Generic "we miss you" messages get ignored because they could have been sent to anyone.

If she replies, book her in the same conversation. If she does not, send one gentle follow-up a week later and then stop. Two messages say you care; four say you are desperate. Many of these replies arrive after hours — a client scrolling at 11pm decides then, not the next morning. ## Days 31–60: deepen the relationship

The second visit is where a one-off client becomes a regular. This is the visit where remembering matters most. Greet her by name, remember the drink, the parting, the chair she liked. Recognition beats discounts every time — nobody switches salons over ten dirhams, but plenty switch because nobody remembered them.

While colour is processing, mention the brow shape or express manicure that fits in the same visit — not to sell, but to show her this salon can look after more of her routine. Ask one honest question at the chair before she leaves — "was there anything today that wasn't quite right?" — and write the answer in her note. Clients who feel heard at visit two rarely drift away silently at visit five.

## Days 61–90: make it official

By the third visit, the client has chosen you — now help her make it permanent. Offer her a standing slot: the same Saturday morning, the same Thursday evening, held for her each week. Invite her into the loyalty programme, not with a generic card but with a personal note: "you've been with us three months now — this is yours."

Mark the 90-day point with something small and personal, not a voucher. A handwritten-style message from her stylist, a free gloss with her usual colour, a "welcome to the family" at the chair. Small gestures land because they are specific; big discounts land as noise.

## Three mistakes that undo the plan

First, discounting the second visit, which teaches clients to wait for the next offer instead of booking on merit. Second, broadcast messages instead of personal ones — a client can tell when a message went to four hundred people, and it tells her she is one of four hundred. Third, and most common, asking the same questions twice. Nothing ends a new relationship faster than a salon that forgot the allergy, the formula, or the name it was told three weeks ago.

## A simple way to start this week

You do not need new software or a big launch. Take this week's new clients and write their notes tonight. Set one follow-up date for each of them, three weeks out. Give one team member ownership of the list, and check it every Monday morning. After a month, count one number: how many first-time clients reached a third visit? If that number climbs, your salon is growing in the way that lasts — not by finding more strangers, but by keeping the people who already walked through the door.
