Ask a loyal salon client why she keeps coming back, and she will rarely start with the haircut. She will tell you about the woman at the desk who remembers her name, knows her coffee order, and asks about her daughter's exams before she has even sat down. The service matters, but the welcome is what makes a client feel she belongs — and belonging keeps her from trying the salon that just opened two doors down.

In UAE salons, where clients have endless choice and every mall holds three competitors, the front desk is your most underused retention tool. Most owners invest in stylists, products and interiors, then leave the first impression to whoever happens to be free. That is backwards. The receptionist meets every client twice on every visit — on arrival and on departure — and those two moments shape whether the next booking happens at all.

## The first two minutes decide the next visit

A client forms her opinion of a visit before the stylist picks up a pair of scissors. She walks in, and in a few seconds she knows whether she is expected or an interruption. A warm greeting by name and a clear "your stylist will be with you in five minutes" costs nothing and changes everything.

The opposite is painfully common. A client walks in and the person at the desk is on the phone, finishing a message, or simply not looking up. Nobody is rude; nobody is unkind. But the client has just been told, without a word, that she is not the priority — and next time she needs a cut, she will be a little more open to trying somewhere else.

Train your team on a simple rule: the person who walks through the door is always more important than the phone, the screen or the task in hand. Acknowledge first, finish the admin second. In a busy Dubai salon at 7 pm that discipline is hard to keep — which is why it is worth having.

## Remembering is a system, not a talent

Regulars stay because they feel known — but "being known" should not depend on one receptionist's memory. Build it into the desk routine: a short note on every regular client — name, usual service and stylist, drink, any allergy, and one personal detail such as a child's name or a trip she mentioned. Two lines per client is enough.

When she arrives, the desk glances at the note and the greeting becomes personal without any effort. "Welcome back, Sara — karak with less sugar, as usual?" That single sentence does more for retention than any loyalty card. It tells her this salon pays attention.

The note also protects you when staff change. A salon where the knowledge lives only in one person's head loses a chunk of its client relationships every time someone resigns. Write it down, keep it at the desk, and update it after every visit.

The same applies before she arrives. ## The wait is part of the welcome

Even the best-run salon runs late sometimes. What matters is not the delay — it is how the desk handles it. A client who is told honestly, "Amira is running about ten minutes behind, can I get you a karak while you wait?" feels respected. A client who sits for fifteen minutes with no word feels forgotten, and she will start scrolling for alternatives on her phone.

Give the front desk permission to manage waits properly. Offer a drink without being asked. Give a real time, not a vague "soon". If the delay is long, offer a genuine choice: wait with something extra on the house, or rebook with priority. Clients forgive delays when they are treated like adults. They do not forgive being ignored.

## The goodbye books the next visit

The last two minutes of a visit matter as much as the first two. Most salons end with a bill and a wave. The salons that keep regulars end with a conversation. The receptionist confirms the result was what the client wanted, mentions the next step — "your roots will need attention in about four weeks" — and offers two specific dates before the client reaches the door.

That moment is where retention is won or lost. A client who leaves with her next appointment already in the diary does not need to be won back later. A client who leaves with a vague "see you soon" must decide afresh next time — and every fresh decision is a chance to drift.

Make the goodbye personal too. Use her name, thank her by name, and ask one quick question — "was everything as you wanted today?" — so a quiet client can mention the small thing that would otherwise become the reason she never returns.

## Train the desk like you train the stylists

None of this happens by accident. Stylists get training days, product education and feedback on every cut; receptionists often get a chair, a phone and a "you'll pick it up". Salons with trained, confident front-desk teams keep more regulars, because every client interaction is handled by someone who knows what good looks like.

Hold a short weekly desk huddle — fifteen minutes, no more. Review one thing that went well and one thing that could have gone better, and role-play the hard moments: the walk-in with no appointment, the unhappy client, the fully booked stylist. Celebrate the receptionist who turned a complaint into a rebooking.

## When the desk gets it wrong

Mistakes will happen. A name will be forgotten, a booking will be missed, a client will wait too long. What keeps the client is not perfection — it is recovery. Apologise quickly and plainly, fix the problem without excuses, and follow up personally the next day. A client looked after well through a mistake is often more loyal than one who never had a problem at all.

The welcome is the service, and the front desk is where it lives. Invest in the people who greet your clients, give them the notes and the training to make every arrival feel personal, and your regulars will be clients who never think of leaving.
