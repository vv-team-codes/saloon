Every salon in the UAE knows this message. It usually arrives on WhatsApp, often late at night: "I have a wedding on Saturday — can you do my hair and makeup?" The event changes, but the pressure is the same. The date cannot move, the client will be photographed all evening, and the result has to be right the first time.

A regular haircut can be rescheduled without drama; a wedding-morning booking cannot. Handled well, a deadline enquiry becomes a loyal client and referrals from everyone who saw her that night. Handled badly, it becomes a rushed job and a review with photographs attached. The difference is rarely skill — it is the first few messages.

## Three questions before any promise

The biggest mistake is answering "Can you do it?" before asking the questions that matter. A yes given too quickly becomes a promise the diary cannot keep. Reply fast — but with three questions first.

First, when is the event, and what time do you need to be ready? A 7pm wedding needs a very different schedule from a noon wedding — the ready-by time decides everything.

Second, what exactly do you need, and is anyone else coming with you? Hair and makeup for one person is one job. For the bride, her mother and two sisters it is a team job needing several stylists and a much longer block.

Third, have you had this service before, and is there anything we should know about your hair or skin? Previous colour, recent keratin, sensitive skin or allergies all change the plan.

One message, three questions — and the client sees you take her event seriously.

## Work backwards from the event

Once you have the answers, plan the booking backwards from the ready-by time.

For a Saturday evening wedding, trial one to two weeks before — never the day before. Colour happens three to five days before, leaving time to correct the shade if it is not right. The final styling starts early enough that a thirty-minute delay still leaves the client ready on time — for a 7pm wedding, that often means a 3pm start, not 5pm.

Explain this timeline in plain language — most clients have never thought about why colour should not happen the day before a wedding.

## Say the honest thing about what is possible

Sometimes the diary simply cannot fit the request: a Saturday in December, during wedding season, with every stylist booked from noon, cannot absorb a three-hour bridal package at 4pm. Saying yes anyway is where many salons lose money and reputation at once.

An honest no, offered well, keeps the client. Offer the real alternatives: an earlier start with a different stylist, the services split across two chairs at the same time, or a simpler version of the look that fits the time available. If none of those work, say so plainly and offer a weekday trial now, or a referral to a trusted colleague salon. A client who is told the truth will often come back for the next event. A client squeezed into an impossible slot will not — and she will tell her friends why.

## The trial is insurance, not an upsell

Some clients resist a trial, seeing it as an extra charge or an extra trip. Reframe it: the trial is what guarantees the wedding-day result. It is where the stylist learns how her hair holds a curl, which shade suits her skin, and how long the look takes. Without it, the wedding morning becomes the experiment — and nobody wants an experiment on their wedding morning.

Price the trial fairly, crediting part of it against the final booking if you can. Photograph the finished look, note the products used and the time it took, and send the client the photos.

## Lock it in with a deposit and a written confirmation

Deadline bookings deserve stronger confirmation than an ordinary appointment. A deposit — even a modest one — changes the booking from a maybe into a commitment: it holds a long block of your best stylist's Saturday, and it is deducted from the final bill.

Then send a written confirmation on WhatsApp listing everything: the services, the date and start time, the stylists' names, the total price or price range, and the deposit paid. Ask the client to reply confirming it. This one message prevents most misunderstandings that ruin event days — the wrong time, the forgotten trial, the sister never added to the booking. Send a reminder forty-eight hours before, and one on the morning itself.

## When the event is tomorrow and the diary is full

Not every deadline enquiry arrives with weeks to spare. Sometimes the message comes on a Friday night for a Saturday event, and the diary is already full — and the instinct is to squeeze the client in, because turning away a booking feels like losing money. Resist it — a rushed job done badly costs more than the booking was worth. Instead, be honest and helpful: explain tomorrow is fully booked, offer the closest real alternative, and if a trusted colleague salon has space, say so. Clients remember honesty under pressure, and many will book with you for the next event.

## The after-hours deadline enquiry

Deadline enquiries often arrive after closing time. A bride-to-be searches for salons at 11pm and messages the first three she finds. The salon that replies first usually wins her — because it answered while she was deciding.
