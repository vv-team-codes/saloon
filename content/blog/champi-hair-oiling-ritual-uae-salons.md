Walk into a salon in Karama or Bur Dubai on a Friday evening and you may catch a smell that feels like a childhood memory: warm coconut oil, a hint of curry leaf, the low murmur of a head massage. Hair oiling, the weekly champi that generations of South Asian families practised at home, is quietly becoming one of the most interesting new services on UAE salon menus — not as a free extra before a hair wash, but as a named, priced, bookable ritual.

The UAE is home to millions of people who grew up with oiling as a normal part of life, and the desert climate — dry air, hard water, constant air conditioning — leaves hair parched.

## Why oiling is coming back now

Three forces are pushing the same direction. The first is heritage. Clients who moved to the UAE from India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka grew up with mothers and grandmothers oiling their hair on Sunday mornings.

The second is wellness. Across the beauty world, slow, hands-on rituals are winning ground from quick-fix treatments. Clients are booking head spas and scalp massages because they want the visit itself to feel restorative. A forty-five-minute oiling ritual fits that mood exactly: warm oil, unhurried massage, no screens, no rush.

The third is social media. Short videos of glossy, freshly oiled hair and slow, rhythmic head massages travel well on Instagram and TikTok, and UAE creators have been posting them for months. Clients arrive already convinced.

## What a salon champi ritual actually looks like

The salons doing this well treat it as a proper service with a beginning, a middle and an end — not a splash of oil before the shampoo.

It starts with a short consultation: hair type, scalp condition, allergies, and what the client wants — shine, softness, relaxation, or simply the ritual itself. A client with a nut allergy needs a different oil from one who just wants an hour of calm.

Then comes the oil, warmed — never hot — and chosen for the client. Coconut oil is the classic, but many salons now offer a small menu: coconut, almond, argan, sesame, or a house blend with curry leaf or amla. The oil is worked through the scalp in slow sections, then down the lengths, with a firm but relaxing massage.

The finish is where the service earns its price: a thorough double wash, a light conditioner only on the lengths, and a blow-dry that shows the result. The client should leave with hair that looks glossy and feels soft, not greasy.

## Pricing it as a premium service

The biggest mistake a salon can make with oiling is giving it away. For years, many UAE salons have included a quick oil rub as a free courtesy before a cut or colour. That habit trains clients to value it at zero, which makes charging for it later feel like a price rise.

The salons winning with champi have done the opposite. They price it as a standalone treatment, roughly in the range of a mid-priced facial, priced by time and oil rather than by guesswork. A thirty-minute express oiling sits at one level; a sixty-minute signature ritual with a premium blend sits higher. The menu says exactly what is included, how long it takes, and what the client leaves with. No surprises at the till.

## Staffing and training it properly

A champi service is only as good as the hands delivering it. A stylist who has never done a proper head massage will not learn it from a five-minute briefing. The salons doing it well have picked one or two team members, trained them properly, and made them the named specialists.

Training covers more than technique: which oils suit which hair, how to spot a scalp that should not be oiled that day, what to do if a client mentions an allergy, and how to massage without pulling or tangling. It also covers the conversation — explaining what the oil does, what it does not do, and when the client should come back.

## Fitting it into the diary

Oiling is a gift to the diary because it is flexible. A thirty-minute express version fits neatly into a gap between colour appointments. A full sixty-minute ritual fills a quiet mid-morning or early-afternoon slot, the hours when many UAE salons have chairs sitting empty.

A client who books oiling on a Wednesday often books her cut or colour for the weekend. Some salons offer it as a standing fortnightly appointment for regulars — the same slot every two weeks — which turns a one-off treat into recurring revenue. That rhythm suits the service: oiling is a habit, and habits repeat.

## Marketing it without overpromising

Here is where honesty matters most. Hair oiling has real, visible benefits: it softens the hair, adds shine, calms the scalp and feels wonderful. What it does not do is regrow hair, cure hair loss, or fix a medical scalp condition. A salon that promises those things is misleading clients, and in a market as connected as the UAE, the correction will arrive quickly and publicly.

The strongest marketing for this service is simple and visual: real before-and-after photos of real clients, taken in the salon with permission; short videos of the ritual itself; and a clear description, with price and duration, on the website and the WhatsApp menu.

## The bookings angle

Like every service clients discover on social media, champi creates a wave of enquiries that arrive at odd hours. Someone watches a video at midnight, messages the salon at 12:40am, and asks whether you do hair oiling, how much it costs, and whether there is a slot on Friday. If the answer comes the next morning, she may already have booked somewhere else.
