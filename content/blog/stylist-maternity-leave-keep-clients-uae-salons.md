Your most-requested stylist has just told you her news: she is expecting, away for a few months, and planning to come back. You are happy for her — and then the diary flashes before your eyes. The Saturday 5 pm regulars. The colour clients who only book with her.

A stylist on maternity leave is not a stylist who left. Her clients are waiting — but waiting is what clients are bad at. In the months she is away, every regular will need a cut, a colour or a treatment, and without a plan they will quietly find somewhere else. Keeping them is about making the absence feel like a pause, not an ending.

## Tell her clients early — and let her tell them

The first mistake is saying nothing until the week she leaves. Clients notice a new face in her chair and feel blindsided, and a blindsided regular is already halfway out the door.

Tell them six to eight weeks before her last day, and let the stylist tell her own regulars herself, at the chair. A short, warm sentence is enough: she is expecting, away for a few months, and coming back — and the salon already has a plan. Clients take their cue from her confidence.

For regulars she will not see before she leaves, send one personal WhatsApp message in her voice, naming the return month — never a group broadcast.

## The handover is the retention plan

The single most valuable thing in your salon during those weeks is not the cover stylist. It is the handover note.

Before her last day, sit with the stylist and write down what she knows about each of her regulars: the exact colour formula, the cut she does when the client says "the usual", the products that irritate her skin, the way she likes her coffee. One page per regular is plenty. That is the knowledge that makes a client feel known — the whole reason she books with one stylist.

Then arrange one overlap visit: the regular comes in, her stylist does the consultation, and the cover stylist does the service while she is still in the room. It costs an hour of double staffing and buys something no discount can: the client's trust, transferred in person.

## One named cover, not a rotation

Do not spread her clients across whoever is free. Pick one cover stylist, name her, and introduce her as the person looking after those clients until the return — same chair, same Saturday slot, same price where you can. A rotation feels like nobody owns the client. One named person feels like a plan.

Match the cover honestly. If she is your balayage specialist, her colour clients need your strongest colourist, not whoever has a gap on Saturday. Where the match is imperfect, say so plainly and offer the client a choice: the cover stylist, or a short wait for a senior stylist's next opening. Clients forgive an honest mismatch. They do not forgive discovering it in the chair.

## Hold her place in the diary

The moment a regular hears her stylist is away, she wonders whether her slot still exists. Protect it visibly. Keep her regulars' recurring slots pencilled in under the cover stylist's name, and tell the client at the chair: "Your Saturday 5 pm stays yours, and Sara is back in March — I have already put you in for her first week back."

Do not give her peak evening slot to a walk-in because the chair is empty for one week — in a busy UAE salon, the 6 to 9 pm diary is the hardest thing to win back once it is gone. If a slot cannot be filled by the cover stylist, call the client and offer the nearest alternative yourself — before she has to ask.

## Stay in touch while she is away

Three months is long enough for a client to drift, and drifting is silent. One light touch in the middle of the leave keeps the thread alive: a short WhatsApp message, a word from the stylist if she is happy to send one, and the return month repeated. No offers, no pressure. Just the reminder that her chair is still hers.

After-hours enquiries — "when is Sara back?" or "can I book her for March?" — deserve a fast answer even at 11 pm. This is where a 24/7 AI receptionist like ZYNA earns its keep, replying with the return date and taking the booking while the client is still thinking about it.

Two to three weeks before the return, call the regulars first, before the diary opens to everyone, and offer them their old slot back. Clients who get the first call feel like insiders. Clients who have to fight for a slot in a freshly reopened diary feel like strangers.

## The return visit is the real test

The first visit back decides the next year. Give the returning stylist a lighter diary in her first week back, and put the handover notes in her hands before each appointment, so she can pick up exactly where she left off.

Check the diary a month later. Which regulars rebooked, and which went quiet? The quiet ones get one personal note from the stylist herself, asking how the last visit felt. Most of them only need to be asked once.

## What not to do

Do not pretend nothing has changed — clients know, and a salon that acts as if a three-month absence is nothing looks like one that does not value its people. Do not discount the cover visits either: a discount tells the client the cover is worth less, and trains her to expect a cheaper price when her stylist returns. Compete on care and continuity, not on price.

Do not overload the cover stylist. If she absorbs a full diary on top of her own, her own regulars start waiting and you lose clients on both sides. And do not let the handover notes walk out of the door — they belong to the salon, in the salon's client records, where the whole team can use them.

A maternity leave done well does something unexpected. Clients looked after properly while their stylist was away come back more loyal than they left, because they have seen the salon keep a promise for three months. The stylist returns to a diary that is still hers — and the salon has proved, to its best clients, that it is the salon they belong to.
