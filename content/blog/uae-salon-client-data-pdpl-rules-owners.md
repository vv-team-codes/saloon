Every UAE salon owner sits on something valuable: a client list. Names, phone numbers, birthdays, skin notes, colour formulas, appointment history. Most owners think of it as a marketing asset. Under the UAE's Personal Data Protection Law, known as the PDPL, it is also a legal responsibility — and how you handle it is quietly becoming part of how clients judge your salon.

This is a practical guide for owners who send WhatsApp offers, keep client notes on their phones and run a diary full of personal details. Getting this right protects your business and builds the trust that keeps clients booking.

## What the PDPL means for a salon

The PDPL is the UAE's federal data protection law. In simple terms, it says personal data — anything that identifies a person — belongs to that person, and businesses that collect it must handle it fairly, keep it secure and use it only for purposes the client would reasonably expect.

A salon collects a lot of it: names and numbers on booking forms, allergies in consultations, birthdays in loyalty programmes, and photos and payment screenshots in WhatsApp chats. All of it counts as personal data.

The law does not stop you collecting it. It asks you to be deliberate: collect only what you need, tell clients what you do with it, keep it safe and delete it when you no longer need it.

## Consent is the growth question, not just a legal one

The single most useful change an owner can make is to separate two things most salons blur together: permission to contact a client about her booking, and permission to send her marketing.

A client who gives you her number to confirm a haircut has not automatically agreed to receive weekly offers. When salons treat every number in the diary as a broadcast list, clients mute the salon, report the messages, or quietly stop coming — and under the PDPL, marketing without a proper basis is exactly the kind of practice that draws complaints.

The fix is simple. At the first visit, ask one clear question: "Would you like us to send you offers and new service updates on WhatsApp?" Record the answer next to the client's name. The clients who say yes are your real audience — they open messages, they book, and they tell friends. A smaller, willing list outperforms a large, annoyed one every time.

Make it just as easy to stop: every offer message should carry a simple way out, such as replying "stop", and opt-outs should be removed the same day.

## Your phone is your weakest link

In most salons the client data lives in three places: the booking system, the receptionist's phone and the owner's phone. A phone that is lost, shared with a new staff member, or handed to a departing stylist can leak more personal data in an afternoon than a year of marketing ever could.

A few habits close most of the gap. Lock every device that holds client data, including staff phones used for the salon. Do not let client photos, numbers or notes sit in personal photo albums or chat backups nobody controls. When a stylist leaves, collect salon logins, remove her from the salon's WhatsApp groups and business accounts, and change shared passwords the same day. These are the same handovers good owners already do for keys and cash — data deserves the same routine.

If you use a booking system, know what it does with your client list: can you export it, who else can see it, and where is it stored? You should be able to answer a client who asks, "Where is my information kept?"

## Sensitive notes need extra care

Some salon records are more sensitive than others. Allergy notes, skin conditions, pregnancy and medical history shared in a consultation are health-related details, and the PDPL treats health data with extra care. A salon does not need to stop recording them — patch tests and allergy notes keep clients safe, and that is a legitimate reason to hold them.

What matters is access. Not every member of staff needs to see a client's health notes. Keep sensitive details in the consultation record, not in a group chat, and never share a client's photo or treatment details on social media without her clear permission — even with the face cropped out, a client can recognise her own hair, tattoos or skin.

Clients notice this kind of care. In a market where salons compete on trust as much as on price, handling personal details properly is a genuine advantage with first-time clients.

## What to do this week

You do not need a consultant or a new system to get started. One quiet afternoon is enough for the basics. Write down what client data you collect and where it lives — booking system, WhatsApp, paper forms, loyalty app. Add a one-line consent question to your first-visit routine and start recording answers. Clean up your marketing lists so they only include people who said yes. Set a simple rule for staff: client photos and notes stay on salon devices and salon accounts, never personal ones. And pick a retention habit, such as reviewing old client records every few months and removing details you no longer need.

Then write it down in a single page: what you collect, why, how you protect it and how a client can ask you to delete it. Put it where clients can see it and train your team on it in one short meeting.

## Trust is the growth strategy

A client who trusts you with her number, her photos and her health notes books again, refers a friend and forgives a small mistake. A client who feels spammed, exposed or ignored quietly disappears — and in Dubai's small salon scene, that reputation travels fast.

The PDPL is not a threat to salon growth. It is a nudge toward the way the best salons already work: ask before you message, keep personal details private, and treat a client's information with the same care you give her hair. Do that well, and your client list stops being just a list of numbers. It becomes the asset it was always meant to be — a community of people who chose you and keep choosing you.
