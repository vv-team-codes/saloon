Every salon owner who is ready to grow reaches the same fork: buy a salon that already exists, or start a second one from scratch. In practice it is one of the biggest financial decisions a UAE salon owner will ever make. Here is how to weigh it honestly before you sign anything.

## What you are actually buying

When you buy an existing salon, you are not buying chairs and mirrors. You are buying four things, and you need to value each one separately.

First, the client list. A salon with 800 names on its books sounds valuable, but how many of those clients came in the last 90 days? Are they loyal to the salon, or to one stylist who might soon leave?

Second, the location and the lease. A good spot in a busy Dubai neighbourhood or a mall corridor is worth real money. But a great location with a lease that expires in eight months, or a rent that jumps sharply on renewal, is a trap.

Third, the team. A salon that runs well usually runs well because of people, not systems. If the senior stylists can leave freely, the reputation you are buying may walk out with them.

Fourth, the reputation. Google reviews and word of mouth in the building transfer to you — including the bad reviews. A salon with a loyal following is a head start; one with a quiet stream of unhappy clients is a problem you inherit on day one.

## What starting from scratch really costs

Starting your own salon gives you total control: the location, layout, menu, prices and team. Nothing needs fixing, and nothing comes with a hidden history.

What it costs is time. A new salon in Dubai typically needs months before the diary fills: a trade licence, fit-out, approvals, hiring, and then the slow work of turning first-time visitors into regulars. Even with a strong launch, most new salons run below capacity for the first six to twelve months — budget for that quiet period from the beginning.

## The numbers test

Before either route, run the same simple comparison. It takes an afternoon and it stops most bad decisions.

For the existing salon, ask for twelve months of real figures: monthly revenue, rent, salaries, product costs and the owner's drawings. Then ask what the salon earns in a quiet month, not an average month. A salon that looks profitable on an annual average can still bleed cash in January and through the summer, when many UAE residents travel. If the seller will not share real figures, walk away.

For a new salon, build the same twelve months yourself: expected revenue month by month, starting low and rising slowly, against rent, salaries, fit-out repayments and marketing spend. If the new salon only works when the diary fills in month two, the plan is wrong.

Then compare payback time: an existing salon usually pays back faster because revenue starts on day one, while a new salon costs less to buy into but takes longer to turn a profit. The right answer depends on your cash, your risk appetite and your patience.

## The licence and lease traps

In the UAE, the paperwork can decide the deal. Before you buy an existing salon, confirm that its trade licence is current, that the activity on the licence covers the services you plan to offer, and that the licence can transfer to you cleanly. Unlicensed services or outstanding fines become your problem the moment the transfer completes. Get this checked by someone who does UAE business setups every day, not by the seller's word alone.

The lease matters just as much. Check how much time is left on it, the renewal terms, and whether the landlord must approve a change of ownership. A mall salon, a high-street salon and a free-zone salon all follow different rules. If the lease cannot transfer, you are not buying a salon — you are buying second-hand furniture. For a new salon, choose the licence and location together, because the area you want and the licence you need have to match.

## The people question

Salons are people businesses, and both routes live or die on people. When you buy, meet the team before you sign and watch how they treat clients. Ask the seller directly who the clients follow and what happens if the best stylist leaves. If the answer is vague, price that risk into your offer — or walk away.

When you start fresh, hire before you open, not after. A new salon that opens with half a team spends its first month turning clients away. Bring at least one strong stylist with you from your existing salon, and make sure your original salon is covered while they are away.

## Five questions before you sign

Whatever route you choose, answer these five questions in writing before you commit.

1. What does this salon earn in its worst month, and can I survive three of those in a row?
2. How many clients came back in the last 90 days, and are they loyal to the salon or to one person?
3. Can the licence and lease transfer to me on terms I can live with for at least three years?
4. Who runs the salon day to day if I am not there, and will they still be there in six months?
5. What is my exit if it does not work — can I sell, sublet or close without losing everything?

If you cannot answer all five, spend two more weeks finding the answers before you sign.

## A simple way to decide

Buy when the diary is genuinely busy, the team is settled, the lease has years left, and the seller's numbers survive a sceptical reading. You are paying for momentum.

Start from scratch when you want a different concept, area or standard than anything for sale — or when every salon on the market has a visible problem. You are paying in time instead of money.

Many buy their second salon and build their third, or build the second and buy the third. Refuse to rush the decision and check the numbers twice.
