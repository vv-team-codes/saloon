## The question behind the question

"Can I try it first?" arrives in a UAE salon inbox in many forms. A bride wants a trial for her wedding hair and makeup before she commits. A client who has never had a facial asks for a ten-minute taster before booking the full treatment. Someone considering keratin wants a single strand tested before her whole head is treated.

Every version means the same thing: I like what I have seen, but I am not ready to commit. That hesitation is not a rejection. It is the warmest kind of enquiry a salon can receive, because the person asking has already imagined herself in your chair. Salons that treat trial requests as an annoyance lose these clients. Salons that treat them as a proper part of the booking journey convert a large share of them into regulars.

## Answer the real worry, not just the question

When a client asks for a trial, she is usually worried about one of three things: wasting money on something she will not like, looking wrong afterwards, or being locked into a big commitment. A good reply names the worry and removes it.

Instead of "Yes, trials are AED 150," try: "Of course — a trial is the sensible way to do it. We do a 30-minute trial for AED 150, and if you go ahead with the full service within two weeks, we take that off your booking. That way you are not paying extra for being careful."

That reply validates the request instead of making her feel difficult, gives a clear price and duration, and removes the financial penalty for trying first — the real objection hiding behind the question. For treatments where a trial is a safety matter, such as colour or keratin, say so plainly: "We always do a strand test first, so you can see the colour in daylight before we go ahead."

## Decide what a trial actually is — before anyone asks

The salons that handle trial enquiries well have already decided, in advance, what a trial includes. The ones that struggle improvise every time, which is how a "quick taster" quietly eats an hour of a stylist's evening and a full set of products.

Agree with your team on the trial version of your most-asked services. A bridal trial is hair and makeup, about an hour, with photographs taken so the bride can look at them in daylight. A facial taster is ten to fifteen minutes — one section of the face, with the full treatment explained as you go. A colour trial is a strand test plus a swatch conversation, not a half head of colour done free.

Write those definitions down, with a price and a duration for each, and keep them where whoever answers messages can find them. When the next enquiry arrives, the reply is instant and consistent instead of a ten-minute internal discussion while the client waits.

## Price the trial so it converts

The most common mistake with trials is making them free. A free trial sounds generous, but it attracts people who were never going to book, and it teaches your team that their time has no value. It also creates an awkward moment at the end, when the client who paid nothing has no natural reason to book the paid service.

The second mistake is pricing the trial like the full service. Nobody pays full price for a quarter of a treatment, and asking her to do so just confirms her fear that your salon is expensive.

The middle ground works best: a modest fee that is credited against the full booking if she goes ahead within a set window, usually two weeks. The fee filters out the merely curious. The credit removes the risk for the genuinely interested. And the two-week window creates a gentle reason to decide, without pressure. State the credit in the first message — it is the single most persuasive sentence you can write in a trial enquiry reply.

## Book the trial like a real appointment

A trial squeezed in "whenever we have a gap" sends the message that it does not matter. Give it a named slot, a named stylist and a confirmation message, exactly as you would for a full booking. Tell the client what to bring — photos of the look she wants and the outfit she will wear to the event — and what will happen during the visit.

After the trial, the follow-up matters as much as the trial itself. Send a short message the same evening: the photo from the trial, the price and duration of the full service, and two dates in the coming fortnight. Not a hard sell — just the next step, made easy. Many trials fail to convert simply because nobody ever asked the client to book. If she does not reply, one gentle message a few days later is enough. Then leave it — chasing a trial client is the fastest way to make her regret trying.

## When the honest answer is no

Not every trial request should be accepted. Some treatments cannot be trialled in any meaningful way: a full-head colour cannot be half done, a lash lift cannot be tested on one eye, and a treatment that needs three sessions to show results cannot be judged after one. Saying yes to an impossible trial sets the salon up for a disappointed client and an unhappy stylist.

The skill is in refusing without losing the client. Offer the closest honest alternative: "We cannot trial a lash lift, but we can do a patch test and a consultation where we show you photos of clients with a similar eye shape." Clients accept an honest no far more readily than salons expect, provided it comes with a real alternative and a clear next step.

## A client who is nearly yours

Every trial is also a low-pressure first visit. The client learns where to park, meets the team, sits in the chair and discovers that the salon is calm and professional. By the time she books the full service, the hardest part — the first visit — is already behind her.

So when the next message arrives — "Can I try it first?" — treat it as what it is: a client who is nearly yours. Answer fast, answer clearly, and make the trial the easiest yes she will say all week.
