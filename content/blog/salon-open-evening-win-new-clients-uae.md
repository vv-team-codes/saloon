Most salon marketing happens on a screen: an Instagram Reel, a Google listing, a WhatsApp Status that disappears in a day. An open evening is the opposite. You invite the neighbourhood inside, let people see the chairs, meet the team, try a ten-minute taster, and leave with a reason to book. It costs almost nothing, it takes one quiet evening, and in the UAE it works especially well — because here, people choose a salon by feel and trust, and nothing builds trust faster than standing in the room.

## Why an open evening beats another advert

An advert competes for attention. An open evening gives attention somewhere to land. Think of the woman who has walked past your salon in the mall or the community centre a hundred times. She has already half-decided she might try you one day; she just has never had a reason to step inside. A personal invitation is that reason.

It also turns your team into the advert. A Reel can show a good blow-dry, but it cannot show how your receptionist greets a nervous first-timer, or how a stylist listens before she picks up the scissors. Guests remember people far longer than they remember posts — and an evening costs little more than drinks and taster products, so it is one of the cheapest ways a small salon can win new clients without discounting its prices.

## Pick the right evening

Choose your quietest evening, not your busiest. In most UAE salons that means a Sunday or a Monday, when the diary is thin and the team has energy to spare. Avoid Thursday and Friday nights — your regulars need those chairs. Late November is a good moment: clients are already thinking about December parties and weddings, so an evening now books the festive weeks ahead.

Keep it short. Ninety minutes, from 6:00 to 7:30 PM, is enough. A short window makes people arrive on time and keeps the room lively.

## Invite people, don't broadcast

The guest list matters more than the decorations. Start with the people who already like you but rarely book: the WhatsApp enquirers who asked about prices and never came in, the one-visit clients from six months ago, the walk-in who took a card and never called. A personal message — with their name, the date, and one line about what they will see — brings more of them than any poster.

Then add the neighbourhood. A short note in your building's WhatsApp group, a card on the community noticeboard, a word with the café or boutique next door — all reach people who live within a five-minute walk. Cap the list at twenty-five to thirty guests. A room that feels full but not crowded makes the salon look popular; a room with eight people in a big space makes it look empty.

## Plan a 90-minute flow, not a party

Guests need a shape to the evening, or they will drift. A simple flow works: the first twenty minutes are arrival, a welcome drink and a short tour. The middle forty minutes are taster stations. The last twenty are consultations and bookings.

Name one person as host for the night. She greets everyone at the door, introduces guests to the right stylist, and keeps the evening moving. Without a host, guests cluster near the entrance and the stylists stand at their stations waiting. With one, every guest gets a conversation, a taster and a next step.

## Offer a taste, not a treatment

The tasters are the heart of the evening, and they should be short. A ten-minute brow tidy, a hand massage, a gloss demo on one section of hair, a quick skin analysis — each one shows real skill without eating into the diary. Pick three stations at most, and let guests choose one. A guest who tries one thing well leaves wanting the full service; a guest rushed through four tasters leaves feeling processed.

Price the follow-up on the spot, honestly. The stylist who did the taster should be the one to say it: the full treatment takes forty-five minutes, costs this much, and there are two slots this week. That sentence, said by the person who just did the work, converts far better than any voucher handed out at the door.

## Collect the next step before they leave

The evening only pays if guests leave with a booking, or at least a booked intention. Set up a small booking desk near the exit — a real station where someone checks the diary and confirms a date and time. Offer two real slots, not an open invitation to "come any time". A guest who leaves with "Thursday at 6:30" in her phone is a client; a guest who leaves with a flyer is a maybe.

Every guest should also leave with a take-home card: the salon's WhatsApp number, a QR code that opens a chat, and the name of the stylist she met. Ask permission to send one follow-up message, and write it down. In the UAE, where clients message on WhatsApp first and call second, that card is worth more than any goodie bag.

## The follow-up that fills the chairs

The morning after is when the evening turns into revenue. Send every guest a short personal WhatsApp message — thank her for coming, mention the taster she tried, and repeat the slot you discussed. Guests who booked get a confirmation and a reminder closer to the day. Guests who did not book get one gentle message with a quiet-hour slot, and then you leave them alone. One warm follow-up is persuasive; three is pressure.

Expect some messages to arrive late that night, after the guests get home and talk it over with family. An open evening creates a wave of enquiries; the salons that win them are the ones that answer first.

An open evening is not a grand gesture. It is a quiet, practical piece of marketing that suits the way salons in the UAE actually win clients: one person at a time, in the room, with good work they can see and touch. Pick your quietest evening, invite the people who already half-know you, give them something real to try, and book them before they leave.
