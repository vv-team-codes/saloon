She came every five weeks for two years. Same stylist, same colour, same Saturday morning slot. Then the gap stretched to seven weeks, then ten, then nothing. No complaint, no angry message, no dramatic goodbye. Her name simply stopped appearing in the diary, and nobody in the salon ever found out why.

That is how most UAE salons lose their regulars. Not with a row at the till or a one-star review, but with silence. A client who tells you what went wrong is a client you can still keep. A client who leaves quietly takes the answer with her — and whatever pushed her away is probably pushing other clients away too, while you assume everything is fine because nobody complained.

An exit interview is the simplest way to break that silence. It is not a survey and not a sales call. It is one short, personal message to a client who has drifted away, asking what happened — and then actually listening to the answer.

## Why clients leave without telling you

Owners usually guess wrong about why regulars disappear — price, a better salon nearby, or a bad haircut. Sometimes it is one of those. More often it is something smaller and more fixable.

She moved to a new apartment in another part of Dubai and the drive stopped being worth it on a Saturday. Her work hours changed and your evening slots were always full by the time she messaged. The stylist she loved went on leave and the replacement rushed the finish. She waited forty minutes past her appointment time twice and decided her time mattered more elsewhere.

None of those reasons shows up in a review. None of them is a reason to lose a client forever. But you only learn them if you ask — and the only moment to ask is while the memory is still fresh, before she has settled into a new routine somewhere else.

## When to send it — and what it looks like

The right moment is when a regular's usual gap has doubled and no new booking is in the diary. For a five-week colour client, that is around ten weeks. For a fortnightly nail client, around a month. Waiting longer than that turns a warm conversation into a cold call from a salon she has already forgotten.

Keep it to one short WhatsApp message, from a named person rather than the salon account. Something like: "Hi Sara, it's Aisha from the salon. We have missed seeing you and wanted to check in — is everything okay? If there is anything we could have done better, we would honestly love to know."

Three short questions are enough, and you do not need all three in the first message. Let the reply guide you. First, what changed on her side — a move, a new job, a different schedule. Second, whether anything about the last visit could have been better. Third, whether there is anything that would bring her back.

Do not send a survey link. Nobody fills in a form for a salon they have already left. Do not send it at 9am on a workday or late at night. Early evening, when she would once have been in your chair, is the time she is most likely to reply. And do not send it the day after a missed appointment — that reads as chasing a booking. This is a check-in, not a reminder.

## What never to do in an exit interview

The fastest way to waste the conversation is to argue with the answer. If she says the last colour faded too fast, do not explain why she is wrong about her own hair. Thank her, note it down, and ask what would have made it right. The moment she feels lectured, the conversation ends — and so does any chance of her returning.

Do not lead with a discount either. A twenty-percent-off voucher in the first message tells her you think her loyalty has a price, and it teaches her that leaving is how you get a cheaper visit. If a genuine fix brings her back, a small gesture on the return visit is a thank-you, not a bribe. The order matters.

And do not ask the stylist who served her to send the message — if she left because of something that stylist did, she will not say so to them. The message should come from the owner or the front desk.

## Turning answers into fixes

One exit interview is a conversation. Ten of them are a pattern. Keep a simple note of every answer: the client's name, her usual service, and the reason in her own words. Once a month, read the list together as a team and look for the repeats.

Two clients mentioning long waits on Saturday evenings is a diary problem, not bad luck — stagger the starts or add a buffer. Three clients mentioning the same stylist's rushed finishes is a training conversation, not a coincidence.

Fix one thing a month from what the list tells you. That is the whole system. Salons that do this stop losing clients to the same fixable reason twice, because the second time it happens, someone has already changed the diary, the training or the policy.

## Some of them come back

A good share of the clients you message will simply reply, tell you what happened, and book again. Not because you offered anything, but because you noticed.

The ones who do not come back still leave you something valuable: the truth about your salon from the only people who will tell it to you. Your regulars will rarely say the hard thing to your face. Your former regulars have nothing to lose by being honest — and that honesty, collected month after month, is the cheapest retention tool a salon can own.

Start this week. Look at the diary, find the regulars whose gap has doubled, and send three messages. You do not need new software — you need ten minutes, a named sender, and the willingness to hear an answer you might not like.
