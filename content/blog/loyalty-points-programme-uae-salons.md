Loyalty cards are everywhere in the UAE, and most salon owners have at some point printed a stamp card of their own. The trouble is that most salon stamp cards end up forgotten in a handbag or abandoned after two visits. The idea is sound — rewarding repeat visits is one of the cheapest ways to grow a salon — but the execution is usually where it falls apart.

A good loyalty programme does not need an app, a complicated tier system or a big budget. It needs one thing above all: a reward your clients actually want, at a point they can realistically reach.

## Why points work better than discounts

Salons that lean on discounts to keep clients tend to train those clients to wait for the next offer. A 20 percent off voucher brings someone back once, then makes the full price feel expensive the next time. Points work differently. They build slowly, they feel earned, and they keep the client thinking about the next visit instead of the next sale.

A client who is 80 points away from a free blow-dry has a reason to book with you rather than try the salon that opened in the next tower. It also costs you far less than a blanket discount, because you only reward people who actually spent money with you, and you control exactly what the reward is.

## Keep the maths dead simple

The most common reason loyalty programmes fail is that nobody understands them — not the client, and often not the team. If a client has to ask how it works, it is already too complicated.

One point per dirham spent is the easiest structure there is. A client who spends AED 150 on a cut and colour earns 150 points. When they reach 1,000 points, they choose a reward. There is no mental arithmetic and no awkward moment at the till.

Alternatively, a visit-based system suits salons whose average bill varies a lot: one stamp per visit, ten stamps for a reward. Pick one structure and stick with it for at least a year. Changing the rules mid-way is the fastest way to destroy trust in the whole thing.

## Choose rewards that cost you little and mean a lot

The best loyalty reward is something your client would enjoy but would not normally pay for. A complimentary deep-conditioning treatment added to a colour appointment costs you product and fifteen minutes, but it feels like a real treat to the person receiving it. A free express manicure with a haircut, a travel-size product from a range you already stock, or priority booking on a busy Saturday all land in the same category: low cost to you, genuine value to them.

Avoid offering a free full service as the first reward. It is expensive, it attracts bargain hunters, and it teaches clients that the goal is to get something for nothing. Save the bigger rewards for your most frequent clients — the ones who earn the most points deserve a gesture that says so.

## Make joining effortless

A programme that requires a form, an app download and an email confirmation will lose nine out of ten people before they start. Sign-up should take ten seconds at the till: a name and a WhatsApp number, nothing more. That number is all you need to tell a client their balance, remind them when a reward is close, and invite them back after a long gap.

Keep the record digital, even if the programme itself is simple. A shared note on the salon's phone, a spreadsheet, or the notes field in your booking system is enough for a small salon. The moment you rely on a physical card the client has to remember to bring, you lose half your participants. Tell them their balance at the end of every visit — "You have 620 points, one visit away from your free treatment" — and the programme starts selling itself.

## Let your team run it, not own it

A loyalty programme dies when only the owner remembers it exists. Every stylist and receptionist should be able to explain it in one sentence and mention it naturally at checkout. That takes a ten-minute briefing, not a training day.

One person should own the numbers. Once a week, somebody checks how many clients joined, how many rewards were redeemed, and how many loyalty members have not visited in six weeks. That last number matters most. A client who was earning points and then stopped can usually be won back with a short personal message — and the points balance gives you a natural reason to reach out. ## The quiet mistakes to avoid

Never let points expire without warning. A client who discovers her hard-earned balance has vanished will not just leave the programme; she will leave the salon. If you must set an expiry, make it twelve months and send a reminder a month before.

Never make the reward harder to claim than the points were to earn. If redeeming means booking on a specific day, with a specific stylist, and only in quiet hours, the reward becomes a frustration instead of a thank you. Make redemption easy and let clients use it when they actually want it.

And never use the programme as a substitute for good service. Points bring a client back once or twice. What keeps her for years is a stylist who remembers how she likes her hair, a salon that runs on time, and a visit that feels worth the money. A loyalty programme amplifies a good salon. It cannot rescue a bad one.

## A simple launch plan

Week one, decide the structure and the first three rewards. Week two, brief the team and set up the record system. Week three, start signing up every client at checkout with a short, warm explanation — no flyers, no posters, just a conversation. In the first month, check the numbers every week. If sign-ups are slow, the explanation is probably too long. If nobody redeems, the first reward is probably too far away.

A loyalty programme is not a marketing campaign. It is a habit, built one visit at a time, that turns your regular clients into the foundation of the business. In a city where a new salon opens every week, that foundation is worth more than any single promotion.
