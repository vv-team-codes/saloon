A client books a balayage on a beauty app at 6 pm. Ten minutes later, a regular messages on WhatsApp asking for the same stylist at the same time. The diary shows nothing, so your receptionist confirms it. By 6 pm, two clients are standing in your salon and only one chair is free. The diary simply lived in two places at once.

Apps like Fresha and Treatwell bring UAE salons clients they would never have found otherwise: a tourist in Dubai, a new resident in JVC searching for a nail bar, a man in Business Bay wanting a quick trim. But every channel that takes bookings can also start a double-booking. The answer is not to leave the platforms — it is to make sure every booking lands in one diary the whole team trusts.

## One diary, many doors

WhatsApp is one door into your salon. Instagram, the phone, walk-ins, your website and the booking apps are more. Clients do not care which door they used. They care that the chair is waiting when they arrive.

So make one rule: one diary is the truth, and every other system feeds into it. A platform booking is a request until it appears in the master diary; a WhatsApp confirmation is a promise until it appears there too. Use a digital diary that syncs with the platforms; if sync is not available, give one person per shift the job of transferring bookings as they arrive.

## The ten-minute rule

On a booking app, a client expects the instant confirmation she gets when booking a hotel. If it sits unconfirmed for hours, she assumes it failed and books elsewhere — or messages the salon on WhatsApp asking whether it went through, creating the very duplicate you were trying to avoid.

Set a ten-minute rule: every platform booking is confirmed, moved or declined within ten minutes of arriving during opening hours. Someone on the team owns the apps. On a busy Saturday, give the quietest stylist a quick app check between clients. "Somebody will check" means nobody checks. After hours, let the platform confirm fixed services automatically, and hold the ones that need a human decision — colour corrections, bridal trials — for a morning reply. UAE clients often book between 9 pm and midnight.

## When the platform and the salon disagree

Every platform booking carries two risks a WhatsApp booking does not: the price on the app may be out of date, and the duration may not match what the service really takes. A client books a 60-minute facial your team knows takes 75 minutes. The diary says one thing. Reality says another. The rest of the day pays for the difference.

Fix this at the source. Audit your platform listings once a month: right price, duration and stylist on every service. Turn off services you no longer offer, and stylists who have left. A listing still showing a stylist who resigned in September is a booking for a person who does not exist. When a mismatch still slips through, handle it before the client reaches the chair — a quick message about the longer slot turns a problem into proof the salon is paying attention.

## Protecting the peak hours

UAE salons live and die by their evening diary. Between 5 pm and 9 pm, demand peaks and the chairs are at their most valuable. A badly timed platform booking does not just fill a chair — it blocks the slot your best regular would have taken.

Some salons close the platforms at peak hours, throwing away the new clients the platforms exist to bring. Instead, shape what the platforms can sell when. Let the apps offer your full menu during quiet hours, when an extra booking is pure gain. During the evening peak, let them offer services that fit neatly into your diary — express treatments, blow-dries, manicures — and keep long services for bookings that come through a conversation. Most platforms let you set availability by service and by time of day. Use that.

## The cancellation gap

Platforms also change how cancellations behave. A client who books through an app can often cancel through the app, silently, at midnight, without ever telling the salon. The chair sits empty the next morning while the diary still shows it as full — until someone notices, usually too late to fill it.

The fix is a daily habit. Every morning, before the first client arrives, the person opening the salon checks every platform for overnight cancellations, updates the master diary, and offers freed peak slots to the waitlist. It takes five minutes. A quick WhatsApp message to a regular — "A 7 pm slot just opened with Sara tonight, would you like it?" — fills a chair that would otherwise stay empty.

## Four numbers to check each week

Once a week, check four figures: bookings per platform and how many were new clients; how many platform bookings were changed, cancelled or double-booked, and why; commission paid versus revenue earned; and how many platform clients came back for a second visit. A platform that sends clients once and never again is a marketing cost, not a growth channel.

If a platform sends plenty of bookings but almost no repeat clients, the problem is usually not the platform: nobody collected the client's details, nobody followed up, and nobody invited her to book directly next time. The first visit is the platform's win; the second is yours to earn, so record the client's name, number and preferences in the salon's own system at the first visit.

## Start with one platform, done properly

If your salon is not on any platform yet, do not join three at once. Pick the one your clients already use — ask your regulars. Set it up properly: accurate services, durations and prices, peak-hour availability, and one person who owns it. Run it for a month, then decide whether a second platform earns its place.

Third-party booking apps are not a threat to a well-run salon diary — they are a door that brings in clients you would never have met. The salons that struggle are the ones that let the platform become a second diary. Keep one diary, give the apps an owner, confirm fast, audit the listings, protect the peak hours, and sweep for cancellations each morning.
