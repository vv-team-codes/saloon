A client decides how she feels about your salon in the first ten seconds after she walks through the door — the temperature, the smell, the light, and whether the person at the front desk looks ready for her, or looks like she has just arrived herself.

That first impression is made before the morning. The best-run salons in Dubai and Abu Dhabi treat the half hour before opening as part of the service itself: a quiet ritual that sets the tone for every appointment that follows. It costs nothing but discipline.

## Start with the room, not the diary

Air conditioning comes first. In the UAE, the gap between the heat outside and the cool inside is dramatic, and a client stepping in from a hot pavement needs the room to feel settled, not still cooling down. Switch the AC on at least thirty minutes before opening, set to the temperature you actually want during the day.

Then light. Switch on the lamps or open the blinds, and look at the room the way the first client will see it. Is a strip light flickering over one chair? Is the waiting area dim while the cutting stations blaze? A quick walk from the door to the mirror takes two minutes and catches what you stop seeing when you work in the same room every day.

Scent comes next, and it needs time. A signature scent sprayed five minutes before opening smells like a spray; given twenty minutes to settle, it smells like the room itself. Deal with the smells you do not want too — yesterday's colour, a damp towel, a bin left out overnight. Open the door for a few minutes if you can, let the room breathe, then close it and let the good scent do its work.

## Reset every station as if it were the first of the day

Every station should be reset to the same standard before the doors open, whether or not it was used yesterday evening. A clean mirror, a fresh towel folded the same way, tools laid out in the same order, and the chair wiped down and set to its starting height. One person can reset a four-chair salon in about ten minutes. A client needs about three seconds to notice a station that was not reset.

The reset is also a stock check in disguise. While you are resetting, you are looking at what is missing: the colour tube that is nearly empty, the foils running low, the shampoo bottle that will not survive the day. Catching that at 9:30 is a small job; catching it at 7 PM, halfway through a balayage, is a crisis. Keep a notepad at the front desk and note anything that needs ordering the moment you spot it. Run the basin taps for a few seconds too — clients remember a cold or scalding basin far longer than a good haircut.

## The five-minute team huddle

A salon runs on its people, and the people need a shared picture of the day before the first client sits down. Five minutes, standing up, diary open — that is all a huddle needs.

Go through the day in order. Who is the first client, what is she having done, and is there anything the team should know — a first visit, a colour correction, a bridal trial, a nervous client after a bad experience elsewhere? Flag the long services, the tight turnarounds, and the gaps where a walk-in could fit. Name the person who owns the front desk for each part of the day, so messages do not sit unanswered while everyone assumes someone else is handling them. A team that has talked through the day does not need to whisper and improvise in front of clients. Keep it short and standing — five minutes, no phones. The moment it becomes a meeting, it stops happening.

## Check the day before the day checks you

While the team resets stations, the person on the front desk should run through the diary with fresh eyes. Confirm the first three appointments by WhatsApp — a short, warm message, not a generic blast — and check that any deposits due yesterday actually arrived. An unanswered client is information, not a problem: it tells you which slot might free up, so you can offer it to the waitlist instead of discovering an empty chair at 11 AM. Check the card machine and the booking link too — a card machine that fails at checkout ruins the last five minutes of a visit, while one that fails at 9:45 is a ten-minute phone call.

Scan the WhatsApp inbox for anything that arrived overnight. Clients in the UAE message late — after dinner, after the children are asleep, sometimes at 1 AM — and a reply sent at 9:30 in the morning, before the salon gets busy, is often the booking that fills a gap later in the day. Leave those messages until the afternoon and you will be replying to people who have already booked somewhere else.

## The first client sets the day

The first appointment of the day carries a particular energy. If the salon is calm, ready and unhurried when she arrives, the team carries that calm into the second appointment, and the third. If the first client arrives to a salon still waking up — music not on, towels still in the laundry, the front desk searching for her booking — the team spends the day catching up, and every client after her feels some of that rush.

That is why the opening ritual matters more than any furniture or product on your shelf. Clients do not remember your checklist. They remember how the salon felt when they walked in, and whether the people inside looked like they were expecting them.

Build the ritual, write it down, repeat it daily. Thirty minutes before opening: air, light and scent. Reset every station. A five-minute huddle. A diary and inbox check. Then open the door. The clients will not know you did any of it. They will only know your salon always feels ready for them — and in a city with a dozen other salons to choose from, that feeling is why they come back.
