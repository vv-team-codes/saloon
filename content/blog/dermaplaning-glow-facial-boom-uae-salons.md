She walks in the week before a wedding and asks for "that facial that makes my makeup sit like glass." She has seen it on her feed a dozen times: a blade gliding across a cheek, fine peach fuzz on a cotton pad, skin that looks lit from inside. She does not know the word dermaplaning yet — but she knows the result she wants.

Dermaplaning is a physical exfoliation treatment. A trained therapist uses a sterile, single-use blade, held at a shallow angle, to gently remove the top layer of dead skin cells and the fine vellus hair — peach fuzz — from the face. There is no machine, no downtime and no recovery period: a client can have it done at lunch and be at a dinner party the same evening.

## Why Dubai clients are asking for it now

The UAE climate does a lot of the selling. Air conditioning, sun and hard water leave skin feeling dull and textured, and a treatment with an instant visible change fits how people here plan their beauty. Most clients book it for a reason on the calendar: a wedding, a National Day gathering, a work event, a photoshoot, a flight home to see family. The result is immediate, which matters where bookings are often made a day or two before the occasion.

It also fits the salon rhythm. Thirty to forty-five minutes slots neatly between a blow-dry and a manicure, and it pairs naturally with services clients already book — a hydrating facial, a brow tidy, a lash lift. The result is visible in the mirror before she leaves the chair, so she is the one who starts the conversation about coming back.

## The honest limits

The trend only works for a salon that is honest about it. Dermaplaning suits most skin types, but not everyone. Clients with active acne, open breakouts, eczema flare-ups, rosacea or very reactive skin should not have it done, and anyone on strong prescription retinoids or acne medication needs a proper consultation first. Turning away the wrong client protects your reputation far more than taking every booking.

Five minutes of consultation — medication, recent peels, sun exposure, expectations — prevents almost every problem, and it builds trust. A client told honestly to wait two weeks after a beach holiday comes back because she felt looked after, not sold to.

Freshly exfoliated skin is more sensitive to the sun, and the UAE sun is not forgiving. Every client should leave with one instruction: broad-spectrum SPF every morning, no sunbathing for forty-eight hours, and no harsh scrubs or active ingredients for a few days.

## Pricing it without racing to the bottom

Price it on skill, not on the blade. The consumable cost is tiny — a sterile blade and some cleanser — but the value is the training, the steady hand and the judgement to know when not to do it. Salons that price at the bottom attract one-time bargain hunters; salons that price confidently, in the AED 180 to AED 350 range for a standalone session, keep the clients who value the result.

The smarter move is the course: once every four to six weeks, when the skin has renewed itself. A course of three sessions, paid for in advance, gives the client a reason to return and the salon predictable revenue through quieter weeks. State the price with the duration — "45 minutes, AED 250" — so a client comparing salons on WhatsApp sees value, not a number to haggle over.

## Fitting it into the diary

It needs one therapist, one chair and no machine warm-up time, so it fills the gaps machines cannot: forty-five minutes between two colour clients, a quiet mid-morning slot on a Tuesday, the last appointment of the evening. With no downtime, it also works as a same-day offer — mention it on WhatsApp Status at 2pm when a chair frees up at 5pm, and have it booked by 3pm.

The one rule is to protect the consultation time. A slot squeezed to twenty minutes because the next client is waiting is how mistakes happen. Block the full forty-five minutes, and if demand grows, add a second trained therapist.

## Training and hygiene

Dermaplaning looks simple on video, and that is exactly what makes it risky in untrained hands. A blade at the wrong angle, too much pressure, or a reused blade can cut or irritate skin, and one bad experience will cost a salon far more than the training course ever would. Every therapist should complete a proper course, practise on models under supervision, and be confident enough to say no when the skin is not suitable.

Hygiene is non-negotiable and easy to show: single-use sterile blades opened in front of the client, gloves, clean hands, a sanitised couch and fresh linen. Clients notice these details, and in a city where clients compare salons carefully, visible hygiene is part of the service.

## Selling it honestly

The best marketing is the result itself, shown honestly. A short video of the treatment, filmed with the client's permission, does more than any poster. Before-and-after photos work when they are real: same light, no filter, no heavy editing. One honest photo earns more bookings than ten polished ones.

Enquiries will arrive the way most salon enquiries arrive here — on WhatsApp, often late at night, often with a photo attached and a question like "is this good for my skin?" A fast, specific reply wins the booking: what it does, how long it takes, the price, and one honest line about who it does not suit. ## A calm way to start

If dermaplaning is not on your menu yet, start small. Train one therapist properly, not the whole team at once. Offer it standalone and as an add-on to your most popular facial for four weeks, and watch which one clients choose. Price it with confidence, photograph the real results, and ask every client how her makeup sat the next morning.

Track three numbers: how many clients rebook within six weeks, how many came from a WhatsApp enquiry, and how many added it to another service. If all three are climbing, you have a service worth keeping. Trends come and go, but the ones that last solve a real problem: tired-looking skin, makeup that will not sit right, and an occasion already on the calendar.
