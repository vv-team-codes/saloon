Every year, the same thing happens to UAE salon owners. The diary that worked perfectly in February suddenly stops working. Daytime chairs sit empty, the phone goes quiet until late afternoon, and then — just as the team is thinking about winding down — a wave of clients wants appointments at nine, ten and eleven at night. Ramadan does not just change when people eat. It changes when they book, when they arrive, and what they expect from a salon.

The salons that handle Ramadan well do not fight this shift. They rebuild the diary around it for four weeks, then switch back.

## Why Ramadan rewrites the diary

This is not a small dip. Many salon owners report daytime bookings falling by half or more during the holy month, while evening demand exceeds what the normal diary can hold. The mistake is to treat this as a slow month. It is a different month, with its own rhythm, and the diary has to match it.

## The daytime quiet: plan for it, don't fight it

The quiet daytime hours are the hardest part of Ramadan for salon owners, because empty chairs feel like lost money. But chasing daytime clients who are fasting, resting or working reduced hours rarely works. A better use of those hours is to make the salon stronger for the evening.

Use the daytime for the work that never gets done when the diary is full: deep cleaning, restocking, training, and maintenance. This is the month to service equipment, refresh the retail shelf, and run the training session your team has been postponing. It is also the right time for client work that does not need a chair — updating client records and preparing the pre-Eid offers.

Some salons also use daytime hours for home visits, which suit clients who prefer privacy during the fasting month. A stylist who would otherwise sit idle can take two or three home appointments in an afternoon, and those visits often convert into loyal in-salon clients after Eid.

## The iftar-to-midnight rush

The real business of Ramadan happens after iftar. Clients break their fast with family, then head out — and salons are high on the list. The busiest window is usually from about 8:30 PM until closing, with demand peaking again in the final ten days before Eid.

This is where the diary needs real redesign. Stylists should start later and finish later, with the strongest team on the floor from 8 PM onwards. Short services — blow-dries, threading, express manicures — should be clustered in the late window, because that is what walk-ins and last-minute bookers ask for after iftar.

It also helps to protect the late diary with deposits. Evening slots in Ramadan are the most valuable hours of the month, and a no-show at 10 PM cannot be refilled the way a 2 PM cancellation can. A small deposit on peak evening appointments, explained politely at booking, keeps the diary honest and the chairs full.

## Rethink opening hours for the month

Many UAE salons adjust their opening hours during Ramadan, and clients expect it. The usual pattern is a later opening — often around noon or 1 PM — and a later close, sometimes midnight or beyond in the final week. Whatever hours you choose, decide them before the month begins and publish them everywhere: Google Business Profile, Instagram bio, WhatsApp status, and the booking link itself.

During Ramadan, the after-hours enquiry is the norm, not the exception. Clients will message at midnight asking for a slot tomorrow evening, and the salons that answer first win the booking. A 24/7 AI receptionist like ZYNA can take those late-night enquiries, answer questions about hours and services, and hold the booking in the diary so the morning team finds it waiting.

Consistency matters more than the exact hours. Pick a Ramadan schedule, stick to it for the whole month, and tell clients clearly when it starts and when normal hours return.

## Staffing the late shift without burning out the team

A later diary means a later team, and tired stylists make mistakes. The salons that get through Ramadan in good shape plan the rota as carefully as the diary itself.

Split the team into two shifts where possible: an early crew that covers the afternoon and a late crew that owns the evening. Nobody should work a full day that stretches from noon to midnight — that is how the final week, the busiest of the month, ends with a sick or exhausted team. Build in a proper break around iftar for fasting staff, with a real meal and time to pray, not a rushed sandwich at the back desk.

If you need extra hands, the last ten days are the time to use them. A part-time stylist on an on-call bench, booked for the final week only, costs far less than turning away pre-Eid clients who will not come back.

## The pre-Eid surge deserves its own plan

Open the Eid-week diary early — at least two weeks ahead — and let regulars pre-book their slots before you open them to everyone. Offer a small number of package slots that bundle the services people actually want together, with honest times attached, so a client booking a full pre-Eid package knows it takes three hours and the diary is protected. And keep a short waitlist for the final two days, because cancellations always happen, and a waitlist turns a cancelled 9 PM slot into a filled chair within minutes.

## A simple Ramadan diary plan

If this is your first Ramadan with a diary to manage, keep the plan simple. Four weeks before the month begins, decide your Ramadan hours and publish them. Two weeks before, message your regulars with the new hours and invite them to pre-book their Eid-week appointments. On the first day, switch the diary to the new pattern and protect the evening with deposits. In the final week, run the late shift at full strength and keep the waitlist warm.

Ramadan rewards salons that respect the rhythm of the month instead of resisting it. The daytime is for preparation, the evening is for earning, and the final week is for delivering the best work of the season. Plan the diary that way, and the holy month becomes one of the strongest periods of the year — not a quiet one to endure.
