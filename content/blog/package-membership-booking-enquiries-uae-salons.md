Selling a package is the easy part. The questions start the day after. "How do I book with my package?" "When does it expire?" "Can my sister use one session?" "I'm travelling for six weeks — can I pause it?" These enquiries arrive, usually on WhatsApp, usually in the evening, and usually from the clients you most want to keep: the ones who already paid you in advance.

How you answer decides whether that package turns into six happy visits or one awkward complaint.

## Why package enquiries feel harder than ordinary bookings

A normal booking enquiry is simple: a service, a time, a price. A package enquiry carries history. The client is asking about money she already spent, sessions she already owns, and rules she may not remember agreeing to. She is not just booking; she is checking whether the deal still holds.

So tone matters as much as facts: a short "yes, you have three sessions left" answers the question but misses the moment. The client is really asking whether you remember her — and whether using the package will be easy. In the UAE, where clients travel for weeks each summer, a rigid "no pauses, no sharing" answer can cost you the client.

## The four questions every package holder asks

Almost every package enquiry is one of four questions — answer all four in one message and most threads close in minutes.

**"How do I book with it?"** The answer should be the same route as everyone else: pick a service, pick a time, mention the package when booking. If booking with a package is harder than booking without one, clients quietly stop using it.

**"How many sessions do I have left?"** Never make the client do the maths. Give the number, plus the expiry date, in the same message: "You have three facials left from your package of six, and they run until 15 March."

**"Can I change or pause it?"** Travel, a new baby, a family emergency, a move across town — life interrupts packages constantly. Decide pause and transfer rules in advance, and write them where the whole team can see them. A one-time pause or a transfer to a named family member costs little and buys goodwill.

**"What happens if my stylist leaves?"** Package clients bond with a person, not a building. Answer early and honestly: the package belongs to the salon, the new stylist is briefed on her notes, and the first visit with the new stylist is a proper handover, not a cold start.

## One answer, every team member

The most common package-enquiry failure is not a bad answer. It is two different answers. The client messages on Monday and is told she can share a session with her sister. She messages on Thursday and is told she cannot. Now the argument is not about the policy; it is about trust.

Packages need one written sheet that every team member can find in ten seconds: how many sessions, how long they last, whether they can be shared or paused, what happens on expiry, and what happens if a stylist leaves. If the answer is not on the sheet, she checks with the owner and gives a time: "Let me confirm that and message you back by 6 pm today."

Decide the goodwill rule in advance too — a short grace period, a one-session extension for a genuine reason — so nobody has to make a financial decision mid-conversation with a client waiting on WhatsApp.

## Turn the enquiry into the next booking

Every package enquiry is a booking opportunity hiding in plain sight. The client is already thinking about her next visit; she just needs a reason to pick a date. So end every package reply with a concrete next step, not an open question.

Instead of "Let us know when you'd like to come in," try: "You have three sessions left. Your usual stylist has Thursday at 6 pm or Saturday at 11 am free this week — shall I book Thursday for you?" Two real times and a yes-or-no question — most clients answer within minutes.

The same applies when the package is nearly finished: "This is your last facial from the package. Would you like to renew the same package, or look at the membership that includes the hair treatments you've been adding?" Have that conversation while the client is still enjoying the package, not months after it expired.

## Expiry dates need a human touch

Expiry is where most package goodwill dies. A client discovers her sessions expired while she was travelling, messages in frustration, and is met with a flat "the package has expired." She hears: your money is gone.

Warn the client twice before expiry — at thirty days and at seven — on WhatsApp, with two real slots to book. The ones who still miss the date deserve a human decision, not an automatic no: a two-week grace period for a first expiry costs far less than losing a client who prepaid for six visits.

Be honest at the point of sale too. Say the expiry date out loud, write it on the receipt or the WhatsApp confirmation, and repeat it at the first booking. Clients who know the rules in advance almost never argue about them later.

## The after-hours problem — and a simple routine

Package questions do not wait for opening hours. They arrive at 11 pm, when a client is planning her week, and at 7 am, when she is checking her diary before work.

The rest needs a routine, written down and used by everyone: one live record per package client — sessions bought, sessions used, expiry date, usual stylist. Answer within the hour during the day. Send expiry reminders at thirty days and seven days. Review your package rules once a quarter: if the same question keeps coming up, the rule is unclear, and unclear rules become arguments.

Packages are a promise: the client trusted you with her money before the service was delivered. Every enquiry is a chance to prove that trust was well placed — answer quickly, answer consistently, and always end with a booking.
