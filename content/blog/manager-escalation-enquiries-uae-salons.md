Every salon owner in the UAE knows the message. It arrives on WhatsApp, usually in the evening: "Can I speak to the manager, please?"

The stylist reading it feels a small jolt. The instinct is either to hand the phone over immediately or to stall with "the manager is busy right now." Both reactions cost bookings. The first hands a routine enquiry to the busiest person in the building; the second makes a client who wanted reassurance feel brushed off.

The truth is that most of these messages are not complaints at all. They are enquiries from clients who want to feel safe before they spend. How your salon answers decides whether they book — and whether they ever come back.

## Why clients ask for the manager

In a UAE salon, the request usually comes from one of four places: the client is about to spend serious money, such as a bridal package or a colour correction, and wants the person in charge to confirm it; she has had a bad experience somewhere else and is checking whether your salon is different; she has an unusual request, a 7am appointment before a flight, and assumes only the manager can say yes; or something has already gone slightly wrong, a booking was moved or a price was misquoted, and she wants the person who can fix it.

What they share is simple: the client wants certainty, and she believes the manager is the fastest route to it.

## The first reply matters more than the meeting

The worst reply is a defensive one. "What is the problem?" sounds like an accusation, and "the manager is not available" sounds like a closed door.

A good first reply welcomes the request, asks one gentle question, and promises a fast answer. Something like this:

"Of course — happy to help. So I can get you the right answer straight away, could you tell me briefly what it is about? Our manager will personally look at it and reply within the hour."

That message works because it does not argue about who the client should talk to. It respects the request while quietly gathering the one piece of information the team needs: what is this about? In most cases, the client answers, the front desk realises it can answer the question itself, and the booking is made in the next message — with the manager's name attached so the client still feels heard.

The golden rule: never make a client repeat herself.

## Three questions before handing over

Train your team to answer three questions silently before any enquiry reaches the owner or manager. Discounts beyond the agreed limit, a refund, a free service, a booking outside normal hours — these genuinely need senior approval. A price range, a service recommendation, a slot tomorrow afternoon — these do not.

Is the client upset, or just careful? An upset client needs the manager's voice quickly. A careful client needs a clear, confident answer with a name attached to it.

Can we answer in this message, with the manager's authority behind it? Many salons solve most escalations this way: the manager writes the answer once, the team sends it. "Our manager, Fatima, has confirmed we can hold the 7am slot for you on Thursday, and the price for the full package is AED 950." The client got the manager's decision. The manager spent thirty seconds on it. Nobody waited.

When the answer genuinely needs the manager, say so honestly and give a time: "She is with a client until 6pm, and she will message you personally by 6:30pm." A specific time is a promise. "Soon" is not.

## When the manager should actually step in

A client who has already spent a lot with you and is unhappy deserves the manager's voice, not a copy-paste reply. A big booking — a bridal party, a corporate day, a full-day hire — is worth a personal message from the owner, because that message is also the beginning of the relationship. And any situation where the client has asked twice and still has no answer should go straight to the top. By the second ask, the issue is no longer the original question. It is the feeling of being ignored.

What the manager should never do is take over a conversation the team was handling well. The strongest salons run the opposite way: the team handles almost everything, and the manager's involvement is a pleasant surprise, not a routine demand.

## A small system that keeps escalations small

Keep a one-line log. Every "speak to the manager" request gets noted with the client's name, the question, and the answer given. After a month, patterns appear: five clients asking about early-morning slots means the salon needs an early-morning policy; four asking about deposits means the deposit message needs rewriting.

Next, write the five most common answers in advance, in the manager's own words, and save them as quick replies. The team sends them instantly, in the manager's voice, and the client never waits. Protect one quiet hour a day for the manager to clear the small pile of enquiries that genuinely need her — many arrive after 7pm, when the salon is at its busiest and the manager is on the floor. Finally, close the loop: whoever answers follows up the next day — the slot is held, the price is confirmed. Clients who asked for the manager remember whether the salon kept its word.

## The client who asked for the manager is a good client

It is easy to read "can I speak to the manager" as a threat. Read it again as a compliment: this is a client who cares enough about her hair, her skin, or her wedding morning to ask for the best answer your salon can give before she spends a dirham. She wants a reason to trust you.

Handle that enquiry well and you do more than save one booking. You show a careful client that someone senior stands behind every promise the front desk makes.
