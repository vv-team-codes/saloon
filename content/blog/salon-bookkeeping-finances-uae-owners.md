Most UAE salon owners did not open a salon because they love spreadsheets. They opened one because they love the craft — the cut, the colour, the moment a client looks in the mirror and smiles. So the books get pushed aside, receipts pile up in a drawer, and nobody quite knows whether last month was actually profitable until the rent is due.

That is how good salons end up in trouble. Not because they lack clients, but because nobody was watching the money. The fix does not need a finance degree — just a few habits, repeated every week.

## Start With One Business Account

The single most useful change a salon owner can make is also the simplest: run every dirham of salon money through one business account, and keep personal spending out of it. In a small Dubai or Sharjah salon it is tempting to pay for a delivery from a personal card, or to take cash from the till and "sort it out later". Later never comes, and by the end of the month nobody can say what the salon actually earned.

A separate account makes the monthly picture honest: money in, money out, balance left. If you pay yourself, do it as a fixed monthly transfer, like a salary. That way the salon's profit is a real number.

## Know Your Real Cost Per Service

Ask most salon owners what a balayage costs them and they will quote the product price. The real cost is bigger: the colour and developer, the stylist's time, the chair time, the air conditioning running while the colour processes, and the card fee on the payment. In a UAE salon, where cooling alone is a serious monthly bill, the overhead inside each service is larger than owners expect.

Work it out once per service and write it down. Add the product cost at the price you actually pay, then add a share of the month's fixed costs — rent, DEWA, licences, insurance — divided across the services you expect to sell. The result is often a surprise: a service that felt profitable barely breaks even, while a quick express treatment quietly earns more per hour than a long colour session.

This matters most when you set prices. When you know the true cost, you can raise the price of one service with confidence, or quietly retire a service that loses money on every booking.

## VAT Without the Panic

Value Added Tax worries owners most, yet it causes the least trouble when handled in small, regular steps. If your salon is VAT registered, treat the VAT you collect as money that is not yours. The moment a client pays, a slice of that payment belongs to the tax authority, and spending it on stock or salaries is how salons end up short at filing time.

The practical habit is a separate VAT pot. Every week, move the VAT portion of that week's takings into a separate savings account. When the quarterly filing comes around, the money is already there, and the filing is an admin task instead of a crisis. Keep every supplier invoice and receipt in one folder, because a clean record makes a VAT return quick.

If your salon is not yet VAT registered, know the threshold and watch your rolling turnover: growing past it without registering is a common and expensive mistake.

## Cash Flow in a Seasonal City

UAE salons do not earn evenly through the year. December and the weeks before Eid are intense; January is quiet; the summer months slow down as families travel. A salon that looks healthy in December can be struggling by February if the busy-season money was spent as though it would never end.

The habit that fixes this is simple: in a strong month, set aside a fixed amount for the slow months before you spend anything else. Many owners move ten to fifteen percent of each strong month's takings into a reserve account and leave it alone until the quiet weeks arrive. When January comes, that reserve pays the rent and the salaries without panic.

Cash flow also depends on money actually arriving. Deposits on long bookings, payment links sent with the confirmation message, and a clear policy on late cancellations all turn a booking on the diary into money in the account. ## The Monthly One-Hour Review

None of this works without a regular look at the numbers. Block one hour a month — the first Monday morning, before the salon opens, works well — and answer five questions. How much came in? How much went out, and on what? Which services earned the most per hour? Which clients rebooked, and which did not come back? And is there enough in the reserve to cover next month's fixed costs?

Write the answers down, in the same notebook or spreadsheet every month. After three months you will have a trend, and trends are where the real decisions live: a service whose earnings per hour keep falling needs a price or diary change, and a month where rebooking dipped is a signal to look at the client experience.

## When to Get Help

There is a point where doing the books yourself stops being sensible. If you are spending your evenings chasing receipts, if VAT filing fills you with dread, or if you genuinely do not know whether the salon made money last quarter, hire a bookkeeper for a few hours a month. In the UAE there are freelance bookkeepers who work with small salons for a modest monthly fee, and a good one pays for themselves by finding the leaks — the subscription nobody uses and the supplier price that quietly rose.

## The Salon That Knows Its Numbers Grows

None of this is glamorous. Nobody opens a salon dreaming of a tidy ledger. But the salons that grow in the UAE — the ones that open a second branch, survive a quiet summer, or simply pay their team on time every month without stress — are almost always the ones whose owners know their numbers. Clean books tell you which services to push, which prices to change, and when you can afford to hire.
