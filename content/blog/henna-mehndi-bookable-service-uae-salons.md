Walk into almost any busy salon in Dubai or Sharjah in the two nights before Eid and you will see the same scene: a queue of women waiting for henna and artists working elbow to elbow. It is wonderful business — and it is gone by the weekend. For years, most UAE salons treated henna exactly like that: a seasonal rush to survive, not a service to build on.

That is changing. More owners now treat mehndi as a proper, bookable service with its own chair, price list and diary slot all year round. Weddings, engagements and Friday-evening treats keep demand going long after the Eid lights come down. If your salon already does henna informally, you are probably leaving money on the table.

## Henna is no longer just an Eid rush

Winter is wedding season in the UAE, and a single wedding can mean a bride, her sisters, her mother and a dozen friends all wanting henna in the same week. Add engagement parties, mehndi nights and tourists who want a small design as a holiday memory, and the picture changes completely.

Mall salons see it in Thursday and Friday evening footfall; residential salons see it in regulars who want the same artist every few weeks.

Younger clients treat henna as fashion, not only tradition, arriving with saved photos of minimal designs and fine-line florals — and willing to pay for something small and beautifully done.

## What clients actually ask for

Requests fall into four groups. Simple palm designs are the volume service: fifteen to thirty minutes, ideal for walk-ins and a natural add-on after a manicure. Bridal henna is the opposite: a two-to-four-hour sitting, booked days ahead, usually with a trial. Then there is party henna, where one artist works through five or ten guests, and the small designs tourists and teenagers ask for on the back of one hand.

Name each one on your menu with a starting price and a realistic time. Put the four options on WhatsApp too, where most henna enquiries start.

## The safety conversation you cannot skip

Natural henna stains the skin a reddish brown that deepens over a day or two. So-called black henna, which stains dark almost immediately, often contains a chemical dye that can cause painful reactions, especially on sensitive skin and children.

Be honest about this in the salon and in your replies. Tell clients what you use, show them the product if they ask, and offer a small patch test for anyone with sensitive skin, anyone who is pregnant, or anyone who has never had henna before. A short, calm explanation at booking protects the client and your reputation — and salons that do this well find the safety conversation actually sells the service, because nervous clients become the most loyal ones.

Note the design, the artist and any reaction on the client's record, so the next visit starts where the last one ended.

## Pricing henna so it pays

The most common pricing mistake is charging by the design's beauty instead of by the artist's time. A beautiful but slow design that takes an hour and earns less than a blow-dry is not a good service, however much clients love it.

Start from time. Work out what one hour of an artist's time needs to earn, then price each design from its real duration, including setting time and aftercare. Simple designs can be a flat rate; bridal work should be a package — both hands to the elbow, feet, and a touch-up visit if needed. Group bookings work best at a per-person rate with a minimum number of guests, so the artist's evening is protected.

Do not discount during Eid itself, when demand exceeds supply. Save offers for the quiet weeks after a festival, when a henna-and-manicure pairing can pull people back.

## Fitting henna into a busy diary

Henna's biggest operational problem is that it is slow. A bridal sitting can occupy a chair for half a day, and during a festival rush that chair could have served six quick clients. The answer is not to refuse bridal work — it is to schedule it deliberately. Give henna its own diary blocks away from the busiest cutting hours, and keep one chair as the henna chair during wedding season.

Enquiries spike late at night, especially before Eid and big wedding weekends, when clients message three salons at once. The salon that answers first usually wins. A 24/7 AI receptionist such as ZYNA can take those late-night enquiries, ask for the occasion and date, and hold a slot before the client moves on. Speed matters more than polish: a clear reply with a price range and two available times beats a perfect reply that arrives in the morning.

## Turning one henna client into a wedding party

Henna has a natural way of multiplying. A bride who loved her design brings her sisters. A guest at a mehndi night books her own engagement henna a month later. Smart salons plan for this instead of leaving it to chance.

At the end of a bridal sitting, offer the bridal party a group rate for the wedding week, and send the bride a WhatsApp message she can forward to her family group. After a party booking, follow up the next day with a photo of the design and a reminder that the same artist is available for the wedding. These small steps cost nothing — and they are how one booking becomes five.

Photograph finished designs, with permission, in good light. Clients choose an artist by her past work, and a strong album is worth more than any advertisement.

## A small start that works

You do not need a grand launch to make henna a year-round service. Start with four menu items, one named artist and a simple booking flow on WhatsApp. Run it for a month and track three numbers: how many henna bookings you take, how much each one earns per hour, and how many of those clients book something else within six weeks.

If the numbers are strong, add a second artist and a bridal package with a trial sitting. If they are weak, the problem is usually the menu, the diary or slow replies — not demand, which is already there all year round.

Henna has always been part of life here. The salons that earn the most from it treat it as a craft worth booking properly — priced honestly, done safely, and easy to book.
