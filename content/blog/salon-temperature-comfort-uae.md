A client steps out of the Dubai afternoon into your salon. Outside it is 38 degrees and humid. Inside, the air conditioning is running at 19. She is wearing a light dress, her shoulders are bare, and within two minutes she has goosebumps. She will sit in that chair for the next ninety minutes, hair wet, a cape around her neck, trying not to shiver while your stylist works.

She probably will not complain. Most clients do not. They just decide, quietly, that the visit was not as relaxing as it should have been — and next time, they try somewhere else. Temperature is one of those details a salon owner rarely thinks about and a client never forgets. In the UAE, where the gap between outside and inside can be twenty degrees, it matters more than almost anywhere else.

## The first five minutes decide everything

Comfort is set in the first few minutes of a visit, long before the service begins. A client who walks in hot and sweaty needs a moment to cool down, not a blast of cold air the second she sits down. A client who arrives already cold from an air-conditioned car needs warmth, not another hour under a vent.

If clients rub their arms when they sit down, or keep a cardigan on through a haircut, your salon is too cold for them. If anyone fans themselves or asks for water twice, it is too warm. Set the thermostat a degree or two warmer than you think you need — 23 to 24 degrees feels comfortable for a seated client in light clothing. Your team is moving around, so they feel warmer than the client does. Dress for the client's comfort, not the stylist's.

## Ask, don't assume

No two clients feel temperature the same way. A client having a long colour service will sit still for forty minutes with wet hair. A client having a quick trim is in and out in twenty. An older client, a pregnant client, or someone who has just come from the gym will each want something different.

So ask. One question at booking — "Do you tend to feel the cold, or do you like it cool?" — takes five seconds and changes the whole visit. Note the answer on the client's record, so the blanket is already on her chair when she returns. A good receptionist, human or AI, keeps that note on file — ZYNA, for example, can capture a comfort preference from a WhatsApp enquiry and attach it to the booking.

## Find the cold spots in your salon

Every salon has them. The chair directly under the air conditioning vent. The wash basin area, where wet hair and a draft meet. The corner by the glass door, which warms up in the afternoon sun and then chills when the door opens. Walk your own floor as a client would. Sit in every chair for two minutes. Stand at the basin with wet hair. You will find the spots your team has stopped noticing.

The solutions are simple. Move the worst chair, or redirect the vent louvre away from it. Put a light blanket on the basin chair. Keep the door closed between clients during the hottest hours, and if the afternoon sun hits one side of the room, a sheer blind does more than a lower thermostat ever will.

## Hot services, cold clients

Some services make the temperature problem worse. A long colour process means a client sits still, damp, for half an hour or more. A facial means lying flat under a cool room and a thin towel. A head massage with wet hair is relaxing only if the client is warm enough to relax.

This is where small comforts earn their keep. A warm towel on the shoulders during processing time. A light blanket offered without being asked. A cup of karak or herbal tea instead of iced water for a client who is already cold. None of this is expensive. All of it is noticed, and all of it is remembered when the client decides where to book next time.

Timing matters too. Evening is peak time in UAE salons — clients come after work, often straight from an air-conditioned office, and the salon is at its busiest and coldest. Build a two-minute comfort check into the service itself: when the colour is applied, when the client moves to the basin, when the blow-dry starts. A quick "Are you warm enough?" is free, and it is the question clients remember.

## The little things that keep clients warm

Comfort is a system, not a single gesture. A few habits make it reliable:

Keep a small stack of clean, light blankets where the team can reach them without leaving the client. Offer one before the client asks — offering is hospitality, waiting to be asked is a chore for the client.

Keep the wash basin water warm, not just lukewarm. A cold rinse at the end of a long service undoes an hour of comfort in thirty seconds.

Watch the seasons. Winter evenings in the UAE are mild outside, and a salon set for August will feel freezing in January. Adjust the thermostat with the season, not once a year.

Train the team to notice, not just to ask. A client hugging her own arms, a client who keeps her shawl wrapped tight, a client who asks to move chairs — these are all the same message. The stylist who acts on it without being told is the one clients ask for by name.

## Comfort is the reason they come back

Salon owners spend a lot of time and money on the visible things: the decor, the products, the Instagram feed. But clients do not come back for the decor. They come back for how the visit felt. A salon where a client was warm, comfortable and looked after for the whole appointment is a salon she recommends to her friends — and she will mention the blanket and the tea long before she mentions the haircut.

Temperature is a small thing. That is exactly why it works. It costs almost nothing to fix, it is entirely in your control, and it is the kind of care that no competitor can copy with a discount. Get the comfort right, and the client does not just leave looking good — she leaves feeling looked after. That feeling is what brings her back.
