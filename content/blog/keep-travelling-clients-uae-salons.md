Every summer, a familiar pattern plays out in salons across Dubai and the wider UAE. A regular client mentions, almost in passing, that she is travelling home for six weeks. A family books one last round of cuts before the school holidays, then disappears until September. Nobody has done anything wrong — no complaint, no bad haircut. Yet when autumn arrives, some of those chairs stay empty, not because the client left angry, but because the trip quietly broke the habit.

That is what makes travelling clients such a quiet retention risk for UAE salons. Here, a long absence is completely normal: expatriate clients travel to visit family, escape the summer heat, or spend the school break elsewhere. The danger is not the gap itself. It is what happens during it: the client tries a salon back home, finds something convenient, and by the time she is back in the UAE, rebooking with her old salon feels like effort rather than routine.

The salons that keep travelling clients do not wait until September and hope. They plan a little before departure, stay in light touch while the client is away, and make the return easy. None of it requires discounts or dramatic gestures — just noticing the trip early and making the return simpler than starting somewhere new.

## Start at the last appointment before they leave

The most important retention moment for a travelling client is the appointment just before she goes. Ask, plainly and warmly, how long she will be away and whether she has anything special planned — a wedding, a family event, a holiday where she wants to look her best. That conversation turns a routine visit into a travel plan.

Timing matters too. A colour done the day before a long flight may fade awkwardly by the time she returns, while a cut timed a week or two before travel usually holds its shape better. For humid climates, a lower-maintenance style that survives heat and swimming beats a high-maintenance look.

Before she leaves, agree on the return in principle. It does not need to be a firm booking months ahead, which many clients resist — a simple "Let's get you in during your first week back, around mid-September" plants the idea. Record the expected return window in her notes, along with what was done at the last visit, so nobody starts from scratch when she reappears. That note turns a returning client into a welcomed-back regular instead of a stranger who must explain everything again.

## Stay in touch lightly while they are away

Once a client is travelling, the worst thing a salon can do is go silent for three months and then send a generic "We miss you!" broadcast in September — by then, she may already have booked elsewhere. The other extreme is just as bad: weekly promotional messages on holiday, which feel like spam and teach her to mute the salon's number.

The middle ground is one or two personal messages during the whole trip. A short WhatsApp message halfway through, from the stylist by name, works well: a quick check-in, a useful tip for the climate she is in, and no pressure to book anything — for example, a leave-in tip if humidity is making her hair frizz.

This is also where good enquiry handling pays off quietly. A travelling client who messages from abroad — asking about a return slot, a price, or whether her usual stylist is available — is actively trying to come back. If that message sits unanswered overnight because it arrived outside salon hours, the moment can pass. ## Make the return easier than trying somewhere new

The week a client returns to the UAE is busy: unpacking, school runs, work catch-up, jet lag. Booking a salon appointment is one more task on a long list, easy to postpone — so salons that win the return remove that friction in advance.

A message a few days before the client is due back works better than one a month after she has returned. Keep it specific and easy: name the stylist, suggest two concrete times in the first week back, and remind her that her notes are on file. "Sara has Thursday at 6 pm or Saturday at 11 am free in your first week back — shall I hold one?" is far more effective than "Book your next appointment now." The first is a decision; the second is a chore.

When she does return, make the visit feel like a continuation, not a restart: greet her by name, reference the trip briefly, and pick up where the last appointment left off. A small welcome-back gesture — a scalp massage, a favourite tea, a few extra minutes of consultation — costs almost nothing. What it should not be is a panicked discount, which teaches clients that leaving earns a reward. And never guilt-trip a client about her absence: most have not chosen another salon, they have simply drifted, and a warm message reaches a drifter where a defensive one pushes her away.

## A simple seasonal system any salon can run

None of this needs complicated software — just a single list and three moments in the year. In May and June, staff note expected departures and return windows as travel plans surface. In July and August, one personal check-in goes out to clients who are away. In September, return messages go out just before clients land, with concrete times offered.

Review the list once the season ends: who came back within two weeks, who took longer, and who never came back? Those answers show whether the system is working. Over two or three summers, patterns emerge — clients who had a pre-travel consultation, for example, may return far more reliably than those who simply disappeared. That knowledge lets a small salon compete with a big chain: not by outspending it, but by remembering its clients better.

Travelling clients are not lost clients. They are loyal clients on a pause, and a pause only becomes a goodbye when nobody bridges it. Plan the last appointment well, stay in touch once while they are away, and make the first appointment back the easiest booking of their week. Do that consistently, and the summer exodus becomes what it should be: a short break before familiar faces return.
