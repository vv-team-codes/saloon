Every successful salon in the UAE eventually hits the same wall. Your chairs are full and your prices are fair, yet a whole segment of the market walks past your door. They like your work, but your prices are above what they will pay every month — while your regulars would be unhappy if you dropped your prices to win those walk-ins. One brand cannot serve both groups, so some owners build a second one.

A second, budget brand is not a second branch of the same salon. It is a different name, a different price point and a different promise, aimed at a different client. Done well, it grows your revenue without touching your premium reputation. Done badly, it confuses your clients and splits your team. The difference is in how separate you keep the two.

## Why one brand cannot serve two markets

A premium salon sells more than a haircut. It sells the location, the ambience, the senior stylist and the unhurried hour. That experience costs money to deliver, and the price has to reflect it. If the same salon starts offering a quick cut at half the price, the premium client notices — and wonders what she is paying for.

At the same time, the budget end of the market is enormous. Students, young professionals and families in the suburbs all need a salon, and they choose on price and convenience. A salon that only serves the top of the market leaves that entire segment to competitors. Two brands solve the tension: the premium salon keeps its prices and identity, while the budget brand competes on speed, simplicity and value, in a different location, under a different name.

## Keep the brands apart, or do not bother

The single most important rule is separation. If clients can tell the two salons are the same business, the premium brand loses its shine and the budget brand loses its point. That means a different name, a different look and a different menu, and ideally a different neighbourhood.

A premium salon in Jumeirah or Downtown and a budget salon in a busy community area like International City or Al Qusais are not competing with each other — they are serving different streets. Put them side by side and you have simply opened two doors to the same room.

Keep the menus apart too. The budget brand should offer a tight list of fast, high-volume services: cuts, blow-dries, basic colour, threading and waxing. It should not try to match the premium salon's signature treatments or bridal work. A short menu keeps the budget salon fast and profitable, and it gives the premium salon something the other one cannot offer.

## Staffing two brands without doubling the team

The fear that stops most owners is staffing. A second salon sounds like a second payroll and twice the hiring headaches. In practice, the smartest owners share what can be shared and separate what must be separate.

Junior stylists and trainees often thrive in the budget salon. It gives them volume — twenty cuts a day instead of six — and volume is how a young stylist gets fast and confident. Your senior stylists stay in the premium salon, where their experience justifies the price.

Management is the harder part. One owner cannot run two salons day to day without one of them slipping. The budget salon needs a supervisor you trust, someone who owns the till, the diary and the standards on site. Many owners promote their most reliable senior from the premium salon into that role and backfill the chair behind them. Suppliers and stock can be shared quietly behind the scenes — buying in larger volumes for two salons usually earns a better rate — but never let the shared supply chain become a shared identity in front of clients.

## The numbers: when the second brand pays

A budget salon makes its money on volume, not margin. A premium salon might earn well from six clients a day; a budget salon needs twenty or thirty. That means location matters more than anything. Footfall is the budget brand's oxygen — a busy community street, a mall corridor or a spot near a metro station will do more for it than any advertising.

Work the sums before you sign a lease. Add up the rent, the fit-out, the salaries and the product cost, then divide by the average ticket. If the average ticket is 60 dirhams, you need a realistic number of clients a day to cover the costs — including in the slow summer months, when many residents travel.

Set a simple rule for the first year: the budget salon must pay its own way. Do not let the premium salon quietly subsidise it month after month, or you will never know whether the second brand is a business or a hobby. Review the numbers weekly, and be ready to change the menu, the hours or the prices in the first three months.

## The traps that sink second brands

The most common failure is the premium salon's standards creeping into the budget one. An owner who cannot bear to see a quick blow-dry done in twenty minutes starts adding the premium rituals, products and time — and suddenly the budget salon costs as much to run as the premium one, but charges half the price. Protect the budget brand's simplicity as fiercely as you protect the premium brand's quality.

The second trap is the reverse: budget habits leaking into the premium salon. A team member who has spent a month rushing through cuts in the budget salon brings that pace back to the premium chairs, and a regular notices immediately. Keep the teams mostly separate, and be clear that each salon has its own standard of service.

The third trap is the owner's time. Two salons will happily take all of it, and the premium salon — the business that made the second one possible — is usually the one that suffers first. If you cannot hire a manager or supervisor for the second brand, you are not ready for it.

A second brand is a bold move, but it is a proven one in the UAE, where the gap between premium and budget is wide and both ends of the market are growing. Start with separation, staff it honestly, and judge it on its own numbers.
