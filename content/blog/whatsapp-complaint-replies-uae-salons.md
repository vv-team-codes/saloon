A client who is unhappy at the chair is a problem you can see. You can read her face, lower your voice, and fix the colour before she leaves. A client who is unhappy on WhatsApp is a different kind of problem. You are staring at a message that arrived at 10pm, photo attached, and every unread minute is one she spends deciding whether to tell her friends, leave a one-star review, or never come back.

Most UAE salons are excellent at the first kind of recovery, quietly bad at the second. WhatsApp complaints are not harder to handle — just easier to handle badly, because writing makes every word permanent and every delay visible.

## Why a WhatsApp complaint feels harder

At the chair, tone does half the work. On WhatsApp, tone is whatever the reader imagines, and an unhappy reader imagines the worst. A short reply like "ok noted" reads as dismissive. A long explanation reads as defensive. A message sent at midnight reads as panicked, and a message sent the next afternoon reads as if you did not care.

There is also the screenshot problem: a spoken conversation disappears, but a WhatsApp message can be forwarded to a group chat in two seconds — and in a city where salons live and die by word of mouth, that is a real risk.

## The first fifteen minutes decide everything

Speed is not about solving the problem at 10pm. It is about showing the client she has been heard. A complaint that gets a warm acknowledgement within fifteen minutes almost never escalates. One that sits unread until the next morning has already grown a story around it: the salon does not care, the salon is hiding, the salon will never reply.

Acknowledge fast, even when you cannot fix anything yet. A good first message sounds like this: "Thank you for telling us, and I am sorry the colour did not turn out the way you wanted. I have passed this to the salon manager and we will message you first thing tomorrow morning with a plan to fix it."

That message thanks her, apologises for the outcome, names a real person and a real time, and promises a plan — not a vague "we will look into it".

If your salon closes at 9pm and nobody reads WhatsApp until morning, that gap costs you clients. A 24/7 AI receptionist like ZYNA can acknowledge the message instantly, take the details, and make sure the right person sees it first thing — so no complaint spends a night unanswered.

## The four-part reply that actually works

When you reply properly, follow four steps in order — skip one and the reply wobbles.

First, acknowledge the specific problem. Not "we are sorry for the inconvenience" but "I understand the balayage came out warmer than the ash tone you asked for." Specificity proves you read the message and looked at the photo.

Second, empathise without over-apologising: one sincere apology is enough — three sound like a script.

Third, own what you can and explain what you cannot. If the colour can be corrected, say so plainly and name the fix: a toner, a gloss, a re-do with a senior stylist. If the result she wants is not achievable on her hair, say so honestly now — clients forgive an honest no far more easily than a hopeful yes that fails twice.

Fourth, end with a concrete next step and a time. "Can you come in on Thursday at 5pm? Sara will do the correction herself and there will be no charge." A client with a time in her diary stops being angry and starts being a client again.

## What never to write

Some replies feel reasonable and damage you in writing. Never argue the facts in the chat. "Actually you asked for warm tones" is a sentence that has never saved a client, even when it is true. If there is a genuine misunderstanding, raise it gently in person or on a call, where tone can soften it.

Never hide behind policy in the first message. "As per our policy, corrections must be within 48 hours" reads like a wall. If a policy genuinely applies, explain it after you have offered something human.

Never ask her to delete anything, and never reply to a complaint in a group chat or a broadcast list — move it to a private chat immediately.

## When to move it off WhatsApp

Some complaints should not be settled in writing at all. If the client is very upset, if a skin reaction is involved, or if three messages have passed without progress, pick up the phone. A two-minute call does what ten messages cannot: it proves a real person cares.

Offer the call yourself: "Would it help if I call you for two minutes so we can sort this properly?" It gets you out of the written record before either side says something regrettable.

And if she needs to come back in, make the return visit feel like a welcome, not a summons. Book her with a senior stylist at a named time, and make sure whoever greets her already knows the story. Nothing reopens a wound faster than a client having to explain her complaint to a stranger at reception.

## After the fix, the follow-up is the retention

Most salons stop once the correction is done — a mistake, because the follow-up message is where a complaining client becomes a loyal one. A short message the same evening — "Hope the toner settled well, let us know how it looks in daylight tomorrow" — costs thirty seconds and shows her the salon remembers her.

Then write it down: the complaint, the cause, and the fix on her client record, so no one ever makes her explain it again. If the same problem appears twice in a month, it is no longer a complaint. It is a pattern, and patterns need a team conversation, not another apology.

## The quiet truth about complaints

A client who complains and is handled well is often more loyal than a client who never had a problem. She has seen how your salon behaves under pressure — and in a market as competitive as Dubai's, that trust is worth more than any discount.

So treat every WhatsApp complaint as a second chance, not a threat. Reply fast, reply like a person, fix it properly, and follow up. Most salons will not do all four — the ones that do keep clients that other salons lose.
