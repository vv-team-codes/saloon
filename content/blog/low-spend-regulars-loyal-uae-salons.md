Every UAE salon has her. She books the same small service every two or three weeks — threading, a fringe trim, a quick blow-dry — pays the smallest bill on your menu, and leaves. She has been doing it for a year, and she never books colour, a facial, or a product. Every time she walks in, some part of you wonders whether she is worth the chair.

She is. The low-spend regular is one of the most misunderstood clients in a salon. The mistake is treating her small bill as a small relationship. It is not. It is a habit — and habits are exactly what retention is built on.

## Why she books small

Before you try to grow her spend, understand why it is small. It is rarely that she cannot afford more. Usually it is one of four things.

First, routine. She has a rhythm — threading every fortnight, a blow-dry before the weekend — and she does not think of the salon as a place to explore. The small service is simply her appointment. She is maintaining, not shopping.

Second, trust. She has not yet decided whether your salon is her salon. A big service — colour, a facial, a treatment — is a leap of faith, and she takes it only when she is sure. The small service is her way of testing you, visit after visit, without saying so.

Third, time. The client who books a twenty-minute service often does so because she has twenty minutes. She is between the school run and a meeting, or fitting a visit in before the evening rush. She is not rejecting your menu. She is buying what fits her day.

Fourth, money — sometimes. Some clients genuinely have a tight budget, and a salon that respects that earns a loyalty no discount ever buys. The client who feels judged for a small bill does not come back with a bigger one. She does not come back at all.

## Stop treating her like a problem

The fastest way to lose a low-spend regular is to make her feel like one. It happens in small ways: the sigh when she books threading again, the stylist who suggests colour on every visit, the front desk that only lights up for the big bookings. Clients notice all of it, and in Dubai a salon that makes someone feel small will lose her to the salon two doors down before the week is out.

Treat her visit like any other booking. Greet her by name. Remember her details — how she likes her brows, that she takes her coffee without sugar, that her daughter just started school. The welcome costs nothing, and it is the whole reason she keeps choosing your chair over a cheaper one. She is also your most reliable marketing: the client who comes every fortnight talks about her salon every fortnight.

## Grow the visit, not the bill

The instinct is to upsell her. Resist it. The low-spend regular does not respond to a sales pitch; she responds to a suggestion that fits the visit she is already having.

The rule that works is one small, relevant step at a time, and never on every visit. She is in for threading? Once, when the timing is right, mention the brow tint that would make the shape last longer — one sentence, then drop it. She is in for a blow-dry before a dinner? Mention the ten-minute express treatment that would hold the style through the evening. The suggestion must solve something she already cares about, in the moment she is sitting in your chair.

Accept the no gracefully. "No thanks, just the usual today" is not a rejection of your salon. It is information. The client who is allowed to say no keeps coming back, and the client who keeps coming back eventually says yes — usually to something she chose herself.

## The maths that makes her worth keeping

A client who spends AED 60 every fortnight is worth AED 1,560 a year, and she costs you almost nothing to keep — no advertising, no convincing, no chasing. She books herself, turns up on time, and rarely cancels. Compare that with the new client who spends AED 400 once and never returns. She is also your quietest source of new business: the one who tells her sister, her colleague, her neighbour.

## What never to do

Never make a service conditional on a bigger spend. Never tell her the small service is not worth a booking at peak hours — if you need the chair at 7 pm, manage it with a booking rule that applies to everyone, not a remark aimed at her. Never let a stylist refuse a small service because it is not worth their time. And never use a discount to try to move her up the menu. A discount teaches her that the bigger service is only worth having at a lower price.

If she ever drifts away — the gap between visits stretches, the messages go unanswered — treat her like any other quiet regular. One personal WhatsApp message, warm and specific, mentioning something real: the tint she liked, the appointment time she prefers. Not an offer. Not a blast. A message from a salon that remembers her is usually enough.

## A simple 60-day plan

Start this week. List your ten most frequent low-spend regulars. For each one, write down one thing you know about her that has nothing to do with money: her routine, her timing, the occasion coming up in her life. Then, over the next sixty days, do three things: greet each of them by name and remember one detail; offer one relevant, low-pressure suggestion each, once, and accept the answer; and invite one of them to try a small complimentary add-on during a quiet hour.

Do not measure the result in week one. Measure it in month three, when you look at the same ten names and see who is still booking — and who has quietly added a second service to her visit. That is what retention looks like when it works: not a grand gesture, but a small client who never had a reason to leave, and one day decided she wanted a little more.
