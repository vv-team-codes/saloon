Most salons in the UAE close their diary at eight or nine in the evening. The chairs are wiped down, the lights go off, and the last WhatsApp message of the day goes unanswered until morning. Meanwhile, a client is messaging at 9.40 PM asking whether anyone can fit in a blow-dry before tomorrow's early flight — and she is ready to pay extra for it.

That message is the late-night opportunity. In Dubai and Abu Dhabi, evenings run late: people finish work at six or seven, have dinner, and only then think about themselves. Mall salons watch footfall peak after sunset, and clients who work long hours often cannot visit before eight. Most salons simply leave that demand on the table. Late nights are exhausting, and one tired team member working until midnight can undo a whole week of good service. The answer is not to say yes to every late request. It is to offer late-night appointments deliberately, as a defined service with its own rules, price and limits.

## Decide what late actually means for your salon

Late-night means something different in every salon, so define it first. For a high-street salon, late might mean staying open until ten on Thursdays and Fridays. For a mall salon, it might mean taking bookings until the mall closes. Write it down: which days, which hours, which services, and how many chairs. A vague promise of "we can stay late" becomes a nightly negotiation. A defined offer is something you can plan around, staff properly and price honestly.

Start small. One or two late evenings a week is enough to test demand without disrupting the team. Thursday and Friday are the natural choice in the UAE, as the weekend begins and clients prepare for weddings and gatherings. Track how many of those slots fill over four weeks before you expand.

## Price it like a premium service, because it is one

A late-night appointment costs your salon more than a daytime one. The stylist is working outside normal hours, the building may charge extra for air conditioning and security after closing, and you are giving up the team's recovery time. Charging the same price as a 2 PM appointment makes no business sense, and it teaches clients that late is normal.

Add a clear late-evening supplement — a fixed amount or a percentage on top of the service price — and state it at booking time, not at checkout. Clients accept a premium when they understand what they are paying for: privacy, convenience and a stylist who is there specifically for them. What they do not accept is a surprise. Put the late rate on your price list and in the booking confirmation.

Resist the temptation to discount late slots to fill them. A discounted 10 PM appointment still costs you the same tired team, but at a lower margin. If a late slot does not fill at the premium price, the lesson is that demand is not there on that day — not that the price is wrong.

## Protect the team or the service will not survive

Late-night bookings only work if the people delivering them are willing and rested. Forcing a stylist who started at ten in the morning to take a 10 PM client is how good staff leave. Build late evenings into the rota instead of bolting them onto the end of a normal day.

The simplest model is a late shift. One or two team members start at noon or one o'clock and finish at ten, while the rest of the team works the normal day. Rotate the late shift so the same people are not carrying it every week, and pay it properly: a late-shift allowance, a share of the premium charged, or time off in lieu. A service designed with the stylists will last; one imposed on them will not.

Set a hard finish time and defend it. If the last appointment starts at 9 PM, say so, and do not let a 9.30 PM walk-in stretch it to midnight. Clients respect a boundary stated clearly in advance.

## Choose the right services for late hours

Not every service belongs in a late slot. A full colour correction that needs three hours and careful checking is a poor fit for 9 PM, when everyone is tired and mistakes are more likely. A blow-dry, a root touch-up, a manicure or a brow appointment fits beautifully. Late-night services should be ones your team can deliver well when tired.

A late appointment should also be confirmed with a deposit, because a 9 PM no-show cannot be replaced — there is no walk-in trade at that hour to fill the chair. Take the deposit at booking and send a confirmation on the day, so one cancellation does not cost you the whole evening.

## Make the booking easy at the hour the client is asking

Late-night demand arrives late. The client who wants a 10 PM appointment is usually messaging at 8 or 9 PM, after her own day is done. If your booking channel closes when the salon does, she will message a competitor who answers. Someone has to receive the request, check the diary and confirm the slot — even if the salon floor is empty.

## Keep it small, keep it premium, keep it honest

The salons that make late nights work treat them as a small, deliberate part of the diary — two evenings a week, a handful of chairs, the right services, a fair price and a rested team. They do not apologise for charging more after nine. Clients who truly need a late appointment are happy to pay for it, because the alternative is not getting the appointment at all.

Start with one late evening a week for a month. Note which services book and which clients return. If the slots fill and the team is willing, add a second evening. If they do not fill, you have lost very little — and you have learned that your clients' evenings belong to their families, not to your chairs.
