A regular client leaves your salon, opens Google Maps on the way home, and leaves a one-star review. No phone call first, no message to the front desk, no chance to fix it in the chair. Just one star, a few sharp sentences, and your salon's name attached to it for every future client to read.

The instinct is to feel attacked, to explain yourself, or to ignore it and hope it sinks down the page. None of those work. A public review is not only about the client who wrote it. It is a conversation happening in front of everyone deciding whether to book with you. Handled well, it can keep the client and quietly win you new ones.

## The review is not the problem — the silence after it is

A one-star review is rarely the first sign of trouble. It is usually the last one. The client may have felt rushed at her last visit, or the colour came out warmer than she expected, or she waited forty minutes past her appointment time on a busy Saturday evening. She did not complain in the salon because most people do not. They smile, pay, and leave. The review is where the complaint finally lands.

That is useful information. A client who writes a review still cares enough to tell you what went wrong. The ones who simply never come back tell you nothing. Your job is not to win an argument. It is to win back a person — and to show everyone reading that your salon takes its clients seriously.

Speed matters more than perfection. Reply within a day, ideally within a few hours. A fast, calm reply tells the client she was heard. A review that sits unanswered for a week tells her the opposite.

## Reply in public, fix in private

The golden rule is simple: reply in public, fix in private. The public reply is for the readers. The real conversation — the apology, the detail, the fix — happens in a private message or a phone call. Never argue about what happened in public, and never ask the client to explain herself in front of an audience.

A good public reply has four parts, and it can be short. Thank her for telling you. Apologise for the experience, not for her feelings — "we are sorry the colour did not turn out as you expected" is honest; "we are sorry you feel that way" sounds dismissive. Say what you will do, in one line. Then invite her to a private conversation with a named person and a direct way to reach them.

For example: "Thank you for telling us, Sara — we are sorry your colour came out warmer than the shade we discussed. Please message us on WhatsApp and ask for Mariam, our senior colourist, and we will book you a free correction at a time that suits you." Warm, specific, and it ends with an action, not a defence.

What never to write: a long explanation of why she is wrong, a mention of your policies, or a sentence that starts with "but". Readers do not read a defensive reply and think the salon was right. They read it and think the salon is difficult. One calm paragraph beats five paragraphs of justification every time. Remember who else is reading: a woman choosing a salon reads your reply as a preview of how she would be treated if something went wrong.

## The fix visit: make it easy to say yes

Once she replies privately, the goal is one thing: get her back into the chair, on her terms. Do not ask her to come in "whenever" — that puts the work on her. Offer two specific times, ideally within the week, and make the correction free. A free correction is not a loss. It is the cheapest retention spend in your business, far cheaper than winning a brand-new client through advertising.

Before she arrives, brief the stylist on exactly what the client was unhappy about, in her words, and agree on the plan — the worst outcome is a second disappointing visit. Welcome her warmly: a genuine apology in person and an unhurried appointment do more than any free treatment.

## The follow-up that earns the edit

The fix visit is not the end of the story. A day or two later, send a short personal WhatsApp message asking how the colour is settling — mention the service, not just "hope you are well".

Only after she says she is happy should you gently mention the review. Never ask her to delete it or promise her anything in exchange. A simple line is enough: "We are so glad you love it — if you ever feel like updating your review to reflect the visit, we would really appreciate it." Many clients will update it on their own, and an updated review — one star turned into four, with a note that the salon fixed it — is more persuasive than ten perfect reviews. It shows future clients what happens when something goes wrong. And in a salon, something eventually will.

If she does not update it, leave it alone. Chasing her about it undoes the goodwill you just rebuilt.

## A small system so the next one never sits unread

None of this works if reviews sit unnoticed for days. Turn on notifications for your Google Business Profile so a new review reaches someone the same day. Give one person ownership — the owner or the front-desk lead — and agree on a rule: every review gets a reply within one working day, and any review below three stars gets a phone call or a WhatsApp message the same day. Keep two or three saved reply templates for common situations, but always personalise them with the client's name and the actual service.

A one-star review feels like a loss. Treat it as a second chance instead. Reply fast, fix it properly, follow up personally, and let the readers watch you do it well. The client you win back is often the most loyal client you will ever have — because she has seen how you behave when things go wrong, and she knows she can trust you.
