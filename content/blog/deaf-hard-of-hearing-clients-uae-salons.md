She books on WhatsApp and arrives on time. The stylist asks a question while facing the mirror, the client nods, and the cut begins. Twenty minutes later the stylist realises the nod meant nothing — the client never heard the question. She is deaf in one ear, or hard of hearing, or her hearing aid is losing to the dryers, and she did not want to make a fuss. She will not complain. She simply will not come back.

Hearing loss is one of the most common invisible conditions, and it walks into UAE salons every day. In a city of dozens of nationalities and every age, a salon that communicates well for deaf and hard-of-hearing clients is not doing something extra — it is doing the basics properly, and winning clients competitors quietly lose.

## Hearing loss is invisible — and most clients will not announce it

Roughly one adult in six lives with some hearing loss, and the number rises with age. Add hearing aids that pick up every dryer, clients who lip-read without saying so, and visitors still learning English, and you have a salon full of people who miss more than you think.

Most will never tell you — hearing loss carries a quiet stigma, and many people would rather nod along than explain. A client who agrees to everything and seems vague about the price may simply not have heard you.

So the goal is not to spot every hard-of-hearing client — it is to run the salon so it works for them whether they tell you or not.

## Start with the consultation — written down

The consultation is where the trouble starts. It is usually a chat, with the stylist looking at the hair in the mirror rather than at the client's face. A client who lip-reads gets nothing from the back of a head.

Fix it with paper. Keep a short written consultation card at every station — service, length, colour, price, time — and fill it in together before anything is cut or coloured. Show photos, point, write the price and circle it, then ask her to confirm it face to face. Two minutes of writing prevents the classic salon disaster: a client leaving with a service she never agreed to.

For colour, write the shade name down too. If she returns in six weeks, the card tells the next stylist exactly what happened — no guesswork.

## Five habits that cost nothing

Most of what helps costs nothing — a set of habits the whole team can learn in one short session.

Face the client when you speak. Never talk to the back of a chair, or while her head is under the dryer or in the basin. Wait until you can see her face, and she can see yours.

Do not shout — it distorts words and embarrasses everyone. Speak clearly, at a normal pace, and keep sentences short. One instruction at a time beats a paragraph.

Get her attention first — a tap on the shoulder, a wave in her line of sight, or stepping into view. Never talk to someone who does not know you are talking.

Keep one quieter chair. Music and dryers are the enemy of hearing aids, so one station away from the speakers, where the music is lower, becomes the chair your hard-of-hearing clients ask for by name.

Write down the numbers — price, time, next appointment date — always in writing, on the card or by WhatsApp. Spoken numbers are the easiest to mishear and the most expensive to get wrong.

## The moments salons forget

Some parts of the visit are built, without anyone noticing, for perfect hearing.

Calling a client's name across a busy salon does not work if she cannot hear it. Send a WhatsApp message — "Your stylist is ready, chair 3" — or walk over and catch her eye. A wave beats a shout every time.

The basin is the worst spot for communication: water running, head back, stylist speaking from behind. Give instructions before she lies back, keep them short, and tap her shoulder before you move her or rinse.

Processing time is a long silence for a client who cannot follow the room's chatter. Check in visually — walk past, make eye contact, hold up a mirror or colour chart — and she will feel looked after instead of forgotten.

At checkout, show the bill rather than reading it aloud, and confirm the total in writing. Then send the next appointment by WhatsApp while she is still in the chair, so the date and time land on her phone instead of getting lost on the way out.

## Let the technology do the talking

UAE salons already run on WhatsApp — a gift for deaf and hard-of-hearing clients. Text is clear, it can be re-read, and it leaves a record both sides can check. Make it the default for confirmations, reminders, and rebooking, not a fallback.

A 24/7 AI receptionist for salons and beauty businesses in the UAE, like ZYNA, answers enquiries in writing at any hour — exactly the channel many deaf and hard-of-hearing clients prefer. No call to strain to follow, no message lost overnight.

Inside the salon, the cheapest upgrades are a printed visual menu with prices, so a client can point instead of explain, and a phone buzz for the waiting area. No renovation needed — a printed menu and a team that texts instead of shouts covers most visits.

## Train the team once — and ask, don't assume

None of this works if only one stylist knows it. Book one thirty-minute team session: practise facing the client, the consultation card, and the basin handover. Role-play it once, with the music on and a dryer running, and the team will understand in minutes what instructions never convey.

One rule matters most: ask the client what works for her. Some lip-read, some prefer writing, some hear well with an aid in a quiet corner. Never assume, and never make a show of it. One quiet question at the start — "What's the easiest way for me to explain things today?" — is respectful, practical, and almost always met with relief.

Get this right and the reward is more than one happy client. Deaf and hard-of-hearing clients are among the most loyal regulars a salon can win, because so few places make the effort. They bring their sisters, their mothers, their friends; they rebook, they recommend, and they stay — because in your salon they were fully understood.
