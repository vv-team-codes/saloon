A parent messages your salon on WhatsApp at 9 pm. "Hi, can you cut my daughter's hair? She's four. How much is it?" Behind that simple question sit worries the parent will not say out loud: will the stylist be patient, will my child sit still, will it cost as much as an adult cut?

How you answer decides whether the family ever walks through your door. Get it right, and you have not just booked one haircut — you have very likely won a household that returns every four to six weeks for years.

## What parents are really asking

The typed question is almost never the full question — parents are usually asking five things at once.

Is my child old enough? Parents of toddlers are unsure whether salons accept two- and three-year-olds at all.

Will your team cope? A four-year-old wriggles, changes her mind, and may cry when the cape goes on. The parent wants to know whether your stylists are genuinely comfortable with children.

How much will it cost, and how long will it take? Parents budget carefully, and they need to plan around school runs, naps and siblings.

Can I stay with my child? Many parents assume they will sit beside the chair. Saying this clearly in advance avoids an awkward moment at the door. And is it safe? Parents worry about sharp scissors near small ears. A good reply answers the question asked and settles the ones that were not.

## The four-part reply that books the visit

A good reply follows the same shape every time.

Start with a warm yes and the age rule. "Yes, we love cutting children's hair — we welcome little ones from age two." If your salon has a minimum age, state it plainly.

Next, name the stylist and the approach. "Sara is our children's stylist. She is patient with first-timers, and parents may sit right beside the chair." Naming a person turns a faceless salon into someone the parent can already picture.

Then give the price and the time together. "A children's cut is AED 60 and takes about 30 minutes." Together, they let the parent decide on the spot.

Finally, close with two real slots. "We have Thursday at 4:30 pm or Saturday at 10:00 am — which suits you better?" Two concrete options turn a chat into a booking.

## Three questions worth asking before you confirm

Before you lock the slot in, ask a few quick questions.

Ask the child's age and whether this is a first haircut — a first cut deserves a little extra time and ceremony.

Ask about the hair itself. Is it curly, thick, very fine? Has it been cut before, and did it go well?

Ask what changes how you work. Does the child dislike clippers? Does she need to hold a tablet or a favourite toy? These small details are the difference between a calm visit and a distressed one.

## Pricing a children's cut honestly

Most UAE salons charge less for a child's cut than an adult's, and that is fair when the cut is simpler and quicker. But be honest about where the line sits: a straightforward trim on a six-year-old is one price, while a full restyle on a teenager with long, thick hair is closer to an adult service. Parents accept that when you explain it before the scissors come out, not after.

Say the price in the first message, before the booking is confirmed. A parent who discovers the price at the till feels tricked, however reasonable it is.

Consider a small first-haircut ritual. A certificate with the date, a lock of hair in a little envelope, a photo with the parent's permission. It costs almost nothing and turns a nervous first visit into a memory the family keeps.

## Setting the visit up to succeed

The booking is only half the job. The visit itself needs planning, especially where a child's appointment sits between adult clients.

Time it well. Weekday late mornings and early Saturday appointments work best for young children, before tiredness and hunger set in. The 7 pm evening rush is the worst time for a first haircut.

Prepare the chair before they arrive. A booster seat, a clean cape that fits a small body, a step so the child can climb up herself. A child who feels big enough for the chair behaves like a big girl.

Keep the pace unhurried. A thirty-minute cut needs thirty minutes in the diary, not twenty squeezed between two adult appointments. And have a plan for tears: pause, let the parent comfort the child, offer a drink, try again.

## When the honest answer is no

Not every salon can cut every child's hair, and saying so clearly is better than a bad visit.

If your salon does not accept children under a certain age, say so in the first reply and explain why. If no children's stylist is working on the day the parent wants, offer the next day they are in. If your salon is a quiet, adults-only space, be honest about that too, and recommend a family-friendly salon nearby if you know one.

A polite no, given quickly with a real alternative, often brings the parent back later — for their own appointment, if not their child's.

## The follow-up that wins the family

The visit is over, the child is happy, the parent is relieved. What happens next decides whether this family becomes regulars.

Send a short message that evening. "Thank you for bringing Layla today — she did brilliantly. Her next trim will be due in about five weeks, and we would love to see you both again." Personal, specific, and free of any hard sell.

Note the details — the child's name, the style, what worked. When the family returns and your team remembers, the parent feels known rather than processed.

Children's haircut enquiries are small messages with large consequences. Answer them warmly, completely, and fast, and one nervous parent's question becomes a family that books with you for years.
