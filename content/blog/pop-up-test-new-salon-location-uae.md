A new salon location can double your business, or it can quietly drain it for two years. The difference is rarely the area itself. It is whether real clients there actually book with you, at prices that work, often enough to cover the rent.

Most owners find that out after signing the lease. A pop-up lets you find out before. For a few weeks, you run a small, honest version of your salon in the area you are considering — a handful of services, one or two stylists, a booking link and a WhatsApp number — and you measure what happens. If the numbers hold up, you sign with confidence. If they do not, you have spent a fraction of a fit-out and learned something no property viewing could tell you.

## What a pop-up test actually is

A pop-up is not a launch party or a marketing stunt. It is a short, working trial of your salon in a new place. Two to four weeks is right: long enough for word of mouth to work, short enough to keep costs small.

Keep the offer narrow. Pick four or five services you already do well — a blow-dry, a cut, a gel manicure, a brow shape, a quick facial — and price them exactly as you would in a permanent salon. A pop-up that undercuts your own prices tells you nothing about whether the location works at real prices.

The space can be small: a mall kiosk, a borrowed corner in a gym or boutique, or a hotel spa room on quiet weekday mornings. What matters is whether the people walking past are the people you want as clients.

## Pick the test, not just the place

Start with the question you need answered. "Would clients in JVC book with us?" is a different test from "Would a mall location beat our high-street shop?" A mall kiosk over three weekends tells you whether passing footfall converts into bookings. A weekday pop-up inside a gym or residential building tells you whether residents book mid-morning, when your main salon is usually quiet. Before you start, sort the trade licence and permissions — a pop-up stopped mid-test by paperwork teaches you nothing and costs you twice.

## Measure bookings, not footfall

The mistake owners make with pop-ups is counting the wrong thing. A hundred people stopping to look at your kiosk means very little. Twenty bookings from people who live or work nearby means a great deal. Decide your pass mark before the first day.

Track five numbers every day: bookings taken, and how many came from walk-ins versus WhatsApp; conversion, meaning how many enquiries turned into a booked chair; average bill per client, at your normal prices; repeat intent, meaning how many clients booked a second visit before they left; and enquiry volume after hours, because a location that only works while you are standing there is not a location at all.

A useful pass mark for most UAE salons is this: the pop-up covers its own running costs within the test period, and at least a third of clients rebook or leave a number for the opening list. If it clears that, the location is worth a serious look. If it fills chairs only when you discount, or most clients came from your existing salon's following rather than the new area, the location itself has not proven anything yet.

## Cost it honestly

A pop-up is cheap compared to a lease, but it is not free. Budget for the space rental, a basic fit-out you can move — a chair, a mirror, lighting, towels and product — plus staffing, a small amount of local advertising, and the cost of the hours your team is not spending in your main salon.

That last cost is the one people forget. If your best stylist spends three weekends at a pop-up, your main salon loses her busiest hours — so staff it with one senior and one junior, or run it only on your quietest days, such as Sunday to Tuesday mornings. The test should not damage the business you already have.

Keep a simple daily log of money in, money out, bookings and enquiries. At the end of four weeks you will have a real picture instead of a feeling, and a real picture is what a lease decision deserves.

## Turn test clients into opening-day clients

The pop-up does more than test demand. It builds the client list for the day you actually open. Every person who books should leave with a reason to come back: a founding-client list on WhatsApp, a first-visit offer for the permanent salon, or simply a message when the doors open. Ask for permission to message them, and keep the list separate from your main salon's list so you know exactly how many future clients the new location has already earned. ## When the answer is no

Sometimes the pop-up tells you the location is wrong, and that is a successful test. Maybe the footfall is real but the spending is not. Maybe the area is full of salons and clients already have a favourite. Walking away after four weeks costs you a small amount. Walking away after signing a three-year lease costs you a great deal more.

A no also tells you what to test next. If the pop-up worked on weekends but not weekdays, the area might suit shorter hours. If clients loved the services but balked at the prices, the area might suit a different menu.

## A simple four-week plan

Week one is setup: licence and permissions, the space, the menu, the prices, and a short WhatsApp message to existing clients who live near the new area. Week two is the first real test, with a little local advertising — a mall poster, a community group post, a few flyers in the building. Week three is the adjustment week: fix whatever is not working, whether that is the hours, the services or the way you greet walk-ins. Week four is decision week: run the numbers against the pass mark you set at the start, and decide. If the numbers pass, you sign knowing what your first month looks like. If they do not, you close the pop-up and keep the list for the next location you test. Either way, you have replaced a guess with evidence — the cheapest insurance a salon owner can buy.
