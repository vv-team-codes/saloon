Every salon owner in the UAE knows the feeling. Your busiest stylist hands in her leave dates, and the first thing you do is open the diary: two weeks, right across the busiest stretch of the month, her Saturday already full, and three colour appointments only she has ever done.

This is not a crisis. It is a planning exercise, and the salons that handle it well start the moment the dates are known, not the week before she flies out. A planned absence is the easiest diary problem to solve, because you get something an emergency never gives you: time.

## Start With the Dates, Not the Diary

Agree the exact dates, including her last working day and her first day back. In the UAE, leave often lines up with school holidays, summer travel or a trip home, so some of her clients may be travelling too.

Before you touch a single booking, work out three numbers: her appointments in those two weeks, how many are regulars who book only with her, and how much revenue those two weeks normally bring in. That tells you whether you need a gentle reshuffle or a proper cover plan.

Agree the rules with her now: which clients can another stylist look after, which should wait until she returns, and what notes each client needs — the exact colour formula, the cut she has been growing out, the product she is allergic to. If that knowledge lives only in her head, it leaves with her, so get it into the client record before her last day.

## Tell Her Regulars Before They Hear It From Someone Else

Clients do not mind that their stylist is on leave. They mind finding out at the last minute, or arriving to find a stranger holding the scissors. Tell them early, and tell them personally.

A short WhatsApp message a few weeks ahead does the job. Name the dates, name the stylist, and offer the choice in the same message: book with a named colleague, or move the appointment to the week she returns. No generic broadcast — a client who has seen the same stylist for two years deserves a message that sounds written for her.

Some regulars will quietly decide to wait, then quietly drift somewhere else when the wait feels too long. If a client has not replied within a few days, a short follow-up from the front desk is worth it. One message now is cheaper than winning a client back later.

## Match the Client to the Right Stand-In

The biggest mistake during leave cover is treating every empty chair as interchangeable. A client who books a balayage with a senior colourist does not want the most junior stylist just because she happens to be free, and a client who comes for a quiet, unhurried cut does not want the fastest stylist on the floor.

Pair each regular with one named colleague, and tell the client who that person is and why: she knows your hair, she has done your colour before. "Someone will look after you" is not a reassurance. Where a service is genuinely too specialised to hand over — a correction, a complex colour, a bride's trial — say so honestly and offer the return date instead. Clients respect honesty far more than a brave attempt that goes wrong.

Give the covering stylist a proper handover, not just a name on a list: ten minutes with the client record, the colour formula and a quick word from the stylist herself is the difference between a smooth appointment and a nervous one.

## Reshape the Diary Around the Gap

Two weeks without your busiest stylist is not the time to run the diary exactly as usual. Open her most popular hours to the covering stylists first, and protect the peak evening slots — the 6 to 9 pm window when working clients in Dubai and Abu Dhabi finally get free — for the appointments that bring in the most revenue.

Be honest about capacity. If the diary cannot hold every appointment, do not squeeze clients into rushed slots. A slightly shorter service menu for two weeks, with the most time-heavy services paused, keeps quality up and the team sane — clients would rather have a solid cut this week than a rushed colour that needs fixing next month.

Talk to the team before the leave starts: who covers which regulars, who handles walk-ins, who takes the extra Saturday shift. Agree it in advance and share the extra work fairly — a covering stylist who feels dumped on will not give your regulars her best work.

## Protect the Money, Not Just the Diary

Leave cover is also a revenue question: a fortnight with your top earner away can quietly cost a salon thousands of dirhams, and the damage shows up weeks later in lost regulars, not in one empty day.

Two habits help. First, bring forward what you can: clients happy to come a few days early are doing you a favour. Second, pre-book the return — before a regular leaves her cover appointment, book her next visit with her usual stylist for the week she is back.

Keep your deposit rules the same during leave cover — a client who has paid a small deposit is far more likely to turn up.

## The Return Week Matters as Much as the Leave

The plan is not finished when the stylist walks back through the door. Her first week back should already be filling up before she lands: message her waiting regulars a few days before she returns, with two or three real slots to choose from. The clients who waited deserve first pick of her diary.

Note who accepted cover, who waited and who went quiet — that tells you who needs a personal message in her return week.

The strongest salons in the UAE are the ones where clients trust the salon, not just one person in it — and a well-handled leave is one of the best ways to build that trust. Plan early, communicate honestly, treat every regular like someone worth keeping, and when the systems around the diary work properly — including the way enquiries and bookings are answered around the clock — even the busiest fortnight runs calmly.
