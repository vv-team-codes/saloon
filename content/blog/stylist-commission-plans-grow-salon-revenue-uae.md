Walk into a Dubai salon on a busy Saturday evening and you will see chairs full and stylists moving fast. Walk in on a quiet Tuesday morning and you will see the same stylists, paid the same amount, waiting for the phone to ring. A flat monthly salary pays your team the same whether the chairs are full or empty — and that is the problem. It gives your best stylists no reason to fill an extra chair or turn a one-time visitor into a regular.

Commission changes that. Done well, it links what your team earns to what the salon earns: stylists take home more, clients get more attention, and the salon grows without adding a single chair. Done badly, it turns colleagues into rivals and drives clients away. The difference is in the design.

## Start with a stable base, then add commission on top

The first rule is that nobody should be afraid of a slow week. In the UAE, where many stylists have rent and family commitments, a plan that is all commission and no base pay creates anxiety, not motivation. Your best people will leave for a salon that offers security.

A structure that works for most UAE salons is a fair base salary plus commission on top. The base should cover a stylist's essentials so a quiet week is uncomfortable, not frightening. A common split is a base salary plus 10 to 20 percent commission on services, rising as monthly revenue targets are hit. A stylist bringing in AED 25,000 of services in a month might earn 10 percent; at AED 35,000, the rate steps up to 15 percent. Tiers like this reward growth.

Keep the maths simple enough to explain in one minute: one plain-language page with two worked examples beats a complicated spreadsheet.

## Pay commission on the right things

Commission on services is the obvious starting point, but the behaviours that actually grow a salon are worth rewarding directly. Retail is one: when a stylist recommends the shampoo a client genuinely needs and the client buys it, that sale is worth a small commission, often 10 percent, because it builds revenue without taking up chair time. Rebooking is another: a small bonus per client rebooked before she leaves the chair is money well spent.

Do not pay commission on everything equally. A cut that takes 45 minutes and a blow-dry that takes 20 are not the same contribution, and a flat rate per service will quietly push stylists toward the quick jobs. Pay a percentage of the service price and the balance takes care of itself. And calculate commission on the discounted price when a stylist offers 20 percent off — otherwise you are paying your team to give your money away.

## Protect the teamwork or the plan will backfire

The biggest fear salon owners have about commission is that it turns the floor into a competition. That fear is justified when the plan rewards only individual numbers. If stylists hover at reception to grab walk-ins, or refuse to help a colleague whose client is waiting, your regulars will notice.

Build a team element in from day one. A small monthly team bonus, shared equally when the salon hits its revenue target, reminds everyone that a full diary helps all of them. Make the rules crystal clear: walk-ins go to whoever is free, not whoever is fastest to the door, and a client who asks for a colleague by name belongs to that colleague.

## Run the numbers before you promise anything

Before you announce a new plan, sit down with three months of real figures: what each stylist brings in, what the salon spends on products, rent and utilities, and what the commission would have cost. A plan that looks generous on paper can quietly eat your margin if your service prices are low or your product costs are high. In general, total pay including commission should sit between 40 and 55 percent of the revenue a stylist generates.

Test the plan for one month first. Tell the team it is a trial, pay exactly what the plan promises, and review the numbers together at the end of the month. A trial gives you the freedom to fix problems without breaking a promise.

## The mistakes that kill commission plans

The most common mistake is changing the rules without warning. Nothing destroys trust faster than a stylist hitting a target and discovering the target moved. Once agreed, leave it alone for at least three months; if you must change it, give a full month's notice and explain why.

The second mistake is paying late or paying wrong. Commission must arrive with the salary, on time, every month, with a simple breakdown the stylist can check. If your team has to chase you for money they earned, the plan breeds resentment, not motivation.

The third mistake is secrecy. You do not need to publish salaries, but the rules, tiers and team target should be visible to everyone — transparency turns commission from a management trick into a shared goal.

## Roll it out like a change, not an announcement

A new pay plan is a change to people's livelihoods, so treat it that way: gather the team, explain why you are doing it, walk through the examples, and invite questions. Expect pushback from stylists comfortable on a flat salary, and listen to it: sometimes the worry is fairness, not money, and a small adjustment to the tiers can win them over. Put the final plan in writing, one page per stylist, signed by both sides.

The best time to start is a month before your busy season, when the diary is about to fill and commission is easy to earn — in the UAE, the weeks before Ramadan evenings, Eid or the winter wedding season, when clients book colour, styling and treatments. A stylist who earns a noticeably bigger pay packet in the first month will believe in the plan more than any speech from the owner.

A commission plan is not a magic fix for a salon with empty chairs: if enquiries are not coming in, no pay structure will fill the diary. But once clients are arriving, commission is the simplest way to make sure your team cares as much about a full diary as you do.
