Every salon owner in the UAE knows the scene. A client arrives for a colour appointment, sits in the chair, and only then does the stylist discover she had a reaction to colour at another salon two years ago. Or a bride books a full package and nobody asked whether she is allergic to henna. The conversation that should have happened days ago is suddenly happening in front of a full diary.

Consent forms are not paperwork for paperwork's sake. They are the five-minute conversation that protects the client, the stylist and the business — but the traditional clipboard at reception, filled in while the stylist is already mixing colour, is too late to be useful. The fix is simple: collect consent on WhatsApp before the client ever walks in.

## Why WhatsApp is the right place for consent

In the UAE, the booking conversation already happens on WhatsApp. A client messages at 10 pm about balayage prices, and by the time she has a slot she has exchanged a dozen messages with your salon. Sending the consent form through that same chat feels natural, not bureaucratic. She taps a link, answers a few questions and sends it back from her sofa.

There is a second advantage. WhatsApp keeps a record. When the form comes back in the same thread as the booking, the stylist sees the client's answers alongside the service, the price and the time. Nothing gets lost between the front desk and the chair.

## What a good consent form actually asks

Keep it short: a form that takes ten minutes will not be completed. Five or six questions are enough for most services.

Start with the essentials: full name, the service booked and the date of the appointment. Then ask the questions that change what happens in the chair. Has the client had a reaction to hair colour, bleach, henna or skincare products before? Does she have sensitive skin, eczema or any scalp condition? Is she pregnant or breastfeeding, if the service involves strong chemicals? Has she had the same treatment elsewhere in the last six weeks? Finally, a plain confirmation that she understands the service, the expected result and the aftercare, and agrees to proceed.

For colour and chemical services, add one more line: confirmation that a patch test was done, or that the client has chosen to proceed without one and understands the risk. That single line matters most when something goes wrong — and it is the line most salons never collect.

## When to send it — and what to say

Send the form when the appointment is confirmed — for most UAE salons, 24 to 48 hours before the visit. Too early and it gets forgotten; too late and there is no time to act on the answers.

The message itself should be short and warm. Something like: "We're looking forward to seeing you tomorrow at 6 pm for your balayage with Sara. Before your visit, could you please fill in this quick two-minute form? It helps Sara prepare everything for your hair type and keeps you safe. Here is the link." That is all it takes — no legal language, no sense that the client is signing her life away.

If the form has not come back by the morning of the appointment, send one gentle reminder. Most clients reply within the hour. If it still has not arrived when she walks in, have a paper copy or a tablet ready at reception.

## What to do when an answer raises a flag

The whole point of collecting consent early is that you can act on it before the client is in the chair. A previous reaction to colour means time to plan a patch test or choose a gentler formula. A scalp condition means extra time or a different service. A bride's allergy means time to test an alternative — not a discovery on the wedding morning.

Build a simple rule: any form with a "yes" to a health or allergy question gets flagged to the senior stylist before the appointment. The stylist decides whether to proceed, adjust or call the client — never the person answering WhatsApp at 11 pm. Give the team one clear instruction: flag it, don't guess.

## Making it easy for clients to say yes

Clients complete forms they trust. A form asking for an Emirates ID number or a home address for a haircut will make people hesitate, and rightly so. Ask only for what the service genuinely needs: name, contact number, the service, and the health questions that relate to it.

Make the form work on a phone, because that is where it will be opened. Big buttons, short questions, no tiny text. A free online form tool is enough to start; the technology matters far less than the habit of sending it every time.

## The quiet business benefits

The safety case alone justifies consent forms, but owners who start collecting them on WhatsApp notice three other effects. First, no-shows fall: a client who has already answered questions about her hair has invested in the appointment, and invested clients turn up. Second, chair time is used better. The stylist reads the form before the client arrives, so the consultation starts from knowledge, not from zero. Third, the salon looks professional. A client who receives a clear, caring form before her visit tells her friends, because most salons still hand her a clipboard after she has sat down.

## Start small, then make it a habit

You do not need a new system or a new member of staff. Pick your two highest-risk services — colour and chemical treatments are the obvious starting point — and send the form for those bookings only. Run it for two weeks, watch how many forms come back, and ask your stylists whether the answers helped.

The habit is what matters: a form sent for every colour booking, every time, beats a perfect form sent only when someone remembers. Put the send step into your booking checklist, next to confirming the time and taking the deposit.

Your clients already trust you with their hair, their skin and their wedding morning. Asking a few careful questions before they arrive is not bureaucracy. It is the salon saying, clearly and in advance, that their safety comes first.
