Every growing salon reaches a moment when the owner cannot do it alone any more. The diary is full, regulars ask about a second branch, or a spa room sits empty because there is no money to fit it out. Then someone offers to help — a friend with savings, a regular client who loves the salon, a family member who wants in. A partner sounds like the answer — sometimes it is, and sometimes it is the decision that costs an owner the business she built.

A partnership is not a rescue. It is a growth tool, and it works only when it fits the job. Before you say yes, work through the decision slowly, in writing, while you still have the freedom to walk away.

## When a partner actually makes sense

A partner earns their place when they bring something the salon genuinely cannot get another way: money the business needs to grow, a skill the owner does not have, or time and presence in a second location.

A second branch is the classic case. One owner cannot be in two salons at once, and a partner who runs the new branch day to day is worth far more than a silent cheque. A spa extension is another: if the partner is a qualified therapist who can build and run the spa side, the partnership adds revenue and expertise at once.

But check first whether the problem is really a partner-shaped problem. If the salon needs cash for a few quiet months, a short-term loan is cheaper than giving away a share of the business forever. A partner should unlock growth you cannot reach any other way — not patch a problem that has a simpler fix.

## What a good partner brings — and what they do not

Write down, in one sentence, what the partner brings. Money, a skill, a network of clients, or daily management. If you cannot write that sentence clearly, you do not have a partnership — you have a vague arrangement, and vague arrangements are where salons lose money and friendships.

Be honest about what money alone is worth. Cash can be borrowed, repaid and forgotten; equity is forever. A partner who brings only money is the most expensive money a salon will ever take.

## The four questions before you say yes

Before any talk of percentages, answer four questions together, out loud, in the same room. First, what exactly does each person bring — money, skill, time, clients — and what is each contribution worth? Second, what does each person get: a share of the business, a share of the profit, a salary, or some combination? Third, who decides what? One person must own the diary and the team, one must own the money, and both must know which is which. Fourth, how do you split if it fails? A partnership that starts with an honest answer to the last question is the one most likely never to need it.

## Put the roles in writing

Most salon partnerships fail on the same rock: two owners, no clear division of labour. One books clients the other would have turned away. One discounts a service the other priced carefully. Each thinks the other is in charge of the stock.

Write a simple one-page agreement on roles before you sign anything formal. Who runs the floor day to day? Who hires, and who can let someone go? Who approves spending above a set amount — say AED 2,000? Who answers the enquiries after hours? One name next to each job, not two.

## Money: equity, loan or profit share

There are three honest ways to structure the deal, and they are very different. Equity means the partner owns a share of the business itself. It is permanent, and it is the right choice when the partner will work in the business for years. Keep the majority if you built the brand — control matters more than the exact percentage when the two of you disagree.

A loan means the partner lends money and gets repaid with interest or a fixed return. It ends, and it keeps control with you. It is the right choice when the partner brings only money. A profit share sits in between: the partner takes a percentage of profit for a fixed period, or a percentage of one branch's profit, without owning the business. Whatever you choose, get the numbers written down and checked by someone who understands UAE trade licences — a change in ownership can mean a change to the licence.

## The trial before the signature

Never sign a partnership on enthusiasm. Trial it first: agree a defined project with a clear end date — sixty days running the new spa room, or the December party season at the second branch — with agreed pay and a review date. Sixty days of working together will tell you more than sixty meetings ever will.

Watch for red flags during the trial: a partner who wants control but puts in no money and no hours, who will not put anything in writing, or who pushes to sign before the trial ends. Any one of these is a reason to stop.

## A calmer alternative worth considering

Sometimes the right answer is not a partner at all: a silent investor with a fixed return and no say in daily decisions, a profit-share deal with a senior stylist who already brings clients, or a manager hire that frees the owner to grow the business. Each gives you the help you need without giving away the business you built.

If you do take a partner, the goal is simple: a business bigger, calmer and more valuable than the one you had — with you still in charge of it. If it feels like a tug of war before you have even signed, trust that feeling. The best time to protect your salon is before the paperwork, not after it.
