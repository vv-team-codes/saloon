Every year, a few weeks before Ramadan begins, the same message starts landing in UAE salon inboxes: "Are you open late during Ramadan?" Then a second one: "What time do you close during Ramadan?" And a third, usually after midnight: "Can I come at 11pm?"

These are not casual questions. They come from clients who already intend to book — they just need to know when the salon is open. Ramadan flips the UAE salon day upside down, and the salons that answer the hours question clearly, early and in one message win the bookings.

## What clients are really asking

Behind "are you open late?" there are usually three hidden questions. First, what are your exact hours during Ramadan — not "we open late" but the real opening and closing times. Second, can I book the slot I actually want, which is almost always after iftar. Third, will the service I want still be available that late, or do colour and long treatments stop earlier?

Answer all three in your first reply and the enquiry is nearly a booking. Answer only the first and you will get a follow-up at midnight asking the other two, when nobody is at the desk.

## Answering the hours question in one message

Decide your Ramadan hours at least two weeks before the month starts, and write them down in one place that every member of the team can see. Then build one short reply you can send to every enquiry, on WhatsApp, Instagram or the phone:

"During Ramadan we are open from 12pm to 12am, with our last colour appointment at 10pm and last cut at 11pm. Would you like me to hold a slot for you after iftar this week?"

Notice what that message does. It gives exact times, it separates the services that need an earlier cut-off, and it ends with a booking question instead of a full stop. A client who gets that answer can decide in seconds. A client who gets "yes, we are open late" still has to ask three more questions, and every extra message is a chance to lose her.

Pin the same timings to your WhatsApp business profile, your Instagram bio and your Google Business Profile the day you decide them. Many Ramadan enquiries never become messages — the client checks your profile, sees nothing about Ramadan, and moves on.

## Staffing the post-iftar rush

The pattern in most UAE salons during Ramadan is the same: quiet afternoons, then a wave from around 8:30pm that runs until closing. The mistake is staffing the day as if it were a normal month, which leaves the morning overstaffed and the evening overwhelmed.

Plan the rota around the wave. Start shifts later, so your best stylists are fresh at 9pm rather than tired by it. Keep someone watching the inbox until closing, because the 10pm and 11pm enquiries are often the most valuable ones: clients who have finished iftar, made a decision and want a slot tomorrow. A 24/7 AI receptionist like ZYNA can answer the hours question and hold a slot at 1am, when no human is at the desk, so the client wakes up to a confirmed booking.

Protect the team too. Late finishes every night for a month burn people out, and tired stylists make mistakes on the clients who matter most. Rotate the late shift, give a proper break before the evening wave, and keep a senior stylist on the floor until the last appointment finishes.

## The quiet afternoons still earn money

The midday hours during Ramadan look empty, but they are not worthless. This is when you run the services that do not depend on walk-in traffic: pre-booked treatments, home visits, and the preparation work that makes the evening run smoothly — mixing colour, setting up stations, confirming the night's bookings.

It is also the right time to contact the clients who asked about Ramadan hours but never booked. One short message — "Just a reminder, we are open until midnight during Ramadan, and Thursday after iftar still has two slots" — turns an old enquiry into a new appointment. Do it in the afternoon, when you have time to reply properly, not at 10pm when the floor is full.

## Turning the enquiry into a booking

A Ramadan hours enquiry is a warm lead, and warm leads cool fast. Answer quickly, answer completely, and close with a specific slot. "Would Thursday at 9:30pm work for you?" books far more appointments than "let us know when you would like to come."

Take a deposit for the late-evening slots. The post-iftar hours are your most valuable hours of the month, and a no-show at 10:30pm cannot be refilled at 10:35pm. Clients understand this when it is explained plainly at booking: the deposit holds the chair, and it is moved, not lost, if they reschedule with a day's notice.

Keep a short waitlist for the last ten days of Ramadan and the nights before Eid. These are the busiest nights of the year in UAE salons. A client on a waitlist who gets a message at 6pm — "a 9pm slot just opened, shall I hold it for you?" — becomes a booking and a regular.

## What not to promise

Do not promise hours you cannot staff. Announcing midnight closing and then turning clients away at 11pm because the team has gone home does more damage than shorter hours honestly stated. Do not promise that every service runs until closing either — a full balayage started at 11pm finishes long after midnight, and nobody enjoys that, least of all the stylist.

Be honest about the last days before Eid as well. If you close early on the day before Eid, say so in advance. Clients plan their whole celebration around that final appointment, and a surprise closure loses more than one booking — it loses the trust that brings them back next Ramadan.

## After Ramadan, keep the relationship

When the month ends, the clients you won with clear answers and late-evening care do not disappear. Send a short thank-you message in the first week after Eid, not an offer, just an acknowledgement. The clients who booked three late-night appointments during Ramadan are your most loyal clients of the year.

Ramadan hours enquiries look like a small administrative question. Answered well, they are one of the best booking opportunities in the UAE salon calendar — a month when every client already knows what she wants, and is simply asking who will be open when she wants it.
