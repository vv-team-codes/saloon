It happens in salons across Dubai and Abu Dhabi every single day. A client books a simple cut, settles into the chair, and halfway through the conversation she says it: "Actually, can we add some colour today?" Or a regular who came for a blow-dry spots the treatment menu and asks for a keratin top-up.

The instinct is to say yes immediately. She is happy, she is already in the chair, and the extra service means extra revenue. But an on-the-spot change is never just an extra service. It is extra time, extra product, a different stylist workload and, if the diary is tight, a knock-on delay for the client booked after her. Handled well, it is one of the easiest ways a salon grows its average bill. Handled badly, it is how a calm evening diary turns into a waiting room full of annoyed clients.

## Why "just add it" is never just it

Every service in a salon has three hidden costs: the hands-on minutes, the processing minutes and the finishing minutes. A cut might be forty-five minutes of hands-on work. A root colour is perhaps thirty minutes of application, thirty-five minutes of processing and then a wash and blow-dry on top. When a client adds a service mid-visit, the salon is not adding one thing — it is adding a whole new timeline that has to fit around everything already booked. The risk is highest in the evening, when the UAE diary fills from around 5 PM: a thirty-minute overrun at 6 PM pushes the 6:30, the 7:00 and the 7:30 bookings later too.

## The thirty-second check before you say yes

The best salons do not refuse these requests, and they do not accept them blindly either. They run a quick check that takes about thirty seconds, while smiling and keeping the conversation going.

First, how much real time does the new service need, including processing and finishing? Second, what is booked after this client, and is there any slack in the diary? Third, does the client understand the new price and the new finish time before the work starts? If the answer to all three is clear, say yes with confidence. If any one of them is a problem, that is the moment to offer an honest alternative instead of a hopeful yes.

This check works best when the stylist does not have to do it alone. In many UAE salons the front desk watches the diary, so a stylist can get a yes or no in seconds. The client never sees the mechanics — she just hears a calm, confident answer.

## The processing-time trick

Here is the good news: many of the services clients add mid-visit include processing time, and processing time is the salon's best friend. While colour develops or a treatment sits, the stylist's hands are free. That is the window to finish the cut, tidy the next station or even start a short service for another client.

Smart salons plan for this deliberately. They keep one chair free during busy hours as a float, so a client in processing can be moved without blocking a stylist. They also teach stylists to overlap honestly: never start two clients' hands-on work at the same moment, but do use the quiet processing minutes productively.

## How to price it honestly on the spot

The awkward moment in a mid-visit change is rarely the time — it is the money. A client who agreed to a AED 120 cut did not budget for a AED 350 colour, and finding out at the till feels like a surprise charge, even when the work was worth every dirham.

Say the price before the first drop of colour touches her hair. A simple sentence does it: "We can absolutely do that today — it would be AED 280 on top of the cut, and you would finish around 7:15. Shall we go ahead?" She gets a real choice, with real numbers, while she can still say no gracefully. Salons that do this find that most clients say yes anyway — and the ones who decline come back and book it properly next time.

## When the answer has to be "next time"

Sometimes the honest answer is no — at least, not today. The diary is full, the stylist who does colour is on a break, or the client has a hard deadline like a flight or a dinner reservation. Saying no badly loses a client. Saying no well keeps one.

The formula is simple: a genuine reason, a concrete alternative and a booking made on the spot. "I would love to do the colour, but with your dinner at eight we would be cutting it too fine, and I don't want to rush it. What if we book you in on Thursday at six, same chair, and I will have everything ready?" The client leaves feeling looked after rather than refused, and the salon has a confirmed booking in a quieter slot.

## Train the team with one rule

None of this works if every stylist improvises. Agree on one house rule and repeat it until it is automatic: never promise an added service before checking the diary and the price. New stylists should hear it on their first day, and the front desk should know they are allowed to say no to an overlap that would hurt the next client.

A five-minute Monday huddle, looking at the week's busy evenings and the gaps where added services fit, gives the whole team the same picture.

Mid-visit changes are a sign of a healthy salon. Clients ask for more because they trust the stylist in front of them. The salons that turn that trust into revenue are not the ones that say yes to everything — they are the ones that say yes with their eyes open, price it before they start, and protect the client waiting after. Do that, and the chair booked for a cut quietly becomes one of the most profitable hours of the day.
