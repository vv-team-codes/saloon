A home visit looks like easy money on paper: a blow-dry at her villa, a home-visit fee, and a full service price without tying up a chair. Then the day happens. The stylist leaves JVC at 10, spends 40 minutes crossing town to Mirdif, and has nothing booked until 4 in Dubai Marina. Three hours have gone to the road, and the salon has lost a stylist for the afternoon peak. That is the difference between a home-visit service that grows the business and one that quietly drains it: the diary.

## Cluster by area, never by time

The first rule of a mobile diary is that geography comes before the clock. In the salon, appointments stack by time. On the road, they stack by place. Three clients in one community earn three fees and cost perhaps 20 minutes of driving. The same three clients spread across three areas earn the same fees and cost two hours.

Offer home visits in two or three areas a week — JVC and Al Barsha on one day, Mirdif and International City on another — and only on those days. When a client in a different area asks for a visit, she is offered the next day her area is on the route, not a slot tomorrow that sends a stylist across the city. The few who insist on a different day pay a higher travel fee, which covers the real cost of the trip.

## Travel time is a real appointment

Even two villas in the same community need 15 to 20 minutes between them, and a trip between communities can take 45 minutes or more. If travel time is not in the diary, it gets stolen from the next client, and the whole day runs late.

Give every home visit a visible travel buffer. A 60-minute blow-dry at a villa is a 90-minute diary block: 60 minutes of service, 15 of packing and setup, and 15 of travel. With the buffer in the diary, the stylist arrives on time.

## Protect the evening peak

Home visits are at their best when they fill time the salon would otherwise waste: the quiet weekday morning, the 11 o'clock gap between two in-salon bookings, the Tuesday afternoon when chairs sit empty. Between 5 and 9 in the evening, the salon is at its busiest, and every stylist taken out of the building costs more than a home visit earns.

So keep home visits out of the evening rush. Offer them in the morning and early afternoon, when the diary has room, and keep your best stylists in the building for the peak. A regular who can only do evenings at home is offered a premium evening slot or a weekend morning.

## One stylist, one route, one day

A home-visit day works best when it belongs to one person. Send one stylist out with a full route — four or five visits, all in one area, back to back — and she earns a full day's revenue from the road. Send three stylists out with one visit each and you have paid three travel costs for three fees and lost three people from the salon floor.

Plan the route the evening before, not on the morning. The stylist gets the addresses in order, the services, the client notes, and the kit list. She packs once and follows the route, while the salon knows where she is.

## The kit decides the booking

A home visit is only as good as the kit that arrives with it. A keratin service needs the product, the iron and ventilation the client's bathroom may not have. If the kit is wrong, the stylist discovers it at the client's home, and the appointment fails in front of the client.

So let the kit decide what can be booked at home. Keep a short list of services that travel well — blow-dries, manicures and pedicures, basic facials, simple root touch-ups, bridal trials — and keep the rest in the salon. Anything not on the list is offered in the salon, not as a compromise at home.

## Price the trip honestly

Home visits fail quietly when the price does not cover the real cost: the travel time, the fuel, the kit wear, and the salon chair that sits empty while she is out. A fee that only covers the drive leaves the salon poorer than if the stylist had stayed in the building.

Price from the real cost: the service price, plus a home-visit fee covering travel and setup, plus a premium for evening or weekend visits. Tell the client the full price when she books, in one message, so there is no surprise at the door.

## Confirm the day before, in writing

A home visit has one risk an in-salon booking does not: the address. A wrong building, a gated community that needs a code, a villa with no parking — any of these can eat 20 minutes and push the whole route late. So confirm every home visit the day before, in writing, with three things: the full address, the gate or building access details, and the stylist's arrival time. Ask the client to confirm all three back. If she does not reply, call her.

## Start small, then grow the route

Resist offering home visits everywhere, to everyone, from the first week — that is how a salon ends up with a stylist in three communities in one day and a diary full of apologies. Start with one area, one day a week, and one stylist. Fill that route for a month, then add a second day, then a second area. A mobile diary that grows slowly stays profitable; one that grows fast usually just grows busy.

In a city where clients live spread across villas, apartments and communities, the home visit is a real advantage for a salon that does it well — but only when the diary is planned like a route, not like a salon floor. Cluster the visits, write the travel time into the diary, protect the evening peak, send one stylist out with a full route, let the kit decide what travels, price the trip honestly, and confirm the address in writing.
