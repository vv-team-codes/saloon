Most salon delays do not start at the chair. They start before the client walks through the door. She arrives with wet hair when the colourist needed it dry. She wears heavy makeup to a facial. She has not eaten all day and feels faint halfway through a three-hour balayage. She forgot the photo of the hairstyle she wanted, and now the consultation takes twenty minutes instead of five.

The fix is a short message sent before the appointment. Not a reminder — a preparation message. It tells the client how to arrive, what to bring, and what to skip, so the service starts on time.

## The hidden cost of an unprepared client

Work out what fifteen lost minutes really costs. A salon with eight chairs running a full evening diary loses two hours a day when each appointment starts fifteen minutes late or runs over because of avoidable preparation problems. That is two hours that could have held two more paying appointments, or given your team a proper break before the 7 PM rush.

The cost is not only money. When the diary slips, the next client waits, the stylist rushes, and the whole evening feels frantic. Clients notice, and it shapes whether they rebook.

Preparation problems are also the quietest cause of disappointing results. Colour applied to hair full of styling product does not lift evenly. A facial on skin covered in waterproof makeup starts with ten minutes of removal instead of treatment. These are not skill problems. They are information problems, and a message solves them before they happen.

## What the message should cover

Keep it short — five lines at most, sent on WhatsApp where your clients already talk to you. Three things matter: how to arrive, what to bring, and what to skip.

How to arrive depends on the service. For colour, ask for clean, dry hair, washed the day before rather than the morning of the appointment, with no heavy oils or styling products. For a facial, ask the client to arrive with a bare face if she can, or at least without waterproof makeup. For nails, ask her to arrive with bare nails and no gel or acrylic already on, unless the booking includes removal. For a spray tan, ask for freshly exfoliated skin and loose, dark clothing to wear home.

What to bring is equally simple. The inspiration photo, if she has one — a screenshot is fine, and it saves the long description that never quite matches the picture in her head. For a bridal trial, the veil, the jewellery, and a photo of the dress neckline.

What to skip is the part owners forget to mention. Skip the gym session right before a blow-dry, because sweat undoes the work before she leaves. Skip the heavy lunch before a long colour appointment, but do not skip food entirely — a client who has not eaten will feel dizzy under the dryer. Skip the self-tan before a waxing appointment.

## One message, five versions

Do not send the same preparation message for every service. A cut does not need the same instructions as a bridal trial, and a generic message reads like spam. Write five short templates, one for each of your most-booked services, and save them as quick replies on the salon phone. A haircut template asks for clean, dry hair and a photo of the style; a colour template adds the no-oil rule and the appointment length; a facial template asks for a bare face; a nails template asks for bare nails; a bridal template asks for the veil and jewellery.

Each template should end with one warm line: if anything has changed — a new allergy, a different plan, a later arrival — reply to this message and we will adjust. That line alone prevents half the day's surprises, because it gives the client permission to tell you something has changed instead of hoping you will not notice.

## When to send it

Timing matters as much as content. Send the preparation message with the booking confirmation, while the client is still excited and actually reading your messages. Then send a one-line nudge the day before: looking forward to seeing you tomorrow, a quick reminder to arrive with clean, dry hair.

Do not send it two hours before the appointment — by then she is already on her way and cannot change anything. The day-before message is the one that changes behaviour, because she still has an evening to wash her hair, find the photo, or dig out the veil.

For first-time clients, the preparation message does double duty. It is also their first real impression of how organised your salon is. A salon that thinks ahead about the small details earns trust before the client has even sat in the chair, and that trust is what turns a first visit into a second.

## What never to put in it

Keep the message helpful, never scolding. Never write that late arrivals will be charged, or that unprepared clients will lose their slot — that belongs in your booking policy, not in a friendly preparation note. Never overload it with ten instructions. Three or four points, in plain language, is the limit. Short lines, one idea per line, and the client's name at the start.

## Making it automatic
