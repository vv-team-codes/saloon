A client books a haircut on WhatsApp. She arrives on time, keeps her scarf on, and asks quietly for a chair away from the window. She has not had a salon visit in two years — her hair is thinning after treatment, a scar runs along her hairline, or a skin condition has changed how she feels about sitting under bright lights in front of a mirror.

She is not a difficult client. She is a brave one — she has already done the hardest part by walking through your door, and the next hour decides whether she ever comes back. Most salons never realise how many of these clients they lose — the ones who leave never complain, they simply do not rebook.

## The first thing she notices is not your menu

Long before anyone picks up scissors, a client with hair loss or a visible difference is reading your salon. She notices whether the chairs face the street, whether the waiting area leaves her on display, and whether the front desk smiles or looks at her hair first.

None of this needs a renovation. A chair in a quieter corner, a cape that covers properly, a screen near one station, and a front desk that greets every client the same warm way. These small decisions change the whole visit. If your salon has a private room, offer it for the consultation. If it does not, a quiet corner at a quiet hour does the same job.

The point is not to hide her — it is to give her the choice. Ask, in a normal tone, "Would you like the quieter chair today, or the one by the window?" That one question tells her she is in control.

## Never ask. Let her tell you.

The biggest mistake a salon can make with these clients is curiosity. "What happened to your hair?" is a question no client should have to answer to get a haircut. She may volunteer the story herself — if she does, listen warmly and move on. If she does not, that silence is the answer, and it deserves respect.

Make this a rule for the whole team, not a suggestion. The stylist, the shampoo assistant, the receptionist and the trainee all need to know it — one careless comment can undo an hour of careful work. The rule is simple: talk about the hair in front of you, the service she booked, and the result she wants. Nothing else.

The same care applies on WhatsApp before she arrives. Many of these clients message first, late at night, when the question feels safer to type than to say. A gentle first reply matters more than a fast one — and a 24/7 AI receptionist like ZYNA can answer that first message kindly at any hour, with a booking link and a note about a quieter chair.

## Privacy is a practical decision, not a luxury

A client who is self-conscious about her appearance is not asking for special treatment — just the same dignity every client expects, with a little more care around the moments that feel exposed.

Think through the visit from her side. Keep the consultation low and unhurried, and sit beside her rather than standing over her. At the basin, check the water and ask if she is comfortable before you start. At the mirror, show her the result in stages instead of spinning the chair around for one big reveal. At the till, keep the conversation about the service, not about her.

Photos deserve their own rule. Never take a before-and-after photo of a client with hair loss or a visible difference without asking clearly first, and never post one without written permission, even with her face hidden. For most clients a photo is flattery — for this client, exposure. Ask once, accept the answer, and never ask again.

## Honest advice beats a brave promise

A client with thinning hair or a scarred scalp does not need a stylist who pretends everything is normal — she needs one who is honest about what a service can and cannot do, and kind about it.

That honesty starts in the consultation. If a colour will not take evenly on regrowth, say so before it goes on, and offer the alternative that will look best. If a short cut will show more scalp than she expects, show her with your hands first. If a treatment she read about online is wrong for her hair right now, explain why in plain words and suggest what to do instead.

This is where a salon earns a client for life. The salon that says, "Here is what I can do for you, and here is what I would wait on," is the one she trusts with the next appointment — and tells her friends about.

## The small habits that make her a regular

Regulars are not made by grand gestures, but by a salon remembering what matters to one client and getting it right every time.

Write it in her client notes, beside her colour formula — preferred chair, quieter hour, a particular stylist, no small talk during the wash, a reminder before her usual five-week booking. The next time she messages, the reply can say, "Your usual chair is free on Thursday at 6 PM — shall I hold it?" From a salon that remembered, that sentence is worth more than any discount. Keep the rebooking quiet and simple: two dates, a smile, and a "see you then".

## What to do when you get it wrong

Even a careful team will get it wrong sometimes — a new trainee stares, a stylist asks a question she should not have asked, a client overhears a comment that was never meant for her.

When that happens, do not pretend otherwise. A short, honest apology in the moment — "I am sorry, that was not okay" — followed by a visible change, is worth more than a free service. Then fix the cause — a word with the team member, a note in the file, a change to the station. Clients forgive mistakes. They do not forgive being ignored.

The salons that win these clients are not the most expensive ones in Dubai — they are the ones where a woman can take off her scarf, sit in the chair, and feel like any other client for an hour. Build that feeling into how your team works, and the clients who need it will become the regulars you are proudest of.
