Every salon owner in the UAE has heard the question, usually in a low voice at the front desk or in a late-night WhatsApp message: "Can I have the salon to myself for an hour?" Sometimes it is a bride who wants her trial without an audience. Sometimes it is a client who wears the hijab and wants complete privacy. Sometimes it is a well-known face in Dubai who does not want to be recognised mid-foil.

Most salons say no, or they say yes without thinking it through — and both answers cost money. A flat no sends a high-value client to the salon down the road that will say yes. A careless yes, squeezed between two full columns, leaves the team stressed and the client wondering why she paid extra. A private appointment is not a favour. It is a product, and it can be one of the most profitable hours on your diary.

## Who asks for a private appointment

The clients who ask value discretion, calm and control over their time, and they are usually happy to pay for it. In the UAE, three groups ask most often.

First, brides and bridal parties. A bride's trial, or the morning of the wedding itself, is the one appointment where privacy is not a luxury — it is the whole point. She wants her mother and sisters in the room, nobody else, and a stylist who is not glancing at the clock.

Second, clients who want ladies-only privacy. For many women in the UAE, the ability to remove a hijab, relax and be photographed by nobody is a condition of the visit, not a preference. A private slot is the strongest promise a salon can make on that front.

Third, public figures, influencers and senior executives. They are protecting their image and their time, and a salon that handles them discreetly earns a client who books weekly.

## What a private appointment actually looks like

A private appointment needs a clear definition, or it will mean something different to every client who books it. Decide yours before you sell it.

The simplest version is a closed-door slot: the client books the first or last appointment of the day, the front door is locked to walk-ins, and only her stylist and one assistant are in the room. This costs you almost nothing.

The fuller version is a whole-salon hire: the salon is hers alone for two to three hours, with the team she needs and space for her companions. This is the version brides and VIP clients want, and it commands a real premium.

Whatever version you offer, write it down: what is included, how long it lasts, how many companions can come, what happens if she runs late, and what happens if she cancels. A private appointment that is vague becomes a negotiation, and a negotiation in the middle of the appointment ruins the very calm she paid for.

## Pricing it so it pays

The most common mistake is charging the normal service price and absorbing the cost of the empty chairs. You are selling exclusivity, and that has a price.

The cleanest way to price it is a minimum spend: work out what the salon would normally earn in that hour if every chair were busy, and set that as the floor. If a full salon hour normally brings in AED 600 and the private slot ties up two stylists, a minimum spend of AED 800 to AED 1,000 is honest, not greedy. The client is not paying for a haircut. She is paying for the room, the silence and the undivided attention.

Offer the minimum spend as a package rather than a surcharge. "A private morning, including hair, makeup and refreshments for you and two guests, from AED 1,200" sounds like an experience. "Your blow-dry plus AED 400 for privacy" sounds like a penalty. The number is the same; the feeling is completely different. Be honest about what the price does not include, too.

## Fitting it into the diary

Private appointments only work if they sit in the right place in the diary. The first appointment of the morning and the last appointment of the evening are the natural homes. A private hour at 9 AM, before the salon fills, costs you nothing — the chairs would have been empty anyway. A private hour at 8 PM, after the last regular client has left, is the same. The expensive mistake is placing a private booking at 5 PM on a Thursday, when every chair would have been full and paying.


Staffing matters as much as timing: assign one person to the client and one to the front of house, and make it clear nobody else enters the room unless the client asks.

## The small details that make it feel private

The price gets the client through the door. The details are what make her book again. Start before she arrives: a short WhatsApp message the evening before, confirming the time, the door she should use, and that the salon will be hers alone. It also gives her a chance to ask the small questions she would not ask in front of strangers.



## Telling clients about it — softly

You do not need a loud campaign to sell private appointments — a loud campaign works against you, because discretion is the product. Start with the clients who have already asked. Reply to the next enquiry with a simple offer: "We do offer private appointments — the salon to yourself, first thing in the morning or in the evening. Shall I hold a time for you?" One honest sentence, sent at the right moment, converts better than any poster.

After that, let your regulars find out naturally: a small line on your service menu, a mention when a client books a bridal trial, a quiet word when a regular mentions she hates how busy Saturdays are. The clients who want this already know they want it.
