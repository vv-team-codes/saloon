A message arrives on a Tuesday evening: "I have very sensitive skin and I reacted to a hair colour once. Can I still get balayage?" Another client asks whether a facial is safe while she is pregnant. A third mentions eczema and wants to know if a keratin treatment will irritate her scalp.

These are not ordinary price or availability questions. They carry worry, and the way a salon answers them decides whether the client feels safe enough to book. A rushed reassurance — "Yes, no problem, come tomorrow" — might win the booking and lose the client for good.

## Why these enquiries need a different reply

Most salon enquiries are about choice: which service, which stylist, which time. A health-related enquiry is about risk. The client is checking whether your salon will take her seriously, and she may already have had a bad experience elsewhere.

That changes the tone. Speed still matters, but accuracy matters more. A reply that asks good questions, explains the next step, and is honest about limits will win a careful client over. In Dubai and across the UAE, where clients come from many backgrounds, that calm, professional tone is itself a reason to choose your salon. If the person replying is not trained for these questions, hand over to a senior stylist or therapist before promising anything. Guessing must never happen.

## Ask before you advise

The best first reply does not give an answer. It asks questions, in a way that feels caring, not clinical. Five questions cover most situations: what happened last time, and when? Which service are you considering? Do you have a known allergy, skin condition, or medical advice affecting this treatment? Are you using any medicated skin or hair products? Is there anything else the salon should know?

These questions do not ask the client to diagnose herself, and they do not invite the salon to diagnose her either. They gather facts so a professional can make a judgement, and they give the client permission to be honest. Many clients hide a past reaction because they fear being refused; asked matter-of-factly, they are far more likely to tell the truth.

## What never to promise in a message

Never promise that a product is hypoallergenic, safe for everyone, or will definitely not cause a reaction. Even products marketed as gentle can irritate an individual. Never tell a client that a treatment is safe during pregnancy, while breastfeeding, or alongside a medical condition. That is a decision for the client and her doctor, not for a salon chat.

Instead, use honest, specific language. "We use ammonia-free colour options and we can do a patch test before your appointment" is useful. "This is 100% safe for sensitive skin" is not. Clients told the truth about a small risk trust the salon more, not less.

## Patch tests, consultations, and honest timing

For colour, keratin, lash, and some facial treatments, the practical answer is often a two-step plan: a patch test or consultation first, then the full service. Present that first step as part of the service, not an obstacle.

Explain what the patch test involves and how far in advance it needs to happen — usually at least 48 hours before colour, though the salon's own policy and product guidance should govern. Offer a specific time, not a vague invitation to "pop in sometime". A client who hears "Come in Thursday at 6 pm for a ten-minute patch test, and if all is well we can book your colour for Sunday" has a plan. The same applies to timing: a sensitive-skin facial may need a longer slot or a senior therapist. Say so at the enquiry stage, and clients will accept it when they understand it buys care, not just time.

## Record it, respect it, and pass it on

An enquiry handled well in chat and then forgotten at the chair is worse than a slow reply. If a client mentions an allergy or a previous reaction, that information must reach the person doing the service, with the client's knowledge.

Keep the note factual and brief: the service, the concern, the product if known, and what was agreed, such as a patch test date or a product to avoid. Avoid speculation about a diagnosis, and share the note only with team members who need it. Clients notice when a stylist already knows, without being reminded, that they reacted to a particular product. That continuity often turns a cautious first visit into a regular booking.

## When the right answer is no — or not yet

Sometimes the honest answer is that the salon cannot safely do the requested service, or cannot do it yet. A client with an active scalp condition, or one who reacted to a patch test, needs a clear, kind refusal and a suggestion to speak to a dermatologist or doctor first.

A vague "we are fully booked" when the real reason is safety helps nobody. A respectful explanation — what the concern is, what would need to change, and what alternative the salon can offer — leaves the door open. Offer a gentler service, a consultation, or a future appointment after medical advice. Many clients return later, precisely because the salon was honest when it could have taken their money.

## Turning a careful question into a confident booking

Handled well, a health-related enquiry is one of the strongest bookings a salon can win. The client arrives already trusting the salon and expecting care rather than speed. To close it, end every such conversation with something concrete: a named service, a patch test or consultation time, and a note of what will happen next.

For example: "Thank you for telling us — that helps us plan properly. Let's book a patch test on Thursday at 6 pm, and hold a colour slot for you on Sunday at 5 pm." That is not a sales line. It is a plan, and plans are what worried clients book.

The salons that win these clients are rarely the ones with the loudest promises. They are the ones that ask good questions, answer honestly, and treat a health concern as the start of a relationship. Answer the careful question well, and the client will not just book once.
