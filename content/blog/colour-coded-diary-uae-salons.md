Walk into most UAE salons at 6 pm and you will see the same scene: a front desk juggling a ringing phone, a WhatsApp thread that will not stop, and a diary like a wall of identical text. The information is all there — nobody can read it fast enough.

That is the real cost of a plain diary. A gap that could have held a blow-dry hides between two long colour services, and a stylist's lunch break gets booked over because nobody saw it in small grey letters.

A colour-coded diary fixes this with one rule: every kind of appointment gets a colour, and the whole team uses the same colours every day.

## Start with five colours, not fifteen

The mistake salons make is trying to colour everything. Twenty colours is not a system; it is confetti. Start with five.

The first colour is for long services: colour, balayage, keratin, bridal work. These lock a chair for two hours or more, so they should be impossible to miss — and the front desk stops offering a colour slot at 7 pm when the salon closes at 9.

The second colour is for quick services: cuts, blow-dries, threading, express treatments. These are your gap-fillers — a bright block of quick-service colour shows exactly where a walk-in or waitlist client fits in a 45-minute hole between two colour appointments.

The third colour is for consultations and patch tests. Short, free and easy to forget — a coloured block stops them being rushed or skipped between paying clients.

The fourth colour is for anything that is not a client: breaks, training, stock counts, meetings. In a busy Dubai salon, a stylist's lunch looks identical to free time in plain text. Colour those blocks grey or soft blue, and nobody books over them by accident.

The fifth colour is for uncertainty: tentative holds, clients who asked you to "keep Tuesday in mind", bookings waiting on a deposit. Mark them clearly, with a rule that a hold turns into a confirmed colour or gets released by a set time.

## Colour by service, not by stylist

Some salons colour by stylist instead, but that answers the wrong question. When the phone rings, the front desk needs to know what the appointment is and how long it takes, not who is doing it. A diary coloured by stylist tells you Sara is busy at 5 pm; a diary coloured by service tells you a two-hour colour runs until 7, so the 6 pm blow-dry needs another stylist or another day.

## Make the key visible to everyone

A colour system only works if everyone uses the same colours. Write the key on a card at the front desk and another at the back station, put it in your team WhatsApp group, and repeat it in the first week until nobody has to ask. For a new receptionist, the key is part of first-day training.

Choose colours far apart — a deep green, a warm orange, a soft purple, a clear blue and a grey — rather than five shades of pink. If your diary is on a screen, check it in bright daylight: a mall salon's front desk is often flooded with light, and a colour that looks distinct in a dark office can turn into a smudge under a skylight.

## The 30-second morning scan

The real payoff shows up in the first minute of the day. Before the doors open, the person running the desk should scan the whole day in about thirty seconds and answer five questions: where are the long services, where are the gaps, who has no break, which holds need chasing, and what happens if someone runs late?

Time yourself with your current diary tomorrow morning. If it takes more than a minute to answer those five questions, your diary is costing you money — the same slow reading happens every time the phone rings.

The scan also catches problems while there is still time to fix them: a stylist with four long services and no break, or a day with three uncertain holds and no confirmed bookings after 5 pm. Seeing these patterns at 9 am gives you the whole day to fix them.

## When the day goes wrong, colours save the day

No diary survives contact with a real salon day. A colour runs over by forty minutes, a client arrives late, a stylist calls in sick. This is when a plain diary becomes genuinely dangerous, because the person rescuing the day has to re-read every line under pressure.

With colours, the rescue is visual: the front desk sees at once which quick services can move, which long services cannot, and where the next genuine gap sits. A two-hour colour block cannot be squeezed; a 30-minute block often can. The person on the phone offers a real alternative in seconds instead of putting the client on hold while they decode the diary.

This is also where the fifth colour earns its keep: when a crisis hits, the unconfirmed holds are the first chairs to release, and a diary that shows them clearly lets you free them in one move.

## Keep it simple for a month, then refine

Run the five-colour system for a full month before changing anything. At the end of each week, ask the front desk one question: which colour caused confusion? If two colours are regularly mixed up, change one. If a colour is never used, drop it.

Only after a month should you add anything — perhaps a colour for VIP or first-visit clients, so the team knows who needs extra care without reading a note. Add one at a time, and only when the team asks for it.

A colour-coded diary will not book a single appointment by itself. What it does is give every person in your salon the same clear picture of the day, so the right decisions get made quickly and the wrong ones get caught early. In a busy UAE salon, where the evening rush arrives all at once, that clarity is the difference between a day that feels chaotic and a day that feels in control.
