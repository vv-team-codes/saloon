It sounds like a strange idea at first. Dubai is one of the hottest cities on earth, and now salons are asking clients to sit in a heated cabin on purpose. Yet infrared saunas are quietly becoming one of the most talked-about additions to UAE salon menus — not as a gimmick, but because the treatment fits how people in the Emirates actually live.

Spend a day in a Dubai salon and the logic becomes clear. Clients arrive from 45-degree heat outside into air conditioning set to 21 degrees, then sit under it for an hour or two while colour processes. Shoulders tense and muscles stiffen. A short session of gentle, deep warmth feels less like extra heat and more like the reset the body has been waiting for.

## What an infrared sauna actually is

An infrared sauna looks like a small wooden cabin, but it works differently from the steam rooms and traditional saunas most people know. Instead of heating the air to very high temperatures, infrared panels warm the body directly, at a gentler 45 to 60 degrees. Most sessions last 20 to 40 minutes.

That gentler experience is exactly why it works in a salon. A traditional sauna at 90 degrees leaves a client flushed and exhausted, in no state for a blow-dry or a facial. An infrared session leaves most people relaxed, warm and ready for the next step of their visit.

Salons are honest about what it is and what it is not: a relaxation and wellness treatment, not a medical therapy. The salons doing it well describe the benefits their clients actually report — deep relaxation, loosened muscles and better sleep that night — and avoid promising anything they cannot stand behind.

## Why the UAE market fits heat therapy

Three things about life in the Emirates make infrared heat therapy an unusually good fit. The first is the climate paradox: people here spend most of the year moving between extreme outdoor heat and aggressive indoor cooling, and that constant switching leaves many with stiff necks, tight shoulders and poor sleep.

The second is the wellness culture around Dubai's gyms, hotels and spas. Clients here are already comfortable with recovery rooms, ice baths and breathwork classes. An infrared sauna does not need explaining to someone who already books a sports massage after a padel game.

The third is the winter season. From November to March, when the weather is at its best and social calendars fill up, salons see their busiest months. Evening appointments stretch late and clients want treatments that help them look rested and feel calm before big events.

## How salons are adding it to the menu

The practical side is simpler than most owners expect. A one- or two-person infrared cabin takes up roughly the space of a treatment bed, and many salons place it in a quiet corner or a spare room. Installation is usually a standard electrical connection — no plumbing or steam to manage.

Pricing is where owners need to think carefully. The salons getting the best results treat the sauna as a premium add-on rather than a standalone destination: a 20-minute session priced modestly on its own, then bundled with facials, massages and hair rituals at a small uplift.

The diary matters too. Heat therapy sessions are short, which makes them ideal for filling the awkward gaps between longer appointments. A 30-minute slot in a quiet mid-morning or mid-afternoon hour can be offered to regulars by WhatsApp the same day.

## Pairing heat with the services you already sell

The real money in infrared saunas is not the session fee — it is what the session does to the rest of the menu. A client who arrives tense and leaves relaxed is more likely to book the facial she was considering and to rebook before she leaves.

The best pairings come from thinking about the visit as a sequence. A sauna before a facial warms the skin and relaxes the client, so the facial itself feels better. A sauna after a deep-tissue massage extends the loosened feeling. Each combination needs testing on staff first and pricing so the client feels she got more than she paid for. Whoever runs the cabin also needs to know the basics: session length, when to offer water, and who should not use it — pregnant clients, anyone with a heart condition or very low blood pressure, and clients who feel unwell should be steered to another service, kindly and without fuss.

## Getting the launch right

The salons that have made heat therapy work share a few habits. They start with one cabin, not three, and let demand prove itself before expanding. They introduce it to regulars first, with a complimentary session attached to an existing booking, before they put it on the price list.

They also keep the promise modest. The marketing says relaxation, warmth and a calm half-hour — not detox miracles or medical claims. A client who comes back every fortnight is worth far more than one who tries it once because of an exaggerated claim and never returns.

They make it easy to book, too. Heat therapy enquiries tend to arrive the same way most salon enquiries do in the UAE: on WhatsApp, often late in the evening, often as a simple "do you have sauna?" message. Answering quickly, with a price, a duration and two real time slots, turns curiosity into a booking. ## A trend with staying power

Not every beauty trend survives its first season. The ones that do solve a real problem for real clients, fit the way a salon actually works, and earn their place on the menu through repeat bookings rather than first-time curiosity. Infrared heat therapy is passing those tests in UAE salons because it matches the climate, the wellness habits and the evening rhythms of the market.

For a salon owner weighing up the next addition to the menu, the question is not whether heat therapy is fashionable. It is whether your clients would use it twice a month, whether your diary has the gaps to hold it, and whether your team can deliver it calmly and honestly. If the answer is yes to all three, a small wooden cabin in a quiet corner might become the hardest-working square metre in your salon.
