Every salon owner in Dubai knows the same handful of names. They book every three weeks without being chased, ask for the same stylist, and bring their sister on a Saturday and their mother before Eid. They are the backbone of the business — and most salons treat them exactly like everyone else.

That is a missed opportunity. A regular who feels like an insider books earlier, tries the new treatment first, and forgives the occasional late start. When a new salon opens two doors down with a flashy opening offer, she does not think about switching — leaving would mean giving up a place where she belongs.

Making your best clients insiders needs no loyalty app or big budget — just one small system: a panel of loyal clients who get first access to new services and a say in what your salon offers next.

## Why belonging keeps clients longer than discounts

Discounts buy one visit. Belonging buys years. When a client helps choose a new facial or votes on the season's nail colours, she stops being a buyer and becomes a co-owner of the experience — and that shows up as a client telling her friends, "My salon just launched this. I helped pick it."

This matters more in the UAE than in most markets. Dubai and Abu Dhabi are full of clients who moved here from somewhere else and are still building their routines; a salon that makes them feel known becomes part of their new life. In a city where a new salon opens every month, that feeling is the hardest thing for a competitor to copy.

## Who belongs in the insider panel

Twenty to thirty clients is right for most salons — big enough for real opinions, small enough that every member still feels special. Invite everyone and it is a mailing list, not an insider panel.

Pick clients on behaviour, not on one big spend: members should have visited at least four times in the last six months, rebook before they leave, and reply when you message them. A fortnightly blow-dry client is a better insider than one who spent a fortune once at a wedding and never came back.

The test is simple: do they come often enough to notice change, tell you honestly when something is wrong, and already recommend you unprompted? Yes to all three, and they belong in the panel.

## What insiders actually get

The offer must feel real, not like a dressed-up discount card. Three things work best.

First, first access. When you are about to add a new service — a scalp treatment, a new colour technique, a seasonal facial — insiders try it before anyone else, at a preview price. Invite them on WhatsApp a week before the service reaches your menu, with a named stylist and two or three real slots.

Second, a real vote. Once a quarter, ask the panel one concrete question — which of these two treatments should we add next, which colours should we stock for winter — with two or three clear options, not an open-ended "any ideas?" Then actually do what they choose, and tell them you did. A vote that changes the menu teaches clients this salon listens.


## How to run it without adding work to your week

Keep it light enough to survive a busy month: one WhatsApp broadcast list, one rhythm.

A broadcast list beats a group chat: every client gets a personal message and nobody sees anyone else's number.

One message a month is enough, plus one preview invitation whenever a new service is coming. Keep it short — one thing that is new, one question, one booking link — and send it on a Tuesday or Wednesday evening, when clients are planning their weekend, not on a busy Friday afternoon.

Give one person ownership of the panel, usually the receptionist or salon manager, to send the messages and note replies in client records. With no owner, the panel quietly dies within six weeks. And because replies often arrive late at night, a 24/7 AI receptionist like ZYNA answering those messages and holding the booking means an insider's enthusiasm never cools while she waits for a morning reply.

## Use the panel to test before you spend

This is where the panel pays for itself. Every new service is a gamble — products to stock, training hours, diary space — and testing it on twenty loyal clients first turns it into a small experiment.

Before launch, invite the panel to try the service and ask three questions afterwards: would you book this again, what would you pay for it, and what would you change? Their answers tell you whether to launch, what to charge, and what to fix — before you print a menu or spend a dirham advertising it. A treatment insiders love sells itself.

One JLT salon launched a premium 90-minute scalp ritual with a full campaign, only to find clients thought it too long for a weekday — a panel test with fifteen regulars would have surfaced that in a week.

## What not to do

Three mistakes kill insider panels. First, turning it into a discount club: the moment insiders expect a percentage off everything, you have trained your best clients to wait for offers. First access and influence are the reward, not cheaper prices.

Second, inviting everyone. When a client realises the "exclusive" invitation went to every number in your phone, the feeling of belonging evaporates.

Third, asking and then ignoring the answer. If the panel votes for a service you never add, you have spent trust you cannot easily buy back. Only ask questions you are genuinely willing to act on.

## How to know it is working

Track two numbers. First, the rebooking rate of panel members against your other regulars — clearly higher after three months means the system works. Second, new-service take-up: what share of insiders book a new service within two weeks of its preview? A strong panel converts at a rate your general client list will never match.

Watch the softer signs too: insiders bring friends to try what they helped choose, message you with ideas unprompted, and book their next visit before the current one ends.

Your most loyal clients already chose you once. Give them a reason to keep choosing you — not a cheaper price, but a place in deciding what your salon becomes next. The clients who help build the menu stay to enjoy it.
