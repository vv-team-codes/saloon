Ask a salon owner in Dubai what clients are booking and you will hear the familiar answers: balayage, BIAB nails, a glow facial before a big night out. Ask again, and a newer answer is starting to appear. It is the back facial, known in the trade as the bacial, and it is quietly moving from spa secret to salon staple.

The idea is simple. The back is the one patch of skin almost nobody can see properly and almost everybody worries about. It breaks out, it dries out, and a backless dress puts it on display. In a city that runs on weddings, beach clubs and December party nights, that worry turns into bookings.

## What exactly is a back facial?

A bacial follows the same logic as a facial, adapted for a much larger area. A good one takes 45 to 60 minutes: a deep cleanse, exfoliation, extractions where they are needed, a mask chosen for the skin's condition, and a finishing moisturiser, usually with a short massage worked in.

Back skin is thicker than facial skin, so a gentle enzyme exfoliant or a light peel often does more than a scrub. Extractions need a trained hand and a steady pace. The mask is chosen like a facial mask: clay for oily, congested skin, something calming for sensitivity, something hydrating for skin dried out by sun and air conditioning.

## Why the UAE is the perfect market for it

Few places suit this treatment better. Heat and humidity mean sweat sits on the back for hours. Sunscreen, which everyone here uses heavily, clogs pores when it is not washed off properly. Pool chlorine and sea salt dry the skin out, and the blast of air conditioning on the drive home finishes the job.

Then there is the calendar. Wedding season puts brides and their families in backless and low-backed outfits. December brings party nights and photographs from every angle.

There is also a practical point in its favour. The back is the one area clients genuinely cannot treat themselves. They can put a face mask on at home. They cannot exfoliate between their own shoulder blades. That makes the treatment feel necessary rather than indulgent, which is a much easier sell.

## What goes into a good bacial

The consultation comes first, and it should be a real one, not a form filled in while the client is already lying face down. Ask about breakouts, sensitivity, allergies and sun exposure before choosing products. A client with active, inflamed breakouts needs a different approach from one whose main complaint is dryness.

Training matters more here than with most new services. Extractions on the back are unforgiving, and a therapist who has only ever done facials needs practice on the larger area before taking paying clients. Start with one trained therapist and one treatment bed.

Aftercare is part of the treatment, not an afterthought. Tell the client to skip the gym and the sauna for the rest of the day, avoid heavy sunscreen on the treated area until the next morning, and wear loose clothing home.

## Pricing it so it pays

Price the bacial as a treatment, not as an add-on. In Dubai, a standalone 60-minute bacial typically sits in the same range as a good facial. Pricing it like a scrub signals that it is a scrub, and clients will treat it that way.

A package of three sessions, spaced three to four weeks apart, works better than a single visit for the client and for the salon. Skin does not clear in one session, and a client who books three is a client who comes back twice. Offer the package at the end of the first visit, when the result is fresh.

It also pairs well with services salons already sell. A spray tan sits better on freshly exfoliated skin, so a bacial the day before a tan is an easy suggestion.

## Adding it to the menu without breaking the diary

A bacial needs a bed, an hour and a therapist, the same footprint as a facial. The simplest way to launch it is to carve out two or three slots a week in quiet hours, the late mornings and early afternoons when the diary has gaps, and let demand decide whether it earns evening slots. Time the treatment properly during the trial period, add a ten-minute buffer, and only then publish the slot length.

Do not launch it by printing a new menu. Launch it by offering it to the clients who will love it most: the bride with a fitting next week, the regular who complains about breakouts on her back, the client who books a facial before every event. A service that starts with ten happy clients and their word of mouth beats a service that starts with a poster.

## Marketing it without making it awkward

The one real obstacle is embarrassment. Clients can feel awkward about showing their back, and about asking for a treatment for it. The marketing has to normalise the service before the client ever walks in.

Show the treatment, not the problem. A short video of the mask going on, the room, the calm of it, tells a client what to expect far better than a list of skin concerns.

The enquiry will usually arrive on WhatsApp, often late at night, and often phrased carefully: "do you do something for the back?" A fast, warm reply that names the treatment, the price and the time it takes turns a hesitant question into a booking. ## The honest limits

A bacial is a skin treatment, not a medical one, and the salons that do well with it are the ones that say so. Severe or painful acne, cysts and anything that looks infected belong with a dermatologist, and a salon that refers those clients on earns more trust than one that promises to fix them.

Results take sessions, not miracles. Most clients see smoother, clearer skin after two or three visits.

The back facial is not a revolution. It is a sensible treatment for a part of the body the beauty industry ignored for years. The salons that add it now and price it like the treatment it is will be the ones clients trust with it.
