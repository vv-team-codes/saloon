Every UAE salon owner knows the feeling. You open the salon, answer overnight WhatsApp messages, sort out a late stylist and deal with a refund request — and somewhere in between you are supposed to plan how the business grows. By closing time you have worked a full day and done almost none of the work that grows a salon.

The problem is not effort — most owners work harder than anyone on their team. The problem is that the owner has become the bottleneck: every decision passes through one person, and while that person answers messages, nobody plans next month. If your salon has stopped growing, the reason is often not the market — it is that the business cannot grow past the hours its owner has in a day.

## The signs you need a manager

A manager is not a luxury for big salons — it is the hire that lets a busy salon keep growing past the size one person can supervise. Only you can answer a price question, change a booking or calm an unhappy client. Your stylists wait for you before deciding anything. You spend evenings on WhatsApp and wake up to messages you missed.

Another sign is quieter: you no longer walk the floor and talk to clients, and you have not reviewed prices, suppliers or marketing in months. When the diary is full yet revenue has flatlined, you are not short of clients — you are short of management capacity.

## What a salon manager actually does

A manager makes the day run so the owner can run the business: the diary is confirmed and gap-free, small complaints are handled before they become bad reviews, and problems reach the owner only when they need to.

A manager also runs the team — opening and closing, managing the rota, covering sick days, keeping standards consistent when the owner is away — and watches the weekly numbers: rebooking rates, average bills, chair occupancy, no-shows.

The best managers also know the local rhythm: the evening rush from six, Thursday and Friday as golden slots, Ramadan flipping the diary, and December party season planned weeks ahead.

## When you are ready to hire one

The right time is usually earlier than owners think: if your salon has four or more stylists, a busy diary, and you spend more than half your time on operations, you are ready. Wait until you are exhausted and you will hire in a panic.

Money is the usual worry. In Dubai, a salon manager typically earns between AED 6,000 and AED 12,000 a month, depending on experience and what's included. That is a real cost — but compare it with what the role buys back. If a manager frees twenty hours of your week and you use half of that on growth, the hire usually pays for itself within a few months. The salons that stay small are often the ones whose owners decided they could not afford a manager — then spent another year doing a manager's job for free.

## What to look for

Hiring a manager is not the same as hiring a stylist, and your best stylist is not automatically your best manager. Look for someone who has run a diary before, ideally in a UAE salon or spa. Ask for examples, not adjectives: how would they rescue a day when two stylists call in sick?

Language matters more here than in any other role: your manager speaks to clients all day, on WhatsApp and in person, so they need strong English and ideally Arabic or another language your clients speak, plus the judgement to know what to decide alone. The right person is calm under pressure, comfortable with numbers and genuinely interested in clients.

Start with your own network: other salon owners, suppliers and beauty schools in the UAE often know reliable people looking for a step up, and a personal recommendation beats a polished CV.

## The handover: your first thirty days

Most first manager hires fail not because the person was wrong, but because the handover was vague: the owner shows them around for a day, then keeps answering every message anyway, so the manager never gets real authority and the team keeps going to the owner.

Do it differently. Before they start, write down what only you decide and what they can decide alone — moving a booking, a small refund, a rota change — and when to escalate: a large refund, a staff dispute, anything involving a licence or payment.

Spend the first week working side by side, so they can see how you greet regulars and handle complaints. Then step back in stages: week two they run the floor while you stay in the back office, week three a full day while you are out but reachable, and by week four they run days alone, with a ten-minute daily check-in.

One detail owners forget: after-hours messages. Your manager cannot be on WhatsApp at midnight, and neither should you. ## The mistakes that sink a first manager hire

The most common mistake is hiring a clone of yourself, then competing with them. Agree on the standards that matter — how clients are greeted, how complaints are handled — and let them find their own way within them.

The second mistake is giving the title without the authority. Tell the team clearly that the manager speaks for you on day-to-day decisions, and back them up — otherwise everyone keeps coming to you and the hire is pointless.

The third mistake is never checking the numbers. Rebooking rates, average bills, no-shows, response times and complaints are all measurable — review them together weekly, and judge the hire on them.

## How to know it is working

Give the role three months and judge it honestly. By then you should be spending most of your time on growth, the diary should run without you, and your team should know who to ask when you are not there. You will feel it too: quieter evenings and mornings that stop starting with a crisis.

Hiring a manager is the moment a salon stops being a job and starts being a business — and the moment most owners say they wish they had done it a year earlier. If your diary is full and your time is gone, that moment is probably now.
