Open the diary of a busy Dubai salon and it looks like success. Every chair is full through the 7 PM rush, and WhatsApp enquiries keep arriving after closing. Yet ask the owner when she last sat down to do the supplier order, review her prices or plan the rota, and the answer is usually the same: she does it at midnight, after the last client has gone, or not at all.

That is the quiet problem with a full appointment book: a diary that only holds client appointments has no room for the business itself. Sooner or later something gives — the stock runs out, the rota falls apart, or the owner burns out. The fix is not to work longer. It is to put the owner's own hours into the diary first.

## The hours the diary quietly steals

A salon does not run on appointments alone. Behind every booked chair there is ordering, invoicing, supplier calls, price checks, staff conversations and training. None of that appears in the appointment book, so none of it gets protected time. It gets squeezed into the gaps — and in a UAE salon, the gaps are the first thing to disappear.

A regular asks for the quiet Tuesday 11 AM slot, and the owner gives it away because the chair would otherwise sit empty. A supplier rings during a colour processing wait, and the owner takes the call with foils in her hands. The VAT return gets done on a Sunday night. None of this feels like a decision at the time, but the cost shows up slowly: prices that drift, and a stylist who leaves because nobody had the conversation that would have kept her.

## Block the hours before you open the diary

The rule is simple: your hours go into the diary before the diary opens to anyone else. Treat an owner block exactly like a paid booking — once it is in the book, it is not available.

Start small. Three blocks of forty-five minutes a week is enough to change a salon owner's month. One for money — prices, invoices, the week's numbers. One for people — the rota, a training conversation, a check-in with a stylist. One for the business itself — suppliers, next month's diary, one decision that has been waiting. Forty-five minutes is short enough to defend and long enough to finish something real.

Write the blocks into the diary the same way you write a client booking — as non-client appointments with a label only the team can see, or in ink if you still run a paper diary. An empty hour invites a client. A labelled hour does not.

## What to block, and when

Place the blocks where they cost you least. A weekday late morning is usually the softest hour in a UAE salon — after the school-run clients have gone and before the lunch-hour and evening waves build. A Tuesday 11 AM block costs far less than a Saturday 6 PM one. Never block your peak.

Keep the blocks short and regular. One hour every Tuesday beats a heroic three-hour admin session once a month that never happens. The work is weekly — orders, rota, numbers — so the blocks should repeat at the same time each week. The team soon stops asking whether Tuesday 11 AM is free, because they already know it is not. If a block keeps getting eaten by the same interruption, move it to a quieter hour. Do not delete it: a block in the wrong hour is a scheduling error, while a deleted block is a decision to stop running the business.

## The conversation when a regular asks for your blocked hour

Sooner or later a regular will ask for the exact hour you have blocked. Keep it warm, short and honest: "That hour isn't available this week, but I can do Thursday at the same time, or Wednesday at 11:30 — which suits you better?" You do not need to explain what the hour is for. Two real alternatives, offered confidently, close the conversation.

If she is one of your top regulars, offer her first refusal on the next open slot in her preferred window. What you must not do is move the block because the client is standing in front of you and the admin can wait. Admin can always wait — which is exactly why it never gets done.

## Make the team guard it too

A block only survives if the whole team defends it. Your front desk needs to know the blocked hours are not bookable, full stop — not "ask the owner first." The person answering WhatsApp should never offer a blocked hour, because once it is offered, taking it back feels like a refusal. If you use online booking, set the blocked hours as unavailable so they never appear to clients.

Give one person ownership of the diary, and have them check each morning that the blocks are still there. Ten minutes is enough. Keep the blocks visible to the team for what they are: time when the owner is working on the salon, not time off.

Sometimes a block will have to move — a stylist calls in sick, a bridal party needs the morning. The rule is that it moves, it does not vanish: if Tuesday 11 AM is gone, it becomes Wednesday 11 AM, in the diary, before the day ends.

## A one-week starter plan

This week, do three things. Pick three forty-five-minute blocks and write them into next week's diary in ink, in your quietest hours. Tell the team, in one sentence, that those hours are not bookable. Tell your top regulars nothing — they will simply be offered the next best slot.

Then keep the blocks for four weeks and count what changes: the supplier order on time, the rota written before Sunday night, one price fixed, one proper lunch break. If even two of those happen, the blocks have paid for themselves.

A full diary is not the goal. A diary that leaves room for the person running the business is. Block your hours first, and the rest of the diary will fall into place around them.
