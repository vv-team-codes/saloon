It usually arrives on WhatsApp late in the evening, from someone you have never met. "Hi, can I come and have a look at your salon first, before I book?" No service mentioned. No price asked. Just a request to walk through the door, look around, and decide whether this is the right place for her hair, her skin, her nails — and her trust.

Many salons treat this as a non-enquiry. They reply with a price list, a link to Instagram, or a polite "sure, we're open 10 to 9." The client never comes. She was not asking for a brochure. She was asking a question no photo can answer: can I trust you with something personal? The salons that handle this well sell a feeling in that moment — and the feeling books the appointment.

## What she is really asking

Behind the message there are usually three quiet worries, and she will almost never say any of them out loud.

The first is cleanliness. She has read the horror stories — reused tools, stained towels, a tired-looking basin. She wants to see the station, the mirror and the bathroom with her own eyes.

The second is atmosphere. A salon can look perfect on Instagram and feel wrong in person: too loud, too crowded, too rushed, or a room where she would not feel comfortable. She wants to know whether she belongs there.

The third is competence. She has had a bad cut or a colour disaster somewhere else, and she is not ready to hand her hair to a stranger. She wants to meet a human being and judge whether this is a place that listens.

If your reply answers the words — "yes, come anytime" — but ignores those three worries, she will keep looking. Answer the worries, and the visit becomes the booking.

## The four-part reply that works

A good reply has four parts, and it fits in one WhatsApp message: a named person, a specific time, what she will see, and a gentle next step.

Start with warmth and a name. "Of course — we'd love to show you around. I'm Sara, and I'll meet you at the door." A named person turns a building into a host.

Then offer a specific time, not an open invitation. "Would tomorrow at 11 AM suit you? It's our quietest hour, so we can show you around properly." A specific time makes the visit real instead of someday. Late evenings and Saturday afternoons are the worst times — the team is mid-rush, and the client sees chaos instead of calm.

Then say what she will see, in one line. "You can see the colour stations, meet the stylist who would look after you, and check the products we use." That one line quietly answers her three worries before she arrives.

Finally, close with a soft next step, not a hard sell. "No need to decide anything on the day — if you like what you see, we can hold a slot for you." The pressure is off, and that is what makes her decide.

## Make the ten minutes count

The visit should last about ten minutes, planned like a service. The person who replied meets her at the door by name, offers water or karak, and walks her through a short route: reception, her service area, the wash basin, the products on the shelf, the price list on the wall.

Let her meet the stylist who would actually do her service, even for thirty seconds. "This is Maria — she does most of our lived-in colour." A handshake and a sentence from a real person does more than any certificate on the wall.

Show, don't claim. Worried about hygiene? Open the drawer and show the sealed tools. Worried about colour? Show the shade chart and a photo of a recent result on hair like hers. Worried about privacy? Show the quieter corner. Evidence in the hand beats reassurance in the air.

And watch the small things, because she will. A clean bathroom, a tidy station, a stylist who greets her by name, a client in the chair who looks relaxed — these are the details she came to check. The tour is not the time for a hard sell.

## Offer the held slot before she leaves

Here is where most salons lose the booking they just earned. The client smiles, says "lovely, thank you," walks out — and books somewhere else that evening, because nobody gave her a reason to book with you now.

Before she leaves, offer one specific thing: a held slot. "If you'd like, I can hold Thursday at 6 PM with Maria for a consultation and cut — no deposit, and you can change it by WhatsApp." Two dates are better than one, and a no-deposit hold removes the last bit of risk.

If she is not ready, ask one question before she goes: "Is there anything you'd like me to check for you — a price, a time, a service?" Her answer tells you what is still holding her back.

Write her name, her service interest and the held slot in your client notes before the end of the day. If she books, the stylist already knows who she is. If she does not, you have a warm reason to follow up once, two days later: "Hi, it's Sara — just checking whether Thursday at 6 still works for you."

## A small habit with a big return

Tour enquiries are rare — most salons get one or two a week — but they are the warmest enquiries a salon ever receives. This client has already chosen your area, found your number, and taken the trouble to ask. She is one good visit away from becoming a regular, and one careless reply away from disappearing forever.

The habit costs almost nothing: one named host, one quiet-hour time, one ten-minute route, one held slot. Do it well for a month and you will see the pattern — the clients who visit first are the clients who rebook and bring a sister.
