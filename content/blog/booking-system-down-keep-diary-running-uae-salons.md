It is 4pm on a Saturday, the salon is full, and the booking screen freezes. The app will not load, the diary will not open, and the next client is asking what time her colour appointment is. Nobody knows, because the diary lives inside the system that has just stopped working.

Sooner or later every UAE salon owner faces this — a bad update, a dead tablet, a platform outage. What decides whether the salon loses clients is not the technology. It is whether anyone in the building can run the diary without it.

## The first ten minutes decide everything

When the system goes down, the worst thing a team can do is stand around the frozen screen trying the same login again. The rule for the first ten minutes is simple: protect today's diary first, fix the technology second.

One person takes charge of the diary — usually the receptionist or the senior stylist on the floor. Everyone else keeps working on the clients in front of them. The diary owner works out what the rest of the day looks like: who is in the chair now, who is due next, and what still needs to happen before closing.

If the diary is gone completely, the team rebuilds it from messages and memory — the receptionist checks WhatsApp for today's confirmations, and stylists check their own messages, since most know their regulars' times by heart. Within ten minutes, the salon should have a rough list of the rest of the day on paper.

## The printed diary is the cheapest insurance a salon can buy

The salons that survive an outage calmly all share one habit: they print the diary every morning. One sheet behind the desk, with every appointment — name, service, stylist, time and phone number. It costs nothing.

The printout matters most in the evening. The 5pm to 9pm rush is when UAE salons are at their busiest, and when a failure hurts most. A printed sheet turns a crisis into a minor inconvenience: the receptionist works from the paper and ticks off arrivals with a pen.

Some owners print two copies, one for the desk and one for the back room, and keep a notebook beside the till where every phone or WhatsApp booking is written down. The format does not matter. Having something on paper matters.

## Tell clients before they have to ask

Clients notice a frozen screen, and silence makes them nervous. A short, honest message is far better than a long explanation. The receptionist tells the clients in the salon in one sentence: the booking system is down, their appointments are safe, and nothing about their visit changes.

For clients arriving later, a quick WhatsApp message does the same job before they leave home: your appointment today is still confirmed, please come at your usual time. Clients who hear this relax. Clients who arrive to a confused desk start wondering whether their booking still exists.

Do not ask every client to confirm again — it creates a flood of replies the team cannot handle while the system is down. Confirm only the appointments that are genuinely uncertain, the ones nobody can remember or find in messages. The rest runs on the printout.

## Taking new bookings by hand

While the system is down, new bookings still arrive — a walk-in asks for tomorrow, a WhatsApp message asks for next Tuesday. The salon can turn them away, or take the booking by hand and transfer it later. Taking it by hand is almost always right, but it needs discipline.

Every new booking goes into the same notebook, in the same format: name, service, stylist if requested, date, time, and phone number. The person who takes it repeats it back and sends a WhatsApp confirmation straight away, so the client has something in writing even though the system has nothing.

The one rule that prevents chaos is that only one person writes in the book. Two people taking bookings on two different scraps of paper is how double-bookings happen. The diary owner holds the book, and everyone else passes bookings to them. It feels slow for an hour. It saves the next three days.

## Rebuilding the diary the next morning

When the system comes back, the team must put every handwritten change back into it before the next day begins — arrivals ticked off, walk-ins added, new bookings entered, cancellations removed. Done the same evening or first thing the next morning, this takes twenty minutes. Left for a week, it takes hours and causes double-bookings.

The diary owner does the rebuild, working through the notebook line by line and checking WhatsApp for anything confirmed by message but never written down. Then they message anyone whose booking changed, confirming the final time, so nobody arrives at the wrong hour.

## Preventing the next outage

No salon can guarantee its booking system will never fail, but every salon can make the next failure boring instead of frightening. Three habits do most of the work.

First, keep the morning printout. Make it part of opening the salon, like switching on the lights. Whoever opens prints the day, and the diary owner checks it before the first client arrives.

Second, export the diary once a week. Most booking systems let the owner download the week's appointments or send a copy by email. Save it somewhere the team can reach without the system — a shared folder, or a phone. If the platform itself goes down, the export is the diary.

Third, keep one device that can work offline — a charged tablet, or simply a phone with the WhatsApp thread and the printed sheet. The salon does not need a second system, just one way to see the day when the main screen is dark.
