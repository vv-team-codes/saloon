Every salon has one. The client who arrives at the same time every week, settles into the same chair, and greets the team by name before anyone greets her. She has been coming for years — never asking for a discount, never threatening to try the salon next door. She simply comes back, because your salon is part of her life.

Older clients are the quiet backbone of many UAE salons: long-term residents and retired expats who have lived in Dubai for decades. They book steadily, pay on time, recommend you to friends, and stay loyal far longer than the average client. Yet most salons never plan for them. Keeping one comes down to pace, comfort and respect.

## Why older clients are worth keeping

An older regular books a weekly or fortnightly routine — a wash and blow-dry, a root touch-up, a manicure — and keeps it for years. She is not chasing the latest trend or the cheapest offer. She values reliability over novelty, so she is far less likely to drift to a competitor. When she recommends you, her word carries weight: her friends trust her judgement, and they tend to be the same kind of steady, loyal client.

## Slow the pace, not the service

The biggest mistake salons make with older clients is rushing them. The appointment itself need not be longer, but the edges of it should be. She may walk more slowly or need a moment to settle into the chair. A team that hurries her through the door makes her feel like a problem. A team that gives her five unhurried minutes at the start and end of the visit makes her feel welcome.

Build that time into the diary: if her blow-dry takes forty-five minutes, book fifty, with a small buffer after her appointment. The cost is a few minutes of chair time. The return is a client who never feels like a burden.

## Comfort is the service

For an older client, comfort is not a nice extra. It is the service. A chair that is hard to get out of, a basin that strains her neck, or a freezing room will stay in her memory far longer than the blow-dry.

Walk through your salon as she would. Is there a chair with arms she can push herself up from? Is there somewhere to sit while she waits, with water offered without her having to ask? Is the temperature comfortable — remembering she has just come in from forty degrees outside and will feel the cold far more sharply than a younger client? Small fixes matter: a cushion at the basin, a blanket in the cooler months, a magnifying mirror for small print. Speak clearly and face her when you talk, and never discuss her appointment across a noisy room where everyone can hear.

## Respect the routine

Older clients love routine, and routine is the cheapest retention tool a salon owns. The same stylist, the same chair, the same time every week. Once that rhythm is set, she will protect it herself.

Guard that rhythm. When her regular stylist is on leave, tell her in advance and introduce the cover stylist by name, in person, before the day arrives. Never spring a stranger on her at the chair. Keep her client notes up to date so any stylist can step in — her usual colour formula, the length she likes, the fact that she takes her tea without sugar.

## The conversation matters as much as the cut

For many older clients, the chat is half the reason they come. She wants to tell you about her grandchildren, her garden, her trip home. A stylist who asks "How did your daughter's wedding go?" — and means it — is doing retention work that no discount ever could.

Note the small things in her client record: the names of her children, the holiday she is planning, the knee operation she mentioned last month. Ask about them next time. She will notice, and she will tell her friends that your salon is the one where people actually listen. Read her cues too — some love a long chat, others prefer quiet, and a gentle "Shall we keep it quiet today?" is a kindness she will remember.

## Make booking effortless

Older clients book differently, and salons that insist on one channel lose them. Many still prefer to call. Some are happy on WhatsApp but want a voice note or a short call rather than a long typed exchange. A few rely on a daughter or son to book for them. All of these are fine — as long as every route leads to the same diary and the same warm answer.

## Small gestures, and what not to do

You do not need a loyalty programme to keep an older client. You need to notice her. A birthday card, flowers on the anniversary of her first visit, a complimentary hand massage while her colour processes — these cost almost nothing and are remembered for months. The gesture that lands hardest is the simplest: remembering. Her usual order, the quiet chair by the window, that she takes her coffee with milk. When a client feels known, she stops shopping around.

A few things push older clients away faster than any competitor can. Never rush her, never talk down to her, and never assume she wants the cheapest option or the trendiest style. Never upsell aggressively — it damages trust that took years to build. And never make her feel old. Above all, never let her feel invisible: the moment an older regular feels like just another appointment, she will quietly stop coming — without complaining first.

## The regular who stays for years

Older clients will not make your salon look busy on Instagram. What they will do is far more valuable: fill your quiet weekday chairs, pay reliably, recommend you to friends who become regulars themselves, and stay with you for years. In a business where most clients drift away within months, that kind of loyalty is worth protecting — so look at your diary this week, check their standing slots and notes, and ask of each of them: does she feel known here?
