Every salon owner in the UAE knows the sight. You walk past a salon on your street one morning and the shutters are down. The sign is gone, the chairs are empty, and a small notice on the door thanks the clients for their loyalty. Somewhere in your neighbourhood, dozens of regulars have just lost the place they trusted with their hair, their nails and their skin.

Nobody wishes a fellow business ill. But those clients still need a salon. If your salon is the one they turn to, how you handle the next few weeks matters more than any marketing campaign. Done well, a neighbour's closure can bring you a steady stream of loyal new regulars.

## These clients are not ordinary walk-ins

A client whose salon has closed is not browsing. She already has a salon habit, she knows what she likes, and she probably feels a small sense of loss. She is not looking for the cheapest option or the flashiest Instagram page. She is looking for somewhere that feels safe, competent and kind.

Ask what she usually has done and how she likes it, and listen properly before suggesting anything different. Resist the urge to criticise her old salon's work, even if you can see a colour that needs correcting. She chose that salon for years. A gentle "let's see what suits you" earns far more trust than a knowing comment about what the last stylist got wrong.

If she mentions her old stylist by name, take note of it. Clients follow people more than they follow brands, and a freed stylist is often the bridge that brings a whole book of clients with them.

## Be findable in the week it happens

When a salon closes, its clients search. They type "salon near me" into Google Maps, they ask in building WhatsApp groups, and they message the first two or three salons that look promising. The salon that answers first usually wins the first visit, and the first visit often decides everything.

Check that your Google Business Profile is complete and current: correct hours, a working phone number, recent photos of real work, and a handful of recent reviews. A profile that looks alive beats one that looks abandoned.

Then look at your reply speed. Most of these enquiries arrive on WhatsApp, often late in the evening after a long day at work. A reply that comes the next morning is a reply that comes too late — by then she has already booked somewhere else. ## What never to do

A neighbour's closure is a test of character, and your community is watching. Never badmouth the closed salon. The reasons a salon closes are usually private — a rent increase, a family decision, a licence issue — and speculating about them in front of clients looks small. If a client asks what happened, "I don't know the details, but I hope the team lands well" is enough.

Never station your team outside the closed salon, hand out flyers at its door, or message its clients directly. That is poaching, and in a tight-knit community — a single tower in JLT, a villa community in Arabian Ranches, a mall in Sharjah — word travels fast. The clients you win that way arrive with a bad taste already in their mouths.

Never slash your prices to grab the crowd. A discount raid fills chairs for a fortnight and teaches every new client that your real price is the discounted one. The clients worth keeping come for the service, the welcome and the consistency — not the cheapest balayage in the neighbourhood. And never copy the closed salon's menu, prices or branding to look like a replacement. Clients do not want a copy of what they lost.

## Turn one visit into a routine

The first visit is only the beginning. A client who has just lost her salon is auditioning you, and the audition lasts about three visits. The first tells her whether you are competent. The second tells her whether you remembered her. The third tells her whether she belongs.

So remember her. Write down what she had done, how she likes her coffee, which stylist she saw, and when she usually comes. When she returns, greet her by name and pick up where you left off. That single habit — a client record your whole team actually reads — is the difference between a salon that feels personal and one that feels like a queue.

Rebook her before she leaves the chair. Offer two specific times that match her usual pattern — the same Friday evening slot she always had, or the Saturday morning she prefers — rather than a vague "see you soon". A standing slot, held for her each week or fortnight, quietly turns a trial visit into a routine, and routines are what keep salons full through the quiet months.

## Check your capacity before you celebrate

One honest warning: a sudden influx of new clients can hurt as easily as it helps. If your diary is already tight on Thursday and Friday evenings, taking on twenty new regulars without a plan means longer waits, rushed services and disappointed clients — including the regulars you already had.

Look at your quiet hours first. Can some of these new clients be offered a Tuesday morning or a Sunday slot where chairs sit empty? Growth that your diary cannot absorb is not growth; it is a queue that leaks. Ten new regulars visiting every three weeks is real, steady revenue — but only if they stay, and they only stay if the service stays good while the diary grows.

A salon closing next door is nobody's idea of good news. But the clients it leaves behind are real people looking for a new home, and the salon that welcomes them warmly and well will keep many of them for years. Be findable, be kind, be honest about what you can do, and let the work speak. The growth follows on its own.
