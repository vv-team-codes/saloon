Every salon menu changes. A treatment stops selling, a supplier discontinues a product line, the only stylist trained in a technique leaves, or a service takes up a chair that earns more doing something else. Dropping it is usually the right call — but the decision is never just about the diary. It is about the clients who built that service into their routine.

For them, the service was not a line on your menu. It was the Tuesday morning facial before work, the monthly treatment that kept their hair manageable through the Dubai humidity, the one appointment they never cancelled. When it disappears without warning, they hear not a business decision but a salon that stopped caring about what they wanted.

The good news is that losing the service does not have to mean losing the client. Handled well, it is a rare chance for a salon to prove how much it values a regular — and come out with a stronger relationship than before.

## Tell them before they find out

The worst way a client learns her service is gone is by messaging to book it and getting a flat no. That reply feels like a door closing, and most clients will not argue — they will simply book somewhere else.

Tell the affected clients first, personally, and before the change appears anywhere public. A short WhatsApp message from the salon, ideally from the stylist they know, works best: the service is being retired, here is when, and here is what happens next. Do not bury the news in a broadcast or a social media post. The clients who booked that service twenty times deserve to hear it before the internet does.

Be honest about the reason in one sentence. "Our supplier stopped making the product" or "the treatment takes two hours and we can no longer staff it fairly" is enough. Clients respect a real reason. They do not respect a vague "we have refreshed our menu" that sounds like marketing.

## Give them one last booking

If the service is ending for a practical reason rather than a safety one, give its regulars a final window to book it — two or three weeks, framed as a thank-you rather than a clearance: "We wanted you to have the chance to enjoy it once more before it goes."

This turns an ending into a small occasion, and it buys you time: the final appointments are the perfect moment to talk about what comes next in person, while the client is relaxed in the chair.

## Offer a real alternative, not a shrug

"Sorry, we don't do that anymore" is where most salons lose the client. The sentence that keeps her is the one that follows: "But here is what I would do instead, and why it would suit you."

The alternative has to be specific to her. A client who loved a two-hour deep-conditioning ritual does not want a generic "we have other treatments." She wants to hear that the new express repair treatment gives her the same softness in half the time and that you have already checked it suits her hair type. A facial regular wants to know which treatment on the new menu targets the same concern — pigmentation, dryness, tired skin — and what the first session will feel like.

This is where your client notes earn their keep. If you know why she booked that service, you can recommend with confidence. If you do not know, ask her: "What did you love most about it — the result, the relaxation, or the time it took?" Her answer tells you exactly which alternative to offer.

## Make the first alternative visit feel special

The first visit after a favourite service disappears is the moment of truth. If the replacement feels like a downgrade, the client will quietly drift away. If it feels like an upgrade, she will wonder why she waited so long.

Treat that first appointment as a small event. Book it with her usual stylist if you can, give the treatment a little extra time, and finish with five unhurried minutes to talk about how it felt. A warm towel and a drink cost almost nothing.

Some salons offer the first alternative session at the old price, as a one-time gesture that says "we know this change cost you something." Do not make it a standing discount. One visit, one gesture, then the normal price resumes.

## Watch who goes quiet

Not every affected client will complain. A regular who booked every three weeks and suddenly has nothing in the diary for six weeks has already made her decision — she just has not told you.

Two weeks after the change, check which clients who used the discontinued service have not rebooked anything, and send each one a short personal message — not a promotion, not a broadcast — that names the service and asks how she is getting on: "We miss seeing you for your monthly treatment. How is your hair coping without it? There is a new option I think would suit you — would you like me to tell you about it?"

This is the message that wins people back, because it proves someone noticed she was gone. A 24/7 AI receptionist like ZYNA can help here too, by catching the late-evening WhatsApp reply when she answers at 10 PM and making sure her next booking is confirmed before the morning rush.

## What not to do

Do not pretend the service still exists to keep the booking, then switch it at the last minute. Do not offer a discount so large it tells her the replacement is not worth its price. And do not assume that because nobody complained, nobody minded. In a market as competitive as Dubai's, a client who feels overlooked does not complain — she books somewhere else.

## The menu will change again

Services come and go, and every salon in the UAE will retire something a client loved sooner or later. The salons that keep their regulars through those changes are not the ones with the longest menus. They are the ones that treat a discontinued service as a relationship moment: tell the client early, honour what she loved about it, offer something genuinely better, and notice if she goes quiet.

Do that, and the client does not remember the service that disappeared. She remembers the salon that handled it with care — and that is the kind of memory that keeps her coming back.
