It happens in almost every UAE salon, several times a week: "Hi, I want to book a haircut for my wife on Saturday." A husband booking for his mother, a sister booking before a wedding, a friend booking a surprise gift.

The person messaging is not the person sitting in the chair — and that breaks the booking script most salons use. The usual flow assumes the enquirer is the client, so you ask about hair type, allergies and previous colour. But the person on the phone often cannot answer any of it. They guess, promise to check, and never come back — or the booking goes ahead on wrong information, and the salon discovers the problem on a packed Saturday evening.

Third-party bookings are not a nuisance. In the UAE, where gifting a beauty treatment is a normal way of showing care, they are a real source of new clients. Handled well, a first-time visitor becomes your next regular. Handled badly, you lose two people at once: the client, and the person who paid.

## Why these enquiries need a different reply

Your booking has two people in it, and each one needs something different. The booker needs an easy way to pay and arrange the treat; the client needs to know what is happening, when, where, and what to expect. Most salons collect everything from the booker and nothing from the client — then wonder why the client never arrives.

There is also an information problem. A wife booking her husband's first haircut in Dubai may not know his hair type or skin sensitivity. A son booking a facial for his mother may not know about her allergies.

Then there is consent. A surprise booking is only pleasant if the client actually wants it and can make the time. A three-hour colour appointment booked as a surprise for someone who hates sitting in a salon is not a gift — it is an obligation, with your salon in the awkward middle. So slow the conversation down by one message, and do not confirm until you know who is coming, what they want, and how you will reach them.

## The five details to capture before you confirm

You need five answers, and you can ask for them in one friendly message.

First, the client's full name, as it should appear in your diary.

Second, the client's own WhatsApp number, if the booker will share it. It lets you send the confirmation, the location pin and the reminder to the person who actually needs them.

Third, the service as the client would describe it. "A haircut" could mean a trim, a restyle or a full transformation — so ask the booker to check one thing: what do they actually want done?

Fourth, anything about health or history that changes the service — allergies, sensitive skin, a recent colour, pregnancy, or medication that affects the skin. The booker may not know; ask them to check, or ask the client at the start of the visit.

Fifth, who pays and how — the booker in advance, a deposit, or the client at the till? Agree this before the appointment, in writing.

If the booker cannot provide the client's number or the service details, hold the slot as a pencil booking for a few hours with a clear deadline: "We will hold Saturday 5 PM until 8 PM tonight." A held slot with a deadline beats a confirmed booking built on guesses.

## Send the confirmation to the right person

Here is where most salons lose the booking: they send the confirmation to the booker, who forgets to forward it, and the client arrives late, goes to the wrong branch, or does not come at all.

Send the confirmation to the client whenever you can: their name, the service, the date and time, the salon name and location pin, the stylist's name, the duration, and one line about what to expect. Send the booker a separate message confirming the amount. And always send the reminder to the client, not just the booker.

If it is a genuine surprise, agree the plan with the booker: who tells the client, and when. A surprise revealed in the morning works. A surprise revealed in the car park does not.

Gift bookings deserve extra care, because the client arrives with no expectations. Treat the first five minutes as a welcome, not a transaction: explain the service in plain words and offer a real choice — "Your daughter booked the signature facial — would you like that, or something gentler today?" A gifted client who is given a choice relaxes immediately. And this is your best chance to win a new regular: the booker chose your salon, and the client is judging it for the first time.

## When to say no, kindly

Not every third-party booking should be accepted: a booking for a child under sixteen without a parent present, a service the client has not agreed to, or a treatment with health implications the booker cannot answer questions about. These are moments to pause.

The kind reply is honest and specific: "We would love to look after her — we just need her to confirm the service and the time herself. Could you ask her to message us, or shall we call her?" The same applies to payment: settle who pays before the appointment, not at checkout.

## A simple habit your team can use

You do not need a script — just a habit. When a third-party enquiry arrives, ask five things, in your own words: the client's name, the client's number, the service as the client wants it, any health notes, and who pays.

One message can carry all five questions, one follow-up can confirm the slot, and one confirmation goes to the client and one to the booker. That is the whole system — and it fits on a single page by the front desk.

Salons that get this right notice something quickly: third-party bookings stop being the appointments that go wrong, and start bringing in new clients who never would have found you on their own.
