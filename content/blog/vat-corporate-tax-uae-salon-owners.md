Most salon owners in the UAE can talk for an hour about balayage, bridal trials and the Saturday rush. Ask them about tax, and the conversation stops. You opened a salon because you love beauty, not paperwork — but tax is now part of running a business here, and ignoring it can quietly turn expensive.

The good news is that the UAE system is simple compared with most countries. There are two taxes to understand: VAT and corporate tax. Neither is frightening once you know the thresholds, the deadlines and the habits that keep you safe. This is a plain-English guide, not tax advice, but it will help you ask your accountant the right questions.

## VAT: the 5% that runs through every appointment

Value Added Tax has applied in the UAE since 2018, at a flat rate of 5%. As a salon, you charge VAT on most of your services and products, and you claim back the VAT you pay on your own business purchases. The difference goes to the Federal Tax Authority.

Registration is mandatory once your taxable supplies pass AED 375,000 in the previous twelve months, or you expect them to pass that figure in the next thirty days. You can also register voluntarily once you pass AED 187,500, which lets smaller salons reclaim VAT on purchases. If your turnover is below the voluntary threshold and you are not registered, you simply do not charge VAT — but watch the number, because a busy salon can cross it faster than expected.

Once registered, you file a VAT return, usually every quarter, and pay any VAT due by the 28th day after the tax period ends. The return is not complicated, but it depends entirely on your records. If every service, retail sale and refund is logged, the return takes an hour. If your records live in three notebooks and a WhatsApp chat, it takes a week of stress.

## Corporate tax: the 9% that arrived in 2023

Corporate tax is newer, and it still catches salon owners by surprise. Since June 2023, businesses in the UAE pay 9% corporate tax on taxable income above AED 375,000 per year. Income up to AED 375,000 is taxed at 0%. Many small salons owe nothing at all — but they still need to register for corporate tax and file a return, even when the bill is zero.

There is also small business relief: if your revenue is AED 3 million or less in a tax period, you can elect to be treated as having no taxable income at all, up to the end of the 2026 tax year. But it is an election, not an exemption from filing, and it does not remove the need for clean books. The Federal Tax Authority still expects a return, filed within nine months of the end of your financial year.

The number that matters here is profit, not revenue. Your taxable income is your revenue minus your allowable business expenses — rent, salaries, products, utilities, marketing. Every dirham of legitimate expense you can prove is a dirham that does not get taxed.

## The record-keeping habit that saves you twice

Good records are the one habit that serves both taxes and your business. It takes less than an hour a week.

First, separate your money. One business bank account, one business card, and no personal spending through the salon account. The moment personal and business spending mix, every calculation gets harder.

Second, keep every receipt. Supplier invoices, DEWA bills, rent contracts, product orders, marketing spend — photograph them or file them digitally the day they arrive. A receipt you cannot find is an expense you cannot claim.

Third, reconcile weekly. Match your booking diary against your takings, your card machine against your bank statement, and your retail sales against your stock. That catches mistakes while they are still small.

Fourth, keep a simple monthly summary: revenue by service, retail revenue, expenses by category, and profit. It is also the sheet your accountant will ask for first.

## What salons get wrong

The most common mistake is treating VAT as the salon's money. It is not. The 5% you collect belongs to the government, and it needs to sit somewhere safe until you file. Open a separate savings account for VAT and move the collected amount there every week.

The second mistake is forgetting that free services still have tax implications. A complimentary blow-dry or a free trial treatment is generous — but if your salon is VAT-registered, free services can still count as supplies. Ask your accountant how to handle them before you build a habit around them.

The third mistake is assuming a free zone licence means no tax. Free zone salons can qualify for a 0% corporate tax rate if they meet specific conditions — and getting them wrong is costly. If your salon sits in a free zone, have that conversation with your accountant before you assume anything.

The fourth mistake is leaving it all until the deadline. Late filing brings penalties — from AED 500 for VAT up to AED 10,000 for corporate tax. Put the deadlines in your calendar the day you register, and treat them like a client's appointment: non-negotiable.

You do not need an accountant on a monthly retainer to run a small salon, but you do need one at the right moments: when you register for VAT or corporate tax, when your revenue crosses a threshold, when you open a second branch, and when you file your first return.
