Every salon owner in the UAE knows the scene. A regular walks in for her colour appointment, and beside her is a friend, a sister, a mother, sometimes a husband. That person is not booked in. They have come along for the company, or because they had nothing else to do, or because the client was nervous about trying a new salon. They sit down, take out their phone, and wait. Two or three hours pass.

Most salons treat that person as furniture. A chair in the corner, a polite nod, a glass of water if someone remembers. That is a missed opportunity, because the waiting companion is the warmest prospect your salon will ever meet. She is already inside your salon, watching your team work, hearing your clients laugh, seeing the results walk past her in the mirror. She is forming an opinion about your business without a single ad ever reaching her.

The salons that win her over do not run a campaign for it. They simply treat the companion as a guest, not a bystander. That is a small shift in attitude with a very real effect on bookings, especially in Dubai and Abu Dhabi, where so much business still comes through personal recommendation.

## Start by greeting the companion, not just the client

The first thirty seconds set the tone. When a client arrives with someone, greet both people by name if you know it, and by eye contact and a smile if you do not. A simple "Welcome, make yourself comfortable" costs nothing, yet it changes the whole visit. The companion stops feeling like an intruder and starts feeling like a guest.

Train your front desk to ask one friendly question: "Are you joining us for a treatment today, or are you keeping her company?" Never push a service in the first minute — the offer comes later, when she has relaxed and seen the salon at its best.

## Give the waiting seat a purpose

Look at your waiting area through a companion's eyes. Is the chair comfortable for two hours, or is it a hard bench by the door? Is there a table for a coffee, a socket for a phone charger, decent light to read by? In a mall salon, where space is tight, even a small stool near the styling chairs beats a chair facing a blank wall. In a villa or community salon, a proper sofa with a side table makes the wait feel like a visit.

The best waiting seats are close enough to the action that the companion can see the salon working — the colour being painted, the blow-dry taking shape, the smiles at the mirror — but far enough away that she does not feel in the way.

## The offer that works is small and easy

About halfway through the visit, when the companion has settled in, is the moment for a small, easy offer. Not a full service. Not a sales pitch. Something she can say yes to without thinking: a complimentary brow tidy, a quick hand massage while she waits, a ten-minute blow-dry refresh, a polish change. These take almost no diary time if you plan for them, and they give the companion a reason to sit in your chair instead of on your sofa.

The trick is to frame it as hospitality, not selling. "While you are waiting, would you like a quick brow tidy on us?" works. "Can I interest you in our brow service today?" does not. The first sounds like generosity. The second sounds like a till. If she says no, smile and mean it — the offer itself has already told her that this salon is generous, and she will remember that when she needs a salon of her own.

Some salons keep one quiet slot in the diary for exactly this: a short "companion service" that a junior stylist can run between appointments. It costs the salon very little and fills a gap that would otherwise be dead time. During peak evening hours, when the diary is full, the offer simply becomes a voucher for another day — "Next time, your first blow-dry is on us" — which is just as effective, because it gives her a reason to come back.

## Let the companion see the result, not just the wait

The most powerful moment of the whole visit is the mirror reveal at the end. Most salons focus that moment entirely on the client. Bring the companion into it. Ask her what she thinks of the colour. Show her the back of the hair in the hand mirror. Let her be part of the reaction.

When a companion has watched a transformation from start to finish and then been asked her opinion, she has lived the experience almost as fully as the client did. She knows what the service costs, how long it takes, and how her friend feels walking out. That is more convincing than any review she could read online, and many companions book their own appointment at the front desk on the way out.

## A simple system, not a lucky accident

None of this needs to be left to chance. Write it down as a short front-desk standard, the same way you would write down how to answer the phone. Four lines are enough: greet both people, offer the comfortable seat and a drink, make one small offer halfway through, invite the companion into the mirror moment. Add one more line for the booking: if the companion shows interest, offer her a specific slot — "Thursday at six, or Saturday morning?" — rather than a vague "come back anytime."
