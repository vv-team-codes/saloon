A client settles into your chair, slips off her gold bangles, folds her abaya over the back of the seat and tucks her handbag underneath. She is about to spend two hours with her eyes closed at the basin. In that moment she has handed you something more valuable than her booking: her trust.

Most salons never think about belongings until something goes wrong — a bracelet left on a side table, a phone behind a cushion, a handbag knocked over by a trolley. In the UAE, where clients arrive wearing real gold and carrying designer bags, a clear belongings system is not a nice extra. It is part of the service.

## What clients actually leave behind

Walk through your salon on a busy evening and count what is sitting unattended. Handbags on the floor beside chairs. Phones charging at a shared socket. Abayas draped over chair backs. Jewellery removed for a facial or a hair wash and placed on a trolley shelf. Sunglasses, car keys, a laptop in a tote bag.

Every item has a story attached to it. The gold bangles may be a wedding gift. The handbag may hold a passport and a wallet. The phone holds the client's whole life. A client who worries about her bag is not enjoying her appointment — and a client who loses something in your salon rarely comes back.

## The thirty seconds that prevent the problem

The best belongings system costs almost nothing and takes about thirty seconds per client. It starts at the front desk, not at the chair. When a client arrives, the welcome should include one simple offer: a place for her things.

A small locker, a labelled basket, a hook inside a curtained corner — the container matters less than the ritual. The client should see where her belongings go, and she should be the one who puts them there. A basket handed to her at the chair works beautifully: she places her bag, phone and jewellery in it herself, and it stays within her sight.

For longer services, a numbered locker near the entrance is even better. Hand the client the key or the number, and the whole question disappears. Salons in Dubai's malls already do this well, with lockers built into the entrance wall. High-street salons sometimes skip this step, assuming a chair-side hook is enough. It is not. A hook is where things fall from.

## Jewellery deserves its own rule

Jewellery is the item most likely to cause a problem, because it is small, valuable and easily misplaced — and because clients remove it at the worst possible moment, mid-service, when their hands are wet or covered in product.

Make it a team rule: whenever a client removes jewellery, it goes into one place only — a small dish on the trolley, a labelled pouch, the client's own basket. Pick one and make it the rule for every stylist, every time. Never let a ring sit loose on a side table.

Mention it during the consultation too. A simple line — "Would you like a pouch for your rings while we do the colour?" — sounds like care, because it is care.

## Abayas and coats need distance from the chair

Many clients arrive in an abaya or a coat and need somewhere to put it. A chair-back hook feels convenient, but it means the garment spends the whole visit where colour, water and product can reach it. One splash of bleach on a favourite abaya is a disaster no discount can fix.

A proper coat rail near the entrance, with hangers and a little space between garments, solves the problem completely. If space is tight, a curtained rail or a row of hooks in a dry corner works just as well. The key is distance from the service area. Clients who wear an abaya will notice this immediately — and they will remember a salon that protects their clothes as carefully as it styles their hair.

## When something does go missing

Even with a good system, something will eventually go missing. A client will leave a scarf behind. A ring will turn up in the laundry. What happens next matters more than the system itself.

Stay calm and take it seriously. Never suggest the client misplaced it herself, even if that seems likely. Search visibly and thoroughly, in front of her if possible, and involve a second team member. If the item is not found, take her details, promise a specific follow-up time, and actually follow up — a WhatsApp message the same evening saying you have checked every chair costs nothing and means everything.

Keep a simple lost-property log at the front desk: date, item, where it was found, who collected it. Items found after a client leaves should be photographed, bagged, labelled and kept for at least a month. A lost item handled well can build more loyalty than a perfect service, because it proves the salon cares when there is nothing in it for them.

## Make it part of the welcome, not an afterthought

None of this needs a big investment. Lockers or baskets, a coat rail, a jewellery dish and a lost-property log — a salon can set all of it up in a single afternoon for the price of one quiet weekday. The hard part is not the equipment. It is making the habit stick.

Put belongings into your welcome script. The receptionist offers storage on arrival. The stylist offers the jewellery pouch before the service starts. The team checks the chair and basin area after every client, the same way they sweep the floor. Add it to the closing checklist: chairs clear, baskets empty, lockers checked, lost property logged. Within a fortnight it becomes automatic.

That is the real payoff. Clients rarely praise a salon for keeping their bag safe — it feels like something that should simply happen. But they absolutely notice when it does not happen, and they tell people. A salon where a client can close her eyes at the basin without a second thought is a salon she returns to and recommends.

The details clients remember are rarely the ones on the price list. They remember how the salon made them feel: looked after, respected, at ease. A simple system for their belongings delivers all three, every single visit.
