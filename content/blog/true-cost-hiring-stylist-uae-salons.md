Every salon owner in the UAE has done the same sums. The diary is full, the team is stretched, and a new stylist looks like the obvious answer. You check the salary you can offer, it fits, and you start interviewing. Then the first month's bills arrive — and the hire costs far more than the salary you budgeted for.

In the UAE, a stylist's salary is the headline, not the total. Visas, medical checks, housing, insurance, training and the slow first months all sit on top of it — and most owners only discover the real figure after the contract is signed.

## The visa and paperwork bill comes first

For most salons, the first real cost of a hire has nothing to do with hair. A new stylist working in the UAE needs a work permit and residence visa, a medical fitness test, an Emirates ID and health insurance. Together, those steps typically run into several thousand dirhams before the stylist has touched a single client — and the figure is higher if you are hiring from overseas, where flights and document attestation add to the bill.

These are not one-off costs, either. Visas renew, insurance renews, and every renewal lands in the same month as a dozen other bills. Smart owners spread the cost: divide the visa and setup bill across the length of the contract, and add that monthly figure to the salary when you judge whether the hire pays.

## Housing and transport are the costs owners forget

In Dubai and Abu Dhabi especially, where a stylist works and where she can afford to live are often two different places. Many salons provide shared accommodation or a housing allowance, and many more provide transport or a transport allowance.

Be honest about this line in your budget. A housing allowance of 1,000 dirhams a month and a transport allowance of a few hundred more are not perks; in this market they are part of the real wage. The salons that keep people longest are usually the ones that treated housing and transport as part of the offer, not as a favour added later.

## The first three months cost more than they earn

Even a brilliant stylist does not arrive fully productive. The first weeks are shadowing, learning your colour formulas, meeting regulars and building a diary from nothing. In a busy UAE salon, a new stylist typically needs six to eight weeks before her diary is reliably full — and in the quiet summer months, longer.

During that ramp-up you are paying full costs for partial revenue. There is also the product cost nobody budgets for: the colour and treatments used on models and trial clients, and the senior stylist hours spent training instead of earning. None of this is wasted money — it is the investment that turns a new hire into a regular's favourite — but it belongs in the budget, not in the surprise column. If the hire only makes sense when she is fully booked from week one, the hire does not make sense yet. The same goes for the paperwork around the hire: a short, honest probation — with clear targets and a review date written down — is the cheapest insurance a salon owner has. It costs one conversation at the start and saves the cost of carrying the wrong person for a year.

## The most expensive hire is the one who leaves

Here is the number owners rarely calculate: what it costs when a stylist leaves after six months. You pay the setup costs again for the replacement, lose the clients who booked with her specifically, and lose the training you invested — while your remaining team absorbs the extra hours until the replacement is ready.

This is why the cheapest hire is rarely the cheapest stylist. A slightly higher salary that keeps a good stylist for two years is almost always cheaper than a lower salary that loses her in six months. When you compare candidates, compare the full two-year cost of each one, not the monthly salary.

## The simple maths before you hire

Work out the fully loaded monthly cost first: salary, plus visa and setup costs spread over the contract, plus housing or transport, plus insurance, plus a fair share of commission. For many UAE salons that figure lands 30 to 50 percent above the salary alone.

Then work out what the chair has to earn. Take the average bill for the services this stylist will do, and ask how many clients a week she needs to cover her fully loaded cost. If the answer is twenty clients a week and your diary can realistically give her twelve in month one, you have a plan to write, not a problem to ignore. Can you move quiet-hour clients to her, or offer a founding-client rate on her quiet slots? The salons that hire well answer these questions before the first interview, not after the first quiet month.

One more check matters: the timing. Hiring in October, ahead of the December party season and the Ramadan rush, gives a new stylist a full diary to grow into. Hiring in June, at the start of the summer slowdown, asks her to build a book in the hardest weeks of the year. The same hire can be a triumph or a strain depending on the month you choose.

## Hire slower, onboard properly

None of this means you should not hire. A salon that never hires never grows. It means hiring like a business decision, not a diary emergency. Interview properly, run a paid trial shift, agree the probation and the targets in writing, and give the new stylist a real onboarding plan — a named mentor, a staged diary, and an introduction to your regulars.
