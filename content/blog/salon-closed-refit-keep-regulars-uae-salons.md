Every salon owner in the UAE faces it eventually. The flooring needs replacing, the chairs are tired, the landlord has scheduled maintenance, or the whole team simply needs a break after a long season. The shutters come down for a week, sometimes two, and the diary goes quiet. The real worry is not the lost week of income — it is the client who needed a cut while you were closed, tried the salon two doors down, and never came back. A temporary closure only becomes a retention problem when clients feel forgotten.

## Tell them before they hear it from someone else

The single biggest mistake is letting clients discover the closure by arriving at a locked door. A regular who has driven to your mall branch or high-street shop, found the shutters down and received no warning, does not think "they must be renovating." She thinks she has been taken for granted, and she books elsewhere that same afternoon.

Announce the closure three to four weeks in advance, starting with the chair rather than a broadcast message. The stylist tells her own clients while they sit in front of the mirror: the dates, the reopening date, and one sentence about what will be better when they return.

Follow the conversation with a short WhatsApp message that repeats the dates in writing, so she can check them later. Keep it warm and specific: "We close on Sunday the 23rd for our refit and reopen on Monday the 1st. Your chair will be waiting." Vague announcements — "closed for a short while" — create exactly the uncertainty that sends people elsewhere.

## Book their return before you close

This is the most effective retention move of the whole closure, and it costs nothing. Before the last working day, pre-book your regulars into the reopening week. "Same chair, Tuesday after we reopen, 7 pm?" takes ten seconds, and it changes everything. The client leaves with a date in her calendar, not a vague intention to come back sometime. An intention fades in a week; a booking does not.

Give regulars first pick of the reopening diary. It rewards loyalty, and it solves a practical problem too: an empty first week back is a slow, anxious restart, while a full one feels like a celebration. If a regular's usual cycle falls during the closure, offer the slot just before closing or the first slot after reopening, and let her choose.

## Keep the conversation warm while the doors are shut

Silence during a closure is what turns a week's absence into a lost client. You do not need daily messages — one or two well-timed updates are enough. Midway through the refit, send a short WhatsApp update with a real photo: the new chairs arriving, the fresh paint going up. You are not selling anything. You are showing a client that her salon is being cared for, and that something better is waiting for her.

Answer enquiries through the whole closure, quickly. Someone will message at 9 pm asking "are you open tomorrow?" and the speed of the reply matters as much as the answer. A reply within minutes — the reopening date, plus a booking link for the first week back — keeps that person yours. ## Give the urgent ones somewhere to go

Some clients genuinely cannot wait. A bride with a trial booked, a client with a big event, a regular whose roots will not survive another ten days. Pretending they will simply wait is how you lose them quietly.

Be honest instead. If your salon offers home visits, offer one for the truly urgent cases. If a trusted colleague salon nearby can look after them for one visit, say so plainly: "Go to them this once, and your chair here is waiting when we reopen." A client who was helped through an awkward week remembers the honesty.

## Make reopening feel like an event, not a restart

The first week back sets the tone for the next six months. Treat it as a reopening, not a return to normal. Invite regulars personally — a message from their stylist, not a mass broadcast: "We are back on Monday, and your usual chair is ready." Have their usual drink ready, show them the new space with pride, and ask what they think of it. People like being part of a salon's new chapter; they do not like being processed through it.

Resist the urge to discount the reopening away. A blanket offer brings in bargain hunters and teaches regulars that your prices are negotiable. A small, personal gesture — a complimentary blow-dry with their first colour back, a hand-written thank-you note — costs less and says more. Regulars do not come back for a discount. They come back because they were missed.

## When the closure runs longer than planned

Refits slip. A delivery is late, the contractor finds a problem behind a wall, a permit takes an extra week. The owners who lose clients in this moment are not the ones whose refit ran long — they are the ones who went quiet about it.

If the reopening date moves, tell pre-booked clients the same day you know, and give them the new date plus first choice of the revised diary. An honest delay, communicated early, costs very little trust. Silence costs all of it, because a client who arrives on the promised reopening day to find the salon still closed will assume she was never a priority.

Use the quiet weeks for the housekeeping that never gets done when the diary is full: confirm standing appointments, update client notes, and look through the list of regulars you have not seen in a while. A short personal message to a lapsed regular during the closure — "we are refreshing the salon and would love to see you when we reopen" — can win back a client who had quietly drifted away.

A closed salon does not have to mean lost clients. Tell them early, book their return before you close, stay in touch while the work happens, and welcome them back like they were missed — because they were. The salons that keep their regulars through a refit are not the ones with the biggest renovation budget. They are the ones whose clients never once felt forgotten.
