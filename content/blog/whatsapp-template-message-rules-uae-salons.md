Most UAE salon owners think of WhatsApp as a free-for-all: type a message, press send, the client reads it. For a one-to-one chat inside an open conversation, that is mostly true. But the moment you message a client who has not written to you in a while, or you send the same offer to dozens of people at once, different rules apply — and breaking them can quietly limit your number or get it flagged.

WhatsApp calls these pre-approved messages "templates". Knowing which kind you are sending, and when you may send it, is the difference between a WhatsApp channel that fills chairs and one that stops working when you need it most.

## Why WhatsApp treats some messages differently

WhatsApp protects its users from spam, which is why your clients actually open salon messages instead of ignoring them like email. The trade-off is that businesses cannot message anyone, any time, about anything.

The basic rule is simple. When a client messages you first, you have a window of about 24 hours to reply freely. After that window closes, you can only reach them with a template message — one WhatsApp has approved in advance — and only if the client has agreed to hear from you. Marketing messages always need a template, and always need the client's permission first.

This matters for salons more than for most businesses because salon relationships are long and chatty. A regular who books every three weeks often goes quiet for longer than a day between messages. If you want to send her a reminder, an offer, or a "we miss you" note, you are almost always sending a template.

## Utility templates: the messages that keep the diary full

Utility templates cover messages a client needs or expects because of something they already did. Appointment confirmations, reminders, receipts, and updates about a booking are the classic examples.

For a salon, this is the workhorse category. A reminder the day before a colour appointment, a message confirming a rescheduled slot, or a note that a stylist is running ten minutes late are all utility messages. Clients welcome them because they relate to a booking they made, and they are the least likely to be reported or blocked.

The practical rule is to keep utility messages factual and tied to a real booking. "Your appointment with Sara is tomorrow at 6 pm at our JLT branch. Reply YES to confirm" is a utility message. Adding "and enjoy 20% off hair spa this week" to the same message turns it into marketing — and that is where salons get caught out.

## Marketing templates: offers need permission first

Marketing templates cover promotions, offers, new services, festive greetings with a sales angle, and any message designed to bring a client back. They fill quiet weekday chairs, but they come with two conditions: the template must be approved, and the client must have opted in.

Opt-in is usually simple in a salon. A client who ticks a box on your booking form, replies "yes" to a message asking if she would like offers on WhatsApp, or signs up through your booking link has opted in. A client who only ever booked once and never agreed to marketing has not. Sending offers to the second group is the fastest way to collect "report spam" taps, and enough of those can restrict your number.

Keep a simple record of who opted in and when. A column in your client sheet or a label in WhatsApp Business is enough. When a client asks to stop receiving offers, remove them the same day and confirm it. A graceful opt-out protects your number far more than one more promotional message ever earns.

## The wording traps that get templates rejected

Template approval is not automatic, and salons trip over the same few traps.

The first trap is the floating offer. A template that says "Big discounts this weekend!" with no service, no price range, and no expiry reads like spam. A template that says "Our winter glow facial is AED 249 this week, valid until Saturday at our Dubai Marina branch" is specific, honest, and far more likely to be approved.

The second trap is the misleading greeting. Templates that open with "Your appointment is confirmed" when there is no appointment are not clever; they are the kind of message that gets reported. Clients spot this quickly — and block.

The third trap is sending the same marketing template too often. Even a client who opted in will block a salon that sends three offers a week. One thoughtful message a week, tied to something real, performs better than a daily blast and keeps your number healthy.

## What happens when your number gets flagged

A flagged number does not always stop working overnight. More often, the signs creep in: messages take longer to deliver, fewer clients see your Status updates, template approvals start getting rejected, and eventually WhatsApp limits how many people you can message at once. For a salon that runs its diary on WhatsApp, that slow squeeze is worse than a sudden ban.

Recovery is slow and mostly out of your hands. The practical answer is prevention. Send marketing only to people who opted in. Keep utility messages free of sales language. Answer enquiries fast, because a quick, helpful reply stops clients reporting your number out of frustration. And never buy a list of numbers or message people who have never heard of your salon.

## Putting it together: a simple weekly rhythm

One useful rhythm looks like this. Each morning, utility messages go out on their own: confirmations for tomorrow, reminders for today, and any changes to the diary. Marketing goes out once a week, on a day when you genuinely have chairs to fill, to opted-in clients only, with one specific offer and a clear end date. Everything else — questions, price enquiries, reschedules — is a normal reply inside an open conversation.

Treat templates as a tool with rules, not as a loophole. The salons that get the most from WhatsApp in the UAE are not the ones sending the most messages. They are the ones whose messages clients are glad to receive, because every message is either useful, expected, or something the client asked for.
