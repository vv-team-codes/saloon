It usually arrives in the early afternoon, when the diary is already full. A short WhatsApp message: "Hi, I have a flight tonight at 9. Can I get a blow-dry at 5?" A bride's sister in town for a wedding, a business traveller with a 1 a.m. departure, a mum flying home for the holidays who wants her colour done before she sees her family.

In a city built around an airport, pre-flight beauty enquiries are a regular part of salon life. The client is not asking for a favour — she is asking for a service with a hard deadline she cannot move. Handled well, these are some of the most profitable bookings a salon takes: the client is motivated, she will pay for speed, and she remembers the salon that saved her departure day.

## Why pre-flight enquiries are worth taking seriously

A client travelling tonight is a client with a reason to book now. She has a fixed window between now and check-in, and she has already decided she wants the service. That makes her one of the warmest enquiries a salon receives.

There is a quieter value too. Dubai residents travel constantly — school holidays, Eid breaks, summer escapes, work trips. A client who flies four or five times a year needs a pre-flight service four or five times a year. Win her once and her flight schedule does the reminding for you.

## The four questions to ask before you say yes

Before promising anything, the reply needs four facts. Ask for all of them in the first message, because every round of back-and-forth eats into a window that is already tight.

First, what time is the flight, and from which airport? A 9 p.m. flight from Dubai International is a different promise from a 9 p.m. flight from Al Maktoum, where the drive alone can take an hour from most neighbourhoods. Second, what service does she actually want, and is it realistic in the time available? A blow-dry is a yes; a full colour and cut in ninety minutes is not. Third, where is she now, and how is she getting to the airport? Fourth, does she need to go home first, or is she coming straight from the salon with her bags?

## Timing the service to the flight, not the clock

The rule that protects everyone is simple: work backwards from the flight, not forwards from the appointment. A client with a 9 p.m. international departure should be at the airport by 6:30 p.m. at the latest, and by 6 p.m. if she is checking bags. If the drive from your salon takes forty-five minutes, she must leave the chair by 5:15 p.m. — and that means the service must be finished, paid for and packed up by then, not still drying.

Build the buffer into the booking itself. If a blow-dry normally takes forty-five minutes, block an hour for a pre-flight client, and tell her the finish time in the confirmation: "Your blow-dry finishes at 4:45 p.m., which leaves you plenty of time for a 9 p.m. flight."

Be honest about what does not fit. If the only slot leaves her twenty minutes to reach the airport, say so and offer the alternative: a shorter express service, an earlier slot, or a different day.

## What to offer, and what to refuse

Pre-flight clients are ideal candidates for services that travel well. A humidity-resistant blow-dry, a sleek bun or braid for a long-haul flight, a gel manicure, a brow tidy, a lash lift — these still look good when she lands eight hours later. Frame the offer around the journey: "This finish holds up in cabin air and humidity, so you will still look fresh when you land."

Some services are a bad idea before a flight and should be refused kindly. A fresh colour that has not properly settled, a chemical treatment that needs forty-eight hours before washing, a facial that leaves skin flushed an hour before passport control — these create problems at 35,000 feet. The professional answer is not a flat no. It is a no with a better plan: "Colour today will not have time to settle before your flight. Let's do a gloss and a blow-dry today, and book your full colour for the day you land — I will hold the slot now."

That kind of honesty turns a one-off traveller into a regular.

## The reply that books them

Speed matters more here than in almost any other enquiry. A client with a flight tonight is messaging three salons at once, and she will book with the first one that gives her a clear answer. A reply that arrives in five minutes with a specific time, a finish time and a deposit link wins. A reply that arrives in two hours with "What time suits you?" loses.

This is where a salon's after-hours setup earns its keep. Pre-flight messages often arrive at 10 p.m. for a 6 a.m. departure, or at 6 a.m. for a midday flight. ## Turning a one-off into a return visit

The visit itself is short, but the follow-up is where the real value sits. Before she leaves the chair, ask one question: "When are you back?" Note the date. Then, the day she lands back in Dubai, send one message: "Welcome back — hope the trip went well. Shall I hold your usual slot this week?" That single message converts a one-off visit into a regular booking.

If she is a visitor rather than a resident, the follow-up is just as simple: a thank-you message the next morning and an invitation to book ahead on her next trip. Visitors who feel remembered book again on their next Dubai trip — and they tell the friends they are travelling with.

Pre-flight enquiries look like emergencies, but they are really a test of how a salon handles time. Answer fast, ask the right questions, work backwards from the flight, be honest about what fits, and follow up when she lands.
