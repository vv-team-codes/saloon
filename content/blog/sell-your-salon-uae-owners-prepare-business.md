Most salon owners in the UAE start their business thinking about the opening day, not the last one. But one day the question arrives anyway — a move back home, a new opportunity, or a good offer. And that is when many owners discover something uncomfortable: the salon they poured years into is worth far less to a buyer than it is to them.

A buyer is not buying your talent. They are buying a business that keeps earning after you leave. If everything important lives in your head — the client relationships, the supplier deals, the way the diary works — then what you are selling is a room full of chairs. The owners who get the best price spent a year or two making the business separable from themselves, long before they thought about selling.

## Why most salons sell for less than owners expect

A buyer values a salon on what it earns, how reliably, and how risky the takeover feels. They see a spreadsheet, a lease, a team and a client list — and ask what happens to each when you walk out the door.

The most common reason a sale price disappoints is that the earnings cannot be proved. Cash that never passed through the books, discounts given off the record, personal expenses mixed into the business — all of it makes the real profit look smaller than it is. A buyer who cannot trust the figures will walk away or pay for the worst case.

## Start with clean, honest books

If a sale is even a distant possibility, this is the first job. A buyer will want to see at least two years of clear financials: revenue by month, costs broken down sensibly, and a profit figure that can be checked against bank statements.

Separate business money from personal money completely, record every sale through the till or booking system, and keep supplier invoices and staff costs in order. Be honest about owner benefits too — the car, the phone, the family treatments — so a buyer can see the true cost of running the salon without you. Owners who do this often find the business looks stronger on paper than they thought.

## Make the business transferable, not personal

Walk through your salon and ask one hard question: does this survive without me? If clients book because they trust you personally, if suppliers only deal with you, if the team only takes direction from you, the business is not yet sellable at its full value.

The fix is unglamorous and slow, which is why it should start early. Client records should live in the salon's system, not in your phone — histories, formulas, preferences, allergies, the details that make a regular feel known. Supplier deals should be documented too, with account names and payment terms. Daily routines should be written down as well. All of it turns a personal practice into a business someone else can run from day one.

The team is the biggest part of this. A buyer will ask whether the stylists stay. Contracts, clear pay structures and a team that has worked with clients beyond just you make the handover feel safe. Owners who share client relationships across the team are quietly building sale value every week.

## Protect the lease and the location

In the UAE, the lease can make or break a sale. A salon with six months left on a tenancy and no renewal agreed is a gamble, however good the client base. A buyer needs to know the salon can stay put at a rent they can afford, long enough to earn back what they paid.

Check your tenancy contract now, not when a buyer asks: how long is left, what are the renewal terms, is the rent likely to jump, and can the lease be transferred? If renewal is coming up, negotiate it before you market the business — a fresh two or three year term is a strong selling point. The same goes for the trade licence: make sure it is current, the activities match what the salon actually does, and no fines are waiting to surface.

## Keep the clients in place

A buyer is really buying two things: the earning power of the chairs and the loyalty of the client list. A diary of one-off walk-ins is worth less than a diary of regulars who rebook. So the months before a sale are not the time to coast — keep regulars looked after, offer rebooking at every visit, and let nobody drift away unnoticed.

Resist one temptation: telling clients you are selling before the deal is done. A premature announcement can spook regulars and staff alike. Most owners keep the plan quiet and only introduce the new owner once the sale is agreed. The clients who matter will stay for the salon and the team — not just for you.

## Price it like a buyer, not like an owner

Owners almost always overvalue their salon at first, pricing in the years of effort. Buyers price in the profit, the risk and the work ahead. A realistic way to think about value is a multiple of the salon's genuine annual profit, adjusted for the lease length, the team, the client base and the condition of the fit-out.

Get an independent view before you name a price, from an accountant who knows the UAE beauty market or a broker who has handled salon sales. The right price brings serious buyers quickly, not one that sits advertised for a year while the diary slowly empties. A salon losing momentum while it waits is worth less every month.

## The quiet year that pays for itself

None of this needs a dramatic announcement or a big investment — just about a year of steady work: clean books, written routines, a team that shares clients, a secure lease, and a diary full of regulars. Owners who do it often find the salon easier to run and more profitable. Some decide not to sell at all. A salon ready to sell is simply a well-run salon, and a well-run salon is worth owning, whether you stay or go.
