A new client books a cut and colour at your salon in Dubai. She has never been to you before. She found you on Instagram, messaged at 10 pm, got a slot for Saturday, and now has three days to change her mind. She does not know what your salon looks like inside, who will hold the scissors, or whether the quoted price is the price she will actually pay. Each unknown is a small reason not to turn up.

Most salons answer with a confirmation message. A time, a date and a "see you then" is better than silence, but it misses the real problem. She is not worried about forgetting the appointment. She is worried about walking into a room full of strangers and trusting someone she has never met with her hair, her skin or her wedding look.

The fix is simple and costs nothing: introduce the stylist on WhatsApp before the visit. One warm message from the person who will do the service turns an anonymous booking into a meeting with a real human being. Salons that do this see fewer no-shows, calmer first visits and far more second bookings.

## Why a face and a name change everything

Many salon clients in the UAE are new to the country, new to the neighbourhood, or trying a new salon because their old stylist moved away. They compare Instagram grids, read a few reviews, then message the salon that replies fastest. The booking rests on trust borrowed from a screen, and that trust is fragile.

A message from the stylist herself makes it concrete. "Hi, I'm Mariam, I'll be doing your balayage on Saturday" is a completely different experience from "Your appointment is confirmed." The client can picture the person, and she can ask a question she would never ask a reception desk — whether her hair is too short for the style in her screenshot.

It also changes the maths of cancelling. Once a client has exchanged even two messages with her stylist, not turning up feels like standing someone up rather than deleting a calendar entry. No-shows on first visits drop noticeably, especially for colour and bridal trials.

## What the message should include

Keep it short: a few lines and one photo. The best introductions follow the same shape every time.

First, a greeting with the stylist's name and a recent photo of her — not a stock image. What reassures clients here is a genuine face, smiling, in your salon. Second, a line confirming the service, the day and the time. Third, one useful detail: how long it will take, or what will happen first. "We'll start with a consultation and a strand test, so please come with your hair dry and product-free."

Finish with an open door, not a demand: "If you have any questions before Saturday, just reply here." Some clients will send a photo of their current hair. Some will say nothing at all. Either way the message has done its job, because she knows who is waiting for her.

## When to send it

Send the introduction within a few hours of the booking, while the decision is still fresh. If the booking was made days ahead, add one short message the evening before — "Looking forward to seeing you tomorrow at 5, I'm Mariam." Two messages across the week feel personal. Five feel like spam.

Evening hours work in your favour. Many salon clients in Dubai and Abu Dhabi message after work, between 7 and 10 pm, and a reply that arrives while they are still thinking about the appointment lands far better than one that arrives the next morning. ## Making it happen every time

A good idea that depends on memory lasts about a week. Build it into the booking routine instead. Whoever takes the booking assigns the stylist and hands the conversation over in the same moment. The stylist then sends her own introduction before the end of her shift, from the salon's WhatsApp number, so the client always has one thread and one number.

Save the structure as a quick reply on the salon's WhatsApp Business account, with blanks for the name, service, day and time. The stylist fills in the blanks and adds her photo. A quick weekly check is enough: the owner glances at the week's first-visit bookings and confirms each one got its introduction. If a stylist is on leave, the covering stylist sends the message instead, honestly — "Mariam is away this week, so I'll be looking after you on Saturday."

Never send the introduction from a stylist's personal number. Clients will message that number forever, bookings will scatter across five phones, and the salon loses the thread the moment someone leaves. Everything stays on the business number.

## What not to do

Do not turn the introduction into a sales pitch. A first-visit message that ends with add-ons and a voucher code tells the client the salon cares about the bill, not about her. Save recommendations for the chair, where they can be honest and specific.

Do not ask the client for anything in return before she has even had the service: no review request, no referral ask, no "please share our page". Those asks belong after a good visit, not before one. And respect the clients who do not want a conversation. If she replies with a short "thanks, see you then", take the hint. The introduction worked.

## The second booking starts before the first

The real prize is not the first visit. It is the second one. A client who arrives knowing her stylist's name, who has already exchanged a message or two, and who is greeted by the person she expected, settles faster in the chair. She talks more openly about what she wants, and rebooking with the same stylist feels natural.

That is how regulars are made in a market as competitive as the UAE. Not with a grand loyalty scheme, but with a small human moment at exactly the right time. An introduction costs your team about two minutes per client. Do it for every new booking for a month, and watch the no-show rate and the rebooking rate.
