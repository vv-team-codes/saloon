Most UAE salon days do not fall apart in one dramatic moment. They unravel slowly: a colour runs twenty minutes over at noon, the 4 pm client waits, and by the 8 pm rush the diary that looked perfect at 10 am is a queue of apologies. The diary was never the problem — nobody looked at it properly before the day began.

The fix is simple: a ten-minute morning huddle. Before the first client walks in, the team gathers around the diary and walks through the day together — five quick questions, ten minutes, and a day that starts with a plan instead of a hope.

## Why the diary needs a human look every morning

A booking system shows you appointments. It does not show you the day. It will not tell you that three long colour services are stacked back to back with no rinse gap, or that a first-time balayage client at 5 pm leaves no buffer before a regular at 6 pm.

It also will not tell you what changed overnight. In a WhatsApp-first market like the UAE, enquiries arrive at midnight and cancellations land at 7 am. A diary that was accurate last night can be out of date before the shutters go up. The huddle is the moment the team agrees on what the day really looks like.

Salons that skip this step spend the day reacting. Salons that do it spend the day executing. The difference is ten minutes.

## What the huddle actually is

The huddle happens at the same time every day, before the first appointment, and it lasts ten minutes — timed, not open-ended. Everyone on shift attends, standing, phones away, diary on screen or printed. One person leads it, usually the owner or manager. The leader's job is not to lecture. It is to ask the questions and make sure every answer has an owner.

Keep it standing. The moment people sit down with coffee, ten minutes becomes thirty. Keep it to the day ahead only — yesterday's problems get a separate conversation. The huddle is about today, and only today.

## The five checks that make up the ten minutes

You do not need a complicated agenda. Five checks, two minutes each, cover almost everything that goes wrong in a salon day.

**1. Today at a glance.** How many clients, how many chairs, and where are the gaps? Read the day out loud as a shape, not a list: quiet morning, heavy from 4 pm, a hole at 2 pm. If there is a gap, decide now what fills it — a same-day WhatsApp status offer, a waitlist call, or protected admin time — instead of discovering it at 2 pm.

**2. The risk spots.** Walk through the appointments that can go wrong and name them: a first-time client booked for a two-hour colour with no consultation note, a long service in a short slot, a client who has rescheduled twice already, a bride whose timing cannot slip. For each one, agree who handles it and when the client gets a message. Naming the risk in the morning turns a 6 pm crisis into a 10 am decision.

**3. Cover and breaks.** Who is on, who is off, and who covers what? Check that the stylist covering a colleague's client knows the client's history, that lunch breaks are staggered so the floor is never empty during the afternoon build-up, and that someone is on the front desk during the evening rush. UAE evenings are the busiest hours, after work and after the heat breaks — a team that has not eaten or rested by 6 pm will not survive them well.

**4. The messages to send now.** Some messages are worth sending before the client leaves home: a confirmation for the first-time client who has never found the salon, a heads-up to the client whose service may run long, a reminder to the regular who booked three weeks ago. Agree who sends each one and by when. Messages sent at 10 am prevent phone calls at 5 pm.

**5. The close.** Look at the last appointment of the day and work backwards. What time does the last service actually finish, not what time it starts? Who is staying late, and who is locking up? A diary that ends cleanly protects the team from closing time drifting later every night — and it protects tomorrow, because a team that leaves at midnight cannot huddle at 10 am.

## Running it in ten minutes, every day

Keep it tight with a simple rhythm: the leader reads the day in one minute, the team flags risk spots for three, cover and breaks take two, messages take two, and the close takes two. Anything needing more than a sentence goes on a list for after the huddle.

Write the decisions down — one line per risk, one name per action — on a whiteboard, a printed diary sheet, or a shared note. A huddle with no record is a chat; a huddle with a record is a plan.

## What changes after a month

The first week, the huddle feels like extra work. By the third week, the team starts arriving with observations of their own: a regular who always runs late on Thursdays, a colour service that always needs an extra fifteen minutes, a Friday pattern the diary never showed. By the end of the first month, the huddle has quietly become the salon's early-warning system — small problems caught while they are still small.

The results show up in the places owners care about. Fewer clients kept waiting, because the overrun was spotted at 10 am and the 5 pm client was warned at noon. Fewer double-bookings, because two people checked the same diary. Calmer evenings, because breaks were planned instead of stolen.

None of this needs new software or a new booking system. It needs ten minutes, a diary, and a team willing to look at the day before the day looks at them.
