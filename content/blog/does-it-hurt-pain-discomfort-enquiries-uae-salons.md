It is usually the first question a new client asks on WhatsApp, before she has asked the price: "Does it hurt?" She might mean a wax, a laser session, threading or a peel. Whatever the service, she is really asking one thing: can I trust you with my body?

How a salon answers decides whether she books — and whether she returns. A breezy "no, not at all" wins the booking and loses the client the moment the strip comes off. An honest answer calms her nerves and turns a first-timer into a regular.

## Why "not at all" is the worst answer

A receptionist, keen to close the booking, tells a client the treatment is painless. The client arrives expecting nothing, feels a sharp sting, and decides in that second that the salon cannot be trusted. She may finish the appointment, but she will not rebook — and she will tell everyone she knows exactly what happened.

In the UAE, where so many clients are new arrivals trying a salon for the first time, word of mouth travels fast. One exaggerated promise can cost you five clients you never meet. Honesty is not bad for business. It is the business.

## What she is really asking

Behind the question sit three worries: fear of the unknown — she has never had the treatment; fear of embarrassment — will she cope, will the therapist judge her; and fear of damage — redness or peeling before work or a wedding in the UAE's heat. A good answer covers all three.

## The honest scale: describe, don't rate

Pain is personal, so a number out of ten means nothing. What works is a plain description of the sensation, how long it lasts, and what happens next. Write one honest line for each treatment, and make sure everyone who answers messages uses the same words.

Waxing is a quick sharp sting that fades within seconds, with mild redness for an hour or two. Threading is a series of small pinches, over in minutes. Laser feels like a warm snap, stronger on coarse hair, with cooling gel making it comfortable. A light peel tingles for a day; a deeper peel stings more and peels for days. Each description tells the truth and names the after-effect before the client discovers it.

## A four-part reply that books the visit

A good WhatsApp reply has four parts, in one message. First, the honest answer: "A Brazilian wax does sting — a quick sharp pull that fades within seconds. Most first-timers say it was much less than they expected." Second, what you do about it: "We use a hard wax for sensitive areas and work in small sections. Numbing cream is available if you ask when you book." Third, the aftercare: "Expect redness for an hour or two. Avoid the gym, pool and hot showers until tomorrow." Fourth, the close: "We have a slot tomorrow at 6 PM or Saturday at 11 AM — shall I hold one for you?" She now knows what to expect and has two times to choose from. That is how an enquiry becomes a booking.

## First-timers need a smaller door

Some clients will read an honest answer and still hesitate. Give them a smaller first step: one underarm before a full leg, the gentlest peel strength, or a bikini line before a full Brazilian wax. Let her decide about the rest in the chair.

This costs the salon almost nothing and wins the client completely. She finds the sensation manageable, trusts the therapist, and books the full treatment herself. The salons that grow fastest are the ones that let a nervous client walk in the side door.

## When the honest answer is no

Some enquiries should not become bookings, and saying so builds the most trust of all. A client on strong acne medication asking for a wax, a client with fresh sunburn asking for a peel, a client with very recent laser asking for threading on the same area — these are moments to pause, not to book.

The right reply explains why, briefly and kindly: "With the medication you are on, waxing can lift the skin, so we would rather not risk it. Threading is safe, or we can book the wax six weeks after you finish the course." She may be disappointed now, but she will remember the salon chose her safety over a sale. 
## Train the team on one answer

The most common failure is not a bad answer but a different answer from every person who replies. One receptionist says "painless", another says "very painful", and the client loses confidence before she arrives.

Fix it with a one-page sheet: for each treatment, the honest description, the comfort measures, the aftercare line and the booking close. Keep it at the front desk and have new staff read it on day one. Agree too on what never gets said: never promise "no pain at all", never compare one client's tolerance with another's, and never joke about the pain. If a client asks whether a treatment is safe with her condition, the answer is to check with her doctor first.
