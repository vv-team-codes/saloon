It arrives as a short WhatsApp message, usually late in the evening: "Hi, next time can I book with Sara instead?" The client is a regular. Her usual stylist is Layla. The person reading the message has about thirty seconds to decide how the salon answers — because the wrong reply loses the client, and the wrong handling loses the stylist too.

Most UAE salons get this message more often than they admit. Clients switch stylists for all kinds of reasons, and most of them are not complaints. A switch handled well keeps the client in the salon and keeps the team intact. Handled badly, it sends the client to the salon down the road and leaves a stylist wondering what she did wrong.

## Why clients switch — and why it is rarely about skill

The first mistake is assuming the client is unhappy. Often she is not. She may want a stylist who is free on Saturday mornings, when her usual stylist is off. She may have seen Sara's work on Instagram and want to try a balayage Sara specialises in. She may simply fancy a change after two years with the same person.

## Never react in the chat

The worst replies happen when the person on the phone reacts before thinking. "Oh no, is something wrong with Layla?" turns a casual request into an accusation the client never made. "Layla will be so upset" makes the client feel guilty for a perfectly reasonable request.

So the reply should do three things and nothing more: accept warmly, confirm the practical details, and close with a booking. Something like: "Of course, happy to book you with Sara. She is in on Saturday — would 11am or 2pm suit you better?" That is the whole message. No questions about why, no apology, no mention of Layla at all. The client asked for a slot, and she got one.

If the client volunteers a reason — "Layla's cuts are great but I want to try Sara's colour" — acknowledge it lightly and move on: "Sara does beautiful colour, you will love it." If the reason sounds like a genuine complaint, do not investigate it over WhatsApp. Note it, book the client, and deal with the service issue separately.

## Telling the original stylist matters more than the reply

The WhatsApp message is the easy half. The hard half is what happens in the salon the next morning. Stylists notice when a regular stops appearing in their column, and they notice fast. If Layla finds out from the diary — or worse, from the client herself in the chair next door — she will assume the worst.

So tell her first, briefly, and before the appointment. A quiet word from the owner or manager, not a group announcement: "Mariam asked to try Sara for colour next time. She was happy with you, she just wants to try Sara's balayage." Keep it factual and short. The stylist does not need the full WhatsApp conversation.

This is also the moment to check something the salon should always know: is this the first switch away from Layla, or the fifth this month? One switch is normal. A pattern is information. If several clients are quietly drifting from the same stylist, that is a training or workload conversation to have privately — not a reason to block switches, which only pushes clients out of the salon entirely.

## Make the first visit with the new stylist work

A switch fails most often at the handover, not in the chat. Sara needs to know what Mariam usually has done and what products were used last time — before Mariam sits in her chair, not during the consultation. A client who has to repeat her whole history to a new stylist feels like a new client, and new clients are exactly what she did not want to be.

This is where good client notes earn their keep. A short note on the booking — previous cut, colour formula, allergies, preferences — lets Sara start from where Layla left off. If the notes live only in Layla's head, the switch becomes a gamble.

Sara should also be briefed on tone, not just technique. If Mariam likes a quiet appointment, Sara should know that before she starts chatting. And at the end of the appointment, Sara should not ask "so, will you be coming back to me?" That puts the client on the spot and turns a relaxed visit into a loyalty test. Let the client decide in her own time. A good result and an easy visit do the persuading.

## When the switch is a warning sign

Not every switch is innocent, and a good salon owner knows the difference. A client who switches after a visibly bad result, or who also stops replying to messages, is telling you something. The right response is a short, personal follow-up a day or two later — not from the stylist she left, but from the owner or manager: "Hope you loved the colour with Sara. If there is anything we could have done better last time, I would really like to know."

## The diary rules that prevent the mess

Switches go wrong when the diary is not ready for them. Three small rules keep the salon out of trouble. First, never let a switch become a secret: the new stylist, the old stylist and the front desk should all know, so nobody is surprised in front of the client. Second, keep the client's history with the salon, not with one stylist — notes and formulas belong in the shared record, so any stylist can pick up where the last one stopped. Third, if Layla's Saturday fills up because her regulars keep switching away, that is a staffing conversation, not a reason to quietly block the client's choice.

Clients who feel free to move between stylists stay in the salon longer than clients who feel trapped with one. A salon where switching is easy is a salon where clients experiment — a colour with Sara, a blow-dry with Layla — and that experimentation is what turns a one-service client into a whole-routine regular. Answer the message warmly, handle the people kindly, and let the diary do the rest.
