For years, most salon menus quietly stopped at a certain age. The treatments, the marketing photos, the language — all aimed at women in their twenties and thirties. Yet the client sitting in the chair at 10am on a Tuesday, the one who books the longer facial, buys the homecare, and returns every three weeks without fail, is increasingly a woman in her late forties or fifties. Menopause is changing her skin, her hair and her nails, and salons across the UAE are starting to notice — and to adapt.

This is not a niche. In a city where many residents arrived in their thirties and stayed, a large share of salon clients are now moving through perimenopause and menopause. They have disposable income, value expertise, and stay loyal when a salon treats them well. The salons winning them are not the ones shouting about anti-ageing. They are the ones that understand what is changing, and respond with skill and discretion.

## What menopause actually does to skin and hair

The first step is knowledge, not a new product range. When oestrogen falls, skin loses collagen and moisture faster, becomes thinner and more reactive, and heals more slowly. Hair sheds more and grows finer; nails become brittle and ridged. In the UAE, those changes meet constant air conditioning, hard water and intense sun — so treatments that worked five years ago may now leave a client red or tight for a day. A salon that explains this calmly, and adjusts, earns trust that no discount ever could.

## Rethinking the facial menu

The most visible change is in facials. Salons leading this shift are moving away from aggressive exfoliation and strong actives as the default for mature skin, towards barrier-first treatments: gentle enzyme exfoliation, hydrating and calming facials built around ceramides and hyaluronic acid, and LED light therapy offered as a soothing add-on rather than a miracle claim.

Honesty is doing the heavy lifting here. A good consultation now includes questions that would have felt awkward a decade ago — hot flushes during treatments, sensitivity that changes week to week, medication that affects healing. Therapists who ask these questions matter-of-factly, in private, make the client feel seen rather than exposed. Some salons now offer a short "skin reset" consultation as a standalone appointment: thirty minutes, no treatment, just an honest conversation. It converts well, because it removes the pressure to buy on the spot.

## Hair services need a different conversation too

Hair is where many clients feel the change most sharply. A woman who has had thick hair all her life notices the ponytail getting thinner, and the first instinct is often to panic. Salons that handle this well slow down the consultation and look at the scalp, not just the lengths; they are honest about what a cut, a gloss or a volumising treatment can and cannot do; and they suggest realistic routines, like gentler colour schedules and styling that adds volume without heat damage. Some UAE salons are adding scalp-focused services aimed at this group — detox treatments, scalp massage rituals, and low-commitment colour that grows out gracefully. In a market where many clients travel for the summer, a colour that still looks good after six weeks away beats a perfect shade that needs constant upkeep.

## The experience matters as much as the treatment

There is a quieter part of this trend: how the visit feels. Hot flushes are real, and a salon that keeps the treatment room cool, offers water without being asked, and never rushes a client through a flush is remembered. Privacy matters too: a quiet-corner consultation beats a shouted one at a busy front desk. And timing helps — clients over 45 often have more flexibility in the morning and early afternoon, the hours many salons struggle to fill. Mid-morning rituals built around this group fill the quietest chairs with reliable clients.

## How to add it without a gimmick

The salons doing this well are not launching a "menopause package" with a pink ribbon on it. They are doing something quieter and more durable: training the team, adjusting the menu, and changing the language.

Training comes first. A short team session on what menopause does to skin, hair and nails — and, just as importantly, on what not to say — pays for itself quickly. "Anti-ageing" language is on its way out with this audience; they have heard it for decades and they are tired of it. Words like "supporting" and "restoring" land better than "reversing" or "turning back the clock".

Next, the menu. Look at what you already offer and ask which treatments genuinely suit skin that is drier, thinner and more reactive. Often the answer is not a new service at all, but a gentler version of an existing one, with a clearer description and a realistic price: a hydrating facial with a longer massage, a scalp ritual added to a blow-dry, a nail service with proper cuticle care for brittle nails.

Then the marketing. The imagery matters enormously: clients over 45 notice instantly when every face in a salon's Instagram feed is twenty-five. Showing real clients in their fifties — with their permission — does more than any slogan. And the booking path should be easy: many of these clients prefer to message on WhatsApp, ask a question, and book when they feel ready. A salon whose first reply is fast, warm and specific wins the booking; one that leaves a question unanswered overnight often loses it to a competitor. Finally, measure it honestly. Track how many clients over 45 are booking, what they book, and whether they return. If the new services are working, the numbers will show it within a season: higher rebooking rates and longer appointments.

## A loyal audience hiding in plain sight

Beauty trends come and go, but demographics do not. The UAE's salon clients are getting older, and the salons that grow with them — learning what menopause means for skin and hair, training their teams to talk about it well, and designing services that respect the stage of life rather than fight it — are building a loyal client base in the business. These clients book regularly, spend steadily, recommend their friends, and stay for years. They are not asking for a miracle. They are asking to be understood.
