She arrives for a full head of colour, settles into the chair, and pulls a laptop out of her tote bag. She has a call at eleven, a deck to finish before lunch, and three hours of processing time ahead of her. She is not being rude. She is being practical — and she is the kind of client UAE salons see more of every week.

Flexible work is now normal across the UAE, and a salon appointment is one of the few fixed blocks in a fluid calendar. If she can answer emails while her colour develops, the appointment stops competing with her workday and starts fitting inside it. The salon she can always fit in is the salon she keeps coming back to.

## The working client is already in your chair

Most salons do not notice the working client until she asks for the Wi-Fi password, and then they improvise. A password scribbled on a scrap of paper. A socket behind the styling station. A chair in the middle of the floor where every conversation around her is audible on her call.

None of this is a disaster. All of it is small friction that tells her the salon was not built with her in mind. She judges a salon the way she judges a café: can I get things done here? Almost everything she needs costs very little — a reliable connection, a plug within reach, a little light, a little quiet. The salons that get this right simply make it easy, and she notices without being told.

## Wi-Fi that actually works

Start with the connection, because she will test it within a minute of sitting down. Salon Wi-Fi is often an afterthought, and the signal dies in the treatment rooms.

Test it yourself, in every chair. Sit where a client sits, open a video call, and see whether it holds. If the signal is weak at the far end, a small mesh extender costs less than a single balayage and fixes it for years. Keep the password short and printed on a small card at each station.

One rule matters more than speed: never ask a client to join a network that demands her email address or a social login. A simple password, freely given, is part of the welcome.

## Power, light and somewhere to put the laptop

A laptop with a dying battery is a client watching the clock. Every station where a client might sit for more than an hour should have a socket within arm's reach — not across the room, not behind a trolley. A discreet extension lead tucked along the wall does the job in older salons.

Light matters almost as much as power. The chair where she works needs enough light to read a screen without glare. Give the laptop somewhere to live, too: a small side table beats a lap every time.

## The quiet corner — without building a co-working space

Not every client wants to work in the middle of the salon floor, and not every client wants to hear someone else's conference call. The answer is not a renovation. It is one quiet spot.

Most salons already have one: the styling chair at the far end, the spare treatment room between bookings, the waiting area that empties after the morning rush. The team just needs to know to offer it. "Would you like the quiet chair today? It has a plug and the Wi-Fi is strongest there."

The quiet corner also protects the other clients. A salon is a place people come to switch off, and a loud sales call in the next chair ruins that.

## Processing time is the work window

The natural work window in a salon is processing time. Colour developing, a mask setting, a treatment working — stretches of twenty to forty minutes when the client is waiting anyway.

Smart stylists plan for it. They tell her, at the start, how long the wait will be and when they will be back. "Your colour needs about thirty-five minutes. I'll check it at twenty and rinse at thirty-five — you have the whole window." That certainty lets her start a task instead of hovering, half-waiting for someone to appear with a bowl. The worst experience for a working client is a stylist who returns every five minutes with small talk.

## House rules that protect everyone else

Welcoming laptops does not mean the salon becomes an office. Calls should be short and quiet, ideally taken in the quiet corner. If a client settles in for a long video meeting with her camera on, a polite word goes a long way: "Of course, take your call — the quiet chair at the end is best for it, the light is better there too."

Speakers stay off, headphones on. And the laptop closes during the parts of the service that need the client's attention — the consultation, the mirror check, the final reveal. A client who watches her own reflection appear while half-reading a spreadsheet misses the moment the whole visit was built around. A good stylist simply says: "Let's close the laptop for two minutes so you can see this properly."

## Small touches that turn a working visit into a habit

The salons that win working clients do it with details that cost almost nothing. A proper coffee, refilled without being asked. A note in her client record — "works during colour, prefers the quiet chair, oat latte" — so the next visit starts where the last one ended.

Booking matters too. Working clients book between meetings, often late at night, and they message rather than phone. A 24/7 AI receptionist like ZYNA answers those WhatsApp messages the moment they arrive and confirms a real slot — no waiting, no friction, no chasing.

And notice the pattern. A working client who clears her inbox during a colour appointment starts booking longer services, because the time is no longer lost. Over a year, that is a dozen extra appointments, booked in the hours you used to struggle to fill.

Do not charge for Wi-Fi or for the quiet chair — the moment a small comfort has a price tag, it stops being hospitality.

The working client is not a different kind of client. She is the same client, with a laptop. The laptop just means her time is precious, and respecting it earns something rare: a client who never has to choose between her work and her appointment, because she can have both.
