It usually arrives as a short WhatsApp message: "Do you have a prayer room?" Sometimes a second line follows — "And is there a place for wudu?" In a UAE salon, this is one of the most practical enquiries a client can send: the answer decides whether she books a two-hour colour appointment at 1pm or looks for a salon closer to a mosque.

Many salons answer it badly — not rudely, just vaguely: "I think so." "Let me check." The client hears hesitation about something that matters to her, and the conversation quietly dies. Getting this reply right turns a question about facilities into a booked appointment.

## Why this question comes up so often

Prayer times shape the day in the UAE. A colour appointment starting at noon runs through Dhuhr; a bridal trial collides with Asr; a facial finishing at sunset lands on Maghrib. When a client asks about a prayer room, she is doing the maths on a two- or three-hour visit and checking whether your salon fits her day.

The question is also often asked on someone else's behalf — a daughter booking for her mother, a bride asking for her grandmother. Each version is the same buying signal: this client wants to book. And how a salon answers it tells a client how she will be treated in the chair.

## The honest answer, in one message

The reply should do three things: answer directly, give one concrete detail, and end with a booking step. Never answer a yes-or-no question with "let me check" when the answer is something you could have known in advance.

If you have a prayer room, say so plainly: "Yes, we have a small prayer room at the back of the salon, with prayer mats and a quiet space. You are welcome to use it during your visit." It names the room, names what is in it, and gives permission. If you have wudu facilities, say so in the same message: "There is a washroom nearby where you can do wudu comfortably." Answering both at once saves a second round of messages.

If you do not have one, say so honestly and offer the alternative at once: "We don't have a separate prayer room, but there is a mosque a two-minute walk away, and you are welcome to take a break mid-appointment if prayer time falls during your visit." Honesty plus a plan beats a hedged answer every time.

## Whether you have a room or not, make the plan real

A prayer room only helps if clients can use it without awkwardness. Mention it in the first reply and again at the start of a long appointment: "Prayer time is in about twenty minutes — would you like to take a break then?" Keep it simple: a clean mat, a qibla direction marked discreetly, a shelf for a bag, a door that closes — and clear it out if it doubles as storage. Long services are the ones that cross prayer times, so mention the room before the client has to ask.

Most small salons in the UAE do not have a dedicated prayer room, especially in mall and high-street locations. That is fine — as long as the alternative is real. Know the nearest mosque by name and how long the walk takes: "The mosque on the next street is a three-minute walk, and prayer time is at 1:15 — your colour will be processing then, so it is the perfect moment to step out." A quiet corner with a mat is a second option — offer it honestly, never as if it were a proper prayer room. What never works is pretending: a client who arrives expecting a prayer room and finds a stock cupboard will simply not come back.

## The wudu question, and who answers the phone

When a client asks about wudu, she is often also asking whether the services she is booking work with her routine. A manicure just before prayer time is a different decision from one just after it, and a client who does wudu several times a day may want to know whether her nail polish will survive it, or whether a breathable polish is available. Know which of your products are wudu-friendly and say so plainly, and if a treatment needs to stay dry for a few hours, say that before she books, not after. If she asks a detailed religious question your team cannot answer, say so kindly and offer what you do know.

This enquiry also fails because the person replying does not know the answer. In many salons the phone is answered by whoever is free — a junior stylist, a receptionist on her first week. Write the answer down: one line for yes, one for no, one for wudu facilities. Keep it where the phone is answered — a printed card by the desk, a saved quick reply in WhatsApp. And answer in a warm, matter-of-fact tone, never surprised or awkward. In a UAE salon this question is not unusual. It is Tuesday.

## Turn the enquiry into a booking

A prayer-room enquiry is one of the warmest a salon receives: the client has already imagined herself in your chair for several hours. She is planning, not browsing. End every reply with a concrete next step: "We have Thursday at 11am, which finishes before Dhuhr, or Saturday at 2:30pm, after Dhuhr — which suits you better?"

For long services, confirm the plan again on the day. A short morning message — "Prayer time is at 1:15 and you are welcome to use our prayer room during processing" — costs ten seconds and buys trust no advertisement can. And remember it next time: "You used the prayer room last time, so I have kept the 2pm slot free for you again."

## A small question with a large answer

"Do you have a prayer room?" looks like a facilities question. It is really a question about whether your salon sees its clients clearly: their routines, their faith, their days. Know what you have. Say it plainly. Offer the alternative honestly when you do not have it, and close with a time that fits the client's day, not just your diary. Salons win clients on cuts and colour, but they keep them on details like this.
