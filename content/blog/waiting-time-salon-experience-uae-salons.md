Every colour client in your salon spends a good part of their visit doing nothing. The foils are in, the timer is set, and for the next 30 to 45 minutes they sit in your chair with nowhere to go. Most salons treat that stretch as dead time. The client scrolls their phone, the stylist moves on to the next chair, and nobody thinks about it again until the rinse.

That is a missed opportunity. The waiting period is often the longest single stretch of a colour appointment, and it is the part clients remember most clearly when they decide whether to come back. A perfect colour can still leave a client feeling neglected after 40 bored, cold, thirsty minutes. Get the wait right, and they leave telling friends yours is the most relaxing salon in Dubai.

## The wait is part of the service, not a gap in it

The first shift is a mindset one. In a busy UAE salon, especially during the evening rush when chairs are full and the diary is tight, it is tempting to think of processing time as free time for the team. It is not free time. It is still the client's time, and they are paying for it.

Salons that handle this well plan the wait the same way they plan the colour. When the colour goes on, the stylist tells the client what happens next and when: "This needs about 35 minutes. I will check it at 20 minutes, and I will be back to rinse at 35." That one sentence removes the anxiety of waiting. The client can relax because someone is clearly in charge of the clock.

## Comfort first: the chair, the drink and the temperature

Before anything clever, get the basics right. A client sitting still for 40 minutes notices every small discomfort. The air conditioning in UAE salons is often set for a room full of moving people, so a still client in a cape gets cold fast. A light blanket, offered without being asked for, is one of the cheapest loyalty tools a salon can own. Keep a small stack folded near the colour chairs and train the team to offer one as naturally as they offer a drink.

The drink matters more than it sounds. A glass of water is the minimum. Karak, coffee, green tea or a cold infused water costs almost nothing per client and signals that the salon is paying attention. Offer it when the colour goes on, not ten minutes later. And refill it without being asked — a client who has to flag someone down for water has already had a worse visit.



The obvious answer to waiting is a phone, and most clients will reach for theirs. But a salon that relies on the client's phone has given up on the experience. Offer something better, or at least something different.

A short menu of small services that fit inside processing time works well: a five-minute hand massage, a quick brow tidy, a scalp massage while the colour develops. These do not have to be free, and they do not have to be sold hard. A simple line at the start — "While this processes, we can do a quick hand massage for 25 dirhams if you like" — turns dead time into a small extra service the client genuinely enjoys. Many clients say yes simply because it beats staring at the wall.



The single most important habit during processing is the check-in. A stylist who disappears for 35 minutes and returns only to rinse has left the client alone for the longest part of the visit. A stylist who stops by twice, even for 30 seconds, has made the client feel looked after.

The first check-in, around 15 to 20 minutes in, is practical: how is the colour developing, is the client comfortable, do they need anything. The second, a few minutes before the rinse, is about the finish: a quick word about what happens next, a reminder of the blow-dry or treatment that follows. Neither takes more than a minute. Together, they turn a long wait into a visit with three points of contact instead of one.

This is also where a little systems thinking helps. In a busy salon, check-ins get forgotten when the diary is full, so put the check-in on the diary itself. ## Turn waiting time into a quiet recommendation, not a sales pitch

Processing time is a natural moment to talk about what comes next, and it should be used — gently. This is when a stylist can mention the treatment that would help the client's hair between visits, or the home-care product that keeps the colour fresh. The key word is gently. A client sitting with foils in their hair is a captive audience, and they know it. One honest suggestion is helpful. Three is a sales pitch, and it poisons the whole visit.

The best approach is to tie the suggestion to something the client can see or feel: "Your ends feel dry after the summer — a mask today would help, and it only adds ten minutes." That is advice, not a script. And if the client says no, the subject is closed. Clients remember being respected far longer than they remember a product recommendation.



None of this needs a renovation. This week: blankets by the colour chairs, a proper drinks offer, and a stated processing time for every colour client. Next week: the two-visit check-in habit, written into the diary. Week three: one small processing-time service, like a hand massage. Week four: ask colour clients at the till, "How was the wait today?"

Salon owners spend a lot of time thinking about the colour itself — the shade, the technique, the brand. Clients judge all of that, but they also judge the 40 minutes in between. In a market as competitive as the UAE, the salon that makes waiting feel like part of the luxury is the salon they come back to.
