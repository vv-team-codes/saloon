It usually arrives as a short WhatsApp message, sent just before the booking is confirmed: "Can my husband wait inside while I have my appointment?" Sometimes it is a friend, a sister, or the family driver. The question is small, but the answer decides whether the client books at all — because it is really about privacy, comfort and respect.

A client who asks this is already almost yours — she has chosen a service and checked the price, and is sorting out the one detail that stands between her and the chair. Answer well and the booking lands; leave the message unanswered until morning and she books somewhere that answered first.

## What she is really asking

Behind it are three quieter concerns. The first is privacy. In a ladies' salon, clients expect a women-only space, and she wants to know whether that promise still holds. The second is comfort: a three-hour colour appointment is a long time for someone to wait in a car outside a mall in August heat. The third is logistics — her driver may be her only transport, or she may be combining the appointment with a family errand.

She is not asking to bring someone into the treatment room, or asking the salon to change its rules — only whether the salon has thought about the people around her visit. Salons that have, win the booking; salons that sound surprised do not.

## The four-part reply that works

A reply that converts follows the same shape every time.

First, welcome the question. "Of course, happy to help with that" tells her the salon deals with this every day. Nothing loses a booking faster than a reply that makes a normal question feel like a problem.

Second, give the real answer plainly. If companions can wait in reception, say so, and say what the space offers: "Yes, he is welcome to wait in our reception lounge — seating, Wi-Fi and coffee, separate from the treatment area." If your salon is strictly ladies-only, say that just as plainly: "Our salon is a ladies-only space, so we do not have a waiting area for gentlemen inside, but there is a coffee shop two doors down and mall seating just outside."

Third, add the one practical detail: where the waiting area is and roughly how long the appointment will take. "Your colour will take about two and a half hours, and the lounge is at the front of the salon, away from the styling area."

Fourth, close with the booking — the question was the last check before she committed. "Shall I book you in for Thursday at 6 PM? I will note that your husband will be waiting, so the team knows."

## When the answer is no

The hardest version is where the honest answer is no: your salon is ladies-only, your reception is a single open room, or your building does not allow extra visitors. A no is fine. A no that sounds defensive, or arrives after a long silence, is not.

A good no has a simple shape: confirm the rule, explain it in one sentence, then offer the nearest alternative. "We keep the salon ladies-only so every client can relax completely, which is why we do not have a waiting area for gentlemen inside. The coffee shop on the ground floor is a two-minute walk, and we can message you fifteen minutes before you finish."

One mistake to avoid: never make the person waiting feel like a nuisance. Phrases like "we prefer clients come alone" turn a practical question into a small insult, and the client remembers the tone long after she forgets the policy. The policy can be firm. The tone must be warm.

## The waiting area that earns bookings

If you do welcome companions, the waiting area itself becomes part of the service. It does not need to be large — just comfortable, clearly separate from the treatment area, and honest about what it is. A few chairs, a small table, Wi-Fi, a phone charger and a jug of water are enough.

Two details matter most. First, visibility: in a ladies' salon, the waiting area should never have a sightline into the treatment floor, the basins or the changing area. Second, timing: if the appointment is long, tell the person waiting roughly when to expect a message. "We will message you at about 7:30 PM when she is nearly finished" turns a three-hour wait into a plan.

## The variations you will actually get

The husband question is the most common, but not the only version. A driver waiting is a daily reality in the UAE, and drivers are happiest with a shaded seat, water and a clear finish time — they need a plan, not a lounge.

And sometimes the companion is the booking. A mother who brings her teenage daughter for a first haircut, a sister who comes along for a bridal trial, a friend who books her own blow-dry while she waits — these are not waiting-area problems, they are a second booking waiting to happen. The stylist who offers, "Would your sister like a quick blow-dry while she waits? We have a chair free at 6:30," has just filled an empty chair for free.

## A reply you can copy

Keep one saved reply for this enquiry, for the whole team to use:

"Of course — your husband is welcome to wait in our reception lounge while you have your treatment. The lounge is at the front, separate from the treatment area, with seating, Wi-Fi and coffee. Your appointment takes about two hours, and we will message him when you are nearly finished. Shall I book you in for Thursday at 6 PM?"

If your policy is ladies-only, swap the middle for: "Our salon is a ladies-only space, so we do not have a waiting area for gentlemen inside. The coffee shop two doors down is a lovely place to wait, and we will message you fifteen minutes before you finish."

Either way, the structure is the same: welcome, plain answer, practical detail, booking. Write it once, save it, and the next time the question arrives at 10 PM — and it will arrive at 10 PM — the reply goes out in seconds instead of waiting for the morning. That is often the difference between a booking and a lost client.
