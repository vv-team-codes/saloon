It happens in salons across Dubai and Abu Dhabi every week. A regular settles into the chair and pulls a shampoo she bought on holiday from her bag. "Can you use this one instead?" she asks. "It works well on my hair."

It is a small moment, and it is easy to get wrong. A flat no can feel like a rejection of something she trusts; an automatic yes can backfire if the product does not suit the service or clashes with a colour treatment. How your team handles it says a lot about your salon.

## Why clients bring their own products

Understanding the reason makes the answer easier. Most clients are not trying to tell you how to do your job — there is a simple story behind the bottle.

Some have a sensitive scalp and have finally found a product that does not irritate them. Some are loyal to a brand they have used for years. Many UAE residents buy products while travelling and want to use what they already own. Others have curly or chemically treated hair and know exactly which formula behaves.

A few are quietly testing you: a new client who brings her own products is often someone disappointed before, watching whether you listen or dismiss her. Treat it as information, not as an insult.

## The first answer sets the tone

The worst responses are the two extremes. "No, we only use our own products" sounds like a rule being quoted at her. "Sure, no problem" sounds generous until the product underperforms — and then the client blames the result on your salon.

A better first answer acknowledges her reason before dealing with the practical question: "I am glad you found something that works for you — let me have a quick look and we will see how it fits with today's service." That validates her choice, buys the stylist thirty seconds to check the product, and makes it a decision about the service, not about her.

The stylist doing the service should answer, not the receptionist, because the decision is a technical one. If the receptionist takes the booking, she can note it on the diary so the stylist is not surprised at the chair.

## What to check before you say yes

A quick look at the bottle answers most of it. Retail shampoos and conditioners are fine for a wash and blow-dry. Has it been stored properly? A bottle left in a hot car is a hygiene question, not a preference question.

The real line is drawn at chemical services: colour, bleach, keratin and smoothing treatments depend on your salon's products working together in a known way, and an outside product introduces a variable nobody can control. The result — or the damage — lands on your salon. That is the moment for an honest no, delivered kindly: "For colour I need to use the products I know will behave with the colour we are putting on today. But I am happy to use your shampoo for the final wash."

Allergies deserve their own check. If a client says a product is the only one that does not irritate her scalp, believe her and write it on her record. If a dermatologist recommended it, respect that — and a quick patch test is still sensible when anything new touches the scalp during a chemical service.

## When the honest answer is no

Some requests cannot be accommodated. A client who wants her own bleach used, or a home colour kit applied in the salon, is asking for something no responsible salon should agree to. The same applies to products with no label or ingredient list, or a smell that suggests they have gone off.

Saying no well is a skill. Explain the reason in one sentence, then offer the closest alternative. "I cannot use a product I have not been trained on for a colour service, because if the result is wrong I cannot fix it. What I can do is use the gentlest colour range we carry, and your shampoo for the final wash."

Most clients accept that: it is honest, and it shows the salon thinking about her result, not its own rules.

## Make it feel like service, not a concession

When the answer is yes, treat her product like part of the service: decant it into a clean bowl rather than squeezing it straight from her bottle at the basin. If she brings it regularly, label it and keep it with her client file so she does not have to carry it in every time. Note the brand and the reason on her record — "own sulphate-free shampoo, sensitive scalp" — so the next stylist knows without asking.

These small touches turn a concession into care: the client feels heard, the stylist still controls the result, and the salon is flexible without being careless.

## One policy, one voice

The biggest risk is three different answers from three team members: a client told yes by one stylist and no by another will assume the salon is making it up.

Write the policy down in plain language: own products are welcome for washes and styling, not chemical services; the stylist checks the product first; allergies and doctor-recommended products are always respected; the client record notes what was used. Share it in the team chat and pin it near the front desk.

Answer the question before it is asked, too: many clients message on WhatsApp the night before, asking whether they can bring a product — often after closing, when nobody is there to reply. ## The trust underneath the question

At its heart, this is not a question about shampoo — it is a client telling you what she needs to feel comfortable in your chair. Salons that handle it well earn a client who believes the team listens to her, even when the answer is not the one she hoped for.

That belief is what brings her back: she will tell her friends your salon took her seriously, and book the next appointment without shopping around. The next time she pulls a bottle out of her bag, it will feel like a routine between people who know each other — which is exactly what a good salon relationship should feel like.
