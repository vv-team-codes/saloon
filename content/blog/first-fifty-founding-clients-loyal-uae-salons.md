Every salon has a first fifty. They are the clients who booked when the chairs were new, the walls were bare and there were no Google reviews to reassure them. They took a chance on a salon nobody had heard of, in a city where a new salon opens on every high street and in every mall. Years later, they are easy to take for granted — and that is exactly how salons lose them.

Founding clients are not just early customers. They are the people who told their sisters, their colleagues and their neighbours about you when you had nothing but a good haircut and a warm welcome to show for it. In a UAE salon, where so much of the diary is built on word of mouth, that early trust is worth more than any advertising budget. Keeping it is a retention job of its own, and it works differently from keeping an ordinary regular.

## They remember the salon you used to be

A founding client does not compare your salon with the one that opened next door last month. She compares it with the salon she first walked into — the one where the owner did her hair herself, where the place felt small and personal, where she was known by name from the second visit.

That memory is a strength and a risk. When the salon grows — more chairs, more stylists, a booking system, a bigger menu — a founding client can quietly feel that the place she helped build is no longer hers. She does not complain. She simply starts trying somewhere else, once, and then again.

The fix is not to stay small. It is to make sure growth never makes her feel like a stranger. When something changes — a new location, new hours, a new stylist joining the team — she should hear it from you first, personally, before she reads it on Instagram or discovers it at the door. A short WhatsApp message to your earliest clients before a change goes public costs nothing and says everything: you still matter here.

## Know their history, not just their hair

Most salons keep client notes. Very few keep client history. A founding client has a story with your salon: the first service she booked, the stylist she started with, the wedding she was preparing for, the baby she had, the move across town, the year she travelled and came back.

That history is the most powerful retention tool you own, and it costs nothing to use. When she books, the stylist who greets her should know more than her colour formula. "How is your daughter finding her new school?" lands differently from "Same as usual?" It tells her she is not a diary entry. She is a person the salon has known for years.

Put it in writing. A simple client card — first visit date, the services she has tried, the milestones you know about — kept where the front desk and the stylists can actually see it, is worth more than any loyalty app. Review it before her appointment, not after she has left.

## Give them something money cannot buy

Founding clients do not need another discount. In fact, discounting their loyalty can cheapen it. What they value is access and recognition — things a new client cannot buy at any price.

First pick of the diary is the obvious one. When the December party season or the pre-Eid rush opens, your earliest clients should be offered the best slots before the diary opens to everyone. Not as a formal programme with terms and conditions, but as a quiet message: "We are opening the Eid diary on Monday. Would you like your usual Saturday slot held for you?"

Recognition works the same way. Mark the anniversary of their first visit with a small, personal gesture — a handwritten note, a complimentary treatment upgrade, a quiet word from the owner. It does not need to be expensive. It needs to be real, and it needs to be about them, not about the salon. A client who has been with you for four years knows the difference between a genuine thank you and a marketing message.

## Let them shape what comes next

The clients who were there at the start have earned a say in what happens next. When you are thinking about adding a service, changing a price or refreshing the menu, ask them first — not in a survey, but in a conversation.

"Would you use a Saturday morning express blow-dry?" asked at the chair, to five founding clients, will tell you more than a poll sent to a thousand followers. And when you do launch the service they asked for, tell them. People stay loyal to places they helped build.

This also protects you from the most common way founding clients drift away: the salon changes in a direction they never asked for, and nobody noticed until they stopped coming. A client who feels heard will tell you she is unhappy. A client who feels ignored will simply disappear.

## When the salon outgrows them, bring them with you

Growth changes a salon. Prices rise, the team grows, the owner steps out of the chair, the little shop on the high street becomes a bigger space in a mall. Every one of those changes is a moment a founding client can feel left behind.

The salons that keep their first fifty through growth do one thing consistently: they narrate the journey to the people who started it. A message when the second branch opens. A personal invitation to see the new space before it opens to the public. A thank you, by name, in the room, when the owner tells the team how far the salon has come.

In the UAE, where clients move, travel and change routines constantly, a client who has stayed with you for years is rare and valuable. She has seen your salon at its smallest and stayed for its biggest. That kind of loyalty is not maintained by points or offers. It is maintained by remembering — remembering who she is, what she trusted you with, and that the salon she helped build still has a chair with her name on it.
