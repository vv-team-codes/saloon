Ask a room of UAE salon owners when they last paid themselves properly, and the answers are usually the same: a laugh, a shrug, and a version of "whenever there is something left over." The salon is busy, the team is paid, the rent is paid — and the owner, the person who took the risk and signed the lease, takes whatever is left. Some months that is a decent amount. Other months it is nothing at all.

That is not a business. That is a job with extra risk and no payslip — and it quietly holds a salon back, because an owner who never gets paid properly cannot tell whether the business is genuinely profitable or merely busy. The fix is simple to describe and takes discipline to keep: pay yourself first, as a planned cost of the business, not as a leftover.

## Why owners end up last

In the early days, everything the salon earns goes back into the salon. The owner tells herself she will start taking a salary "once things settle down" — but things never settle down. There is always a new service to launch, a slow January to survive, a rent increase to absorb, so the owner becomes the salon's flexible cost, absorbing every wobble so nothing else has to.

The pattern is worse in the UAE because the cost structure is unforgiving. Rent is fixed and high, and salaries and visa costs do not pause when bookings dip. A quiet summer month can drain the account fast, and the owner is the one who skips her own pay. Nobody notices, because the salon still looks fine from the front desk — but an owner running on empty starts discounting to fill chairs and saying yes to work that does not pay. Paying yourself first is the discipline that keeps those decisions honest.

## Start with what the business can actually afford

Before setting a number, work out the salon's real monthly profit — revenue minus every genuine cost, including the ones owners forget: rent, DEWA, salaries, visa and licence costs, products, laundry, card fees and marketing. A salon taking in AED 120,000 a month can easily be spending AED 105,000 once everything is counted.

Then choose a salary the business can sustain in an average month, not a brilliant one. If the salon reliably makes AED 20,000 of profit a month, AED 10,000 to AED 12,000 is a realistic starting point, leaving a buffer for slow weeks and reinvestment. It will feel low — but a salary you can actually pay every month, in December and in July, is worth far more than a generous number that collapses at the first quiet spell.

If the honest numbers say the salon cannot yet afford any owner salary, that is useful information too: the business needs more revenue, lower costs, or both. Plenty of owners discover at this stage that one or two services are losing money, or that off-peak chairs sit empty — and fixing those is what creates room for a salary later.

## The three-account method

The simplest system that works is to split the salon's money into three accounts as soon as it arrives. It needs no accounting software — three accounts and a weekly habit are enough.

The first is the operating account, which pays rent, salaries, suppliers and daily costs. The second is the tax and reserve account, which holds money for VAT, licence renewals and slow months. The third is the owner's pay account, which receives the salary as a fixed transfer on the first of the month, before anything else is spent.

The transfer happens automatically, the same amount every month, busy or quiet. If the operating account runs short, the owner does not quietly skip her salary. She decides, openly, whether to reduce it for a month, pull from the reserve, or fix the cost problem behind the shortfall. That visibility is the whole point: when your pay is a planned transfer, a bad month is a problem you can see and solve. When it is "whatever is left," a bad month is invisible until it becomes a crisis.

## Keep owner pay separate from owner perks

One trap is mixing your salary with things the business pays for on your behalf — a car, a phone, a family holiday booked through the salon. These are not a salary, and they hide the real numbers. If the business pays for something personal, record it as an owner withdrawal, separate from your salary.

If you also work as a stylist, your salary should still reflect your role as owner, not just your hours on the floor — the owner salary covers running the business.

## What to do when revenue dips

A fixed salary does not mean an inflexible one. Summer in the UAE is quiet, and even a healthy salon sees revenue drop when clients travel. Build the salary around the average month and let the reserve carry the lean months. If a dip lasts longer than two months, adjust the salary down temporarily — in writing, with a review date — rather than skipping payments silently.

A calm front desk helps here too: when enquiries are handled steadily through the day and after hours, slow periods hurt less. A 24/7 AI receptionist like ZYNA keeps answering and booking while the owner focuses on the numbers, so the diary stays fuller through the months that test the salary most.

## Review it every quarter

Once a quarter, sit down with three numbers: average monthly revenue, average monthly costs, and your salary. If revenue has grown, raise the salary a little and let the reserve grow a little too. If costs have crept up, find the leak before it eats your pay. A small, regular raise tied to real growth is far healthier than a big jump after one good month.

Over a year, this habit changes how you run the salon. You price services with your own salary in mind, stop discounting work that cannot carry its share of your pay, and can show a future buyer a salon that pays its owner — worth far more than one that merely looks busy.

Paying yourself first is not selfish. It is the clearest test of whether your salon is a real business: if it can pay its owner a steady salary month after month, it can fund its own growth. If it cannot, the numbers are telling you something worth hearing — while there is still time to act.
