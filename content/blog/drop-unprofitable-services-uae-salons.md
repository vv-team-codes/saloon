Every UAE salon has a service that looks busy and still loses money. The diary is full, the stylist is on her feet all day, the client leaves happy — and yet the month ends tighter than it should. The culprit is rarely a lack of clients. More often it is a service that costs more to deliver than it brings in, quietly eating chair time a profitable service could have used.

Most owners never find it, because nobody prices a service by what it really costs. The menu price feels right, the service sells, so it stays. But a service that sells is not the same as a service that pays.

## Start With the Real Cost, Not the Product Cost

Ask an owner what a full-head colour costs and the answer is usually the price of the tubes. The real cost is bigger. Add the developer, the foils, the gloves and the toning product. Add the stylist's pay for the hours the client sits in the chair. Add a share of rent, DEWA and cooling for every hour that chair is occupied — in a UAE salon, cooling alone makes a three-hour colour session surprisingly expensive.

Work this out once per service and write it down. To estimate the fixed-cost share, divide the month's rent, utilities, licence and insurance by the number of service hours your chairs actually deliver. The results often surprise owners: a long, low-priced treatment can lose money on every booking, while a quick express service quietly earns more per hour than anything else on the menu.

## Measure Profit Per Chair Hour, Not Per Service

The number that matters most is not the price on the menu. It is the profit each service earns per hour of chair time. A AED 400 service that takes four hours earns less than a AED 180 service that takes forty-five minutes, once costs are taken out — and it also blocks the chair for the whole evening rush, when your salon earns its best money.

Run the calculation for every service once a quarter: price minus full cost, divided by the hours the appointment actually takes. Rank the services from best to worst — the bottom of that list is where your growth problem lives.

This matters most in the UAE, where the evening peak between 5 PM and 9 PM is when most clients want to book. A low-earning service that fills a chair at 7 PM is a lost booking for a high-earning service a client would gladly have taken instead.

## The Four Kinds of Service on Your Menu

Once you have the numbers, every service falls into one of four groups. The first group earns well and clients love it. Protect these — give them the best chairs, the best stylists and the best evening slots.

The second group earns well but hardly anyone books it. These are hidden gems — a specialist treatment or a premium add-on clients would buy if they knew about it. The fix is marketing, not pricing: mention it at the chair and on your WhatsApp status.

The third group is booked often but earns little. This is the dangerous group, because a full diary hides the problem — often a discounted package, or a treatment priced years ago and never updated. These services need a price rise, a shorter version, or a limit on when they can be booked, such as weekdays only, so they never block a peak evening chair.

The fourth group earns little and hardly anyone books it. These are the services to drop. They cost you menu space, product stock and team attention for almost no return.

## How to Raise the Price Without Losing the Client

The third group is where most owners hesitate. A service has loyal clients, and raising the price feels like a risk. But a price rise on a service that loses money is not a gamble — keeping the price is the gamble, because every booking quietly costs you.

Give clients four weeks' notice, in person and on WhatsApp. Keep the message simple and honest: the price is changing on a set date, and here is what the service includes. Clients accept a clear, confident change far more easily than a nervous one.

## How to Drop a Service Gracefully

Dropping a service is easier than owners expect, as long as it is handled personally. Tell the handful of clients who book it first. A short WhatsApp message works: you are simplifying the menu, this service is being retired, and here is the closest alternative — ideally one that earns better and suits them.

Do not announce it as a loss. Frame it as a change: the service is being replaced by a treatment that gives the same result with a better finish. The client hears an upgrade, not a rejection. Then stop ordering the products for it. Slow-moving stock is dead money on a shelf.

## Fill the Chair You Just Freed

Removing a service is only half the job. The chair hours you freed have to go somewhere better, or the exercise was pointless. Move a high-earning service into those evening slots, open them to your waitlist, or give the time to a hidden gem that earns well but needs more bookings.

This is also the moment to look at your team. A stylist who spent hours on a low-earning service may have a skill that earns far more — a colour specialist doing basic blow-dries, for example. Shifting her hours to her strongest service can lift the whole salon's profit without hiring anyone new.

## A Leaner Menu Is a Stronger Salon

A long menu feels generous, but a salon grows faster with a shorter menu of services that all pay their way. Every service you drop is chair time, product money and team energy returned to the services your clients value most.
