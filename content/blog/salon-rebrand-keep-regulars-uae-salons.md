A new name feels like a fresh start. Maybe the old one never quite fit, or the salon has outgrown it. The decision usually feels exciting in the meeting room — and then, a few weeks after the new sign goes up, a quieter question arrives. Where did some of the regulars go?

Nobody announces that they have left because of a rebrand. They simply search for the old name, find nothing familiar, assume the salon closed, and book somewhere else. A rebrand does not have to cost you clients, but it will if they are the last people to hear about it. Keeping regulars through a name change is a communication job, and it starts long before the painters arrive.

## Tell them before the sign changes

The biggest mistake owners make is treating the rebrand as a surprise to unveil. A surprise works for a product launch. For a salon, where the whole business is trust built visit by visit, a surprise feels like a disappearance.

Tell your regulars first, while they are still in the chair. A short, warm sentence is enough: the salon is getting a new name next month, nothing else is changing — same team, same chairs, same prices. Say it at the end of the visit, when the client is happy with the result. The clients who hear it from you directly will reassure their friends and family for you.

Follow up with one clear WhatsApp message to every regular about two weeks before the change. One message, not a campaign. It should carry the old name, the new name, the date, and one line of reassurance that the team and the services stay the same. Clients in the UAE live on WhatsApp, and a message they can re-read beats a poster they walked past.

## Carry the old name for a season

For at least three months, keep the old name visible everywhere the new one appears. On the door, on the Google Business Profile, on Instagram, on the WhatsApp profile: "New Name — formerly Old Name". It costs nothing and it catches everyone who arrives by memory rather than by a fresh search.

This matters more than most owners expect, because clients find salons the way they always have. A regular who has typed the old name into Google Maps for two years will type it again. If the old name leads nowhere, the booking goes to the salon that appears instead. Keeping both names connected for a season is the cheapest insurance a rebrand has.

For the first month, the front desk should answer the phone with both names: "Welcome back — same salon, new name." To a client who walked in wondering whether she is in the right place, that one sentence is everything.

## Make the first visit back feel familiar

A rebrand is a good excuse to change the wallpaper, the menu design, the uniforms. Change the look, but protect the rituals. The welcome drink she always gets, the stylist who knows her hair, the way the appointment always starts — those are the things that tell a regular she is still in her salon.

Plan the first visit back deliberately. When a regular books under the new name for the first time, greet her by name, mention the change lightly, and then carry on exactly as before. A small gesture helps: a handwritten note at the station, or a complimentary blow-dry upgrade on that first visit under the new name. Not a discount — a welcome. The message is that the salon remembers her, even though the sign is different.

Watch for the regulars who do not come back in the first six weeks. That is the group a rebrand quietly loses, and they rarely complain. Send each one a personal message — not a broadcast, a real message from someone she knows by name. "We have a new name and I wanted to make sure you knew it is still us — same team, same chair. Would you like your usual Thursday slot?" Most of them simply did not know. Catching them at six weeks is far easier than at six months.

## Update every place a client could look

A rebrand fails in the details. Before launch day, list every place the old name appears and work through it: Google Business Profile, Google Maps, Instagram and Facebook pages, the WhatsApp Business profile, the booking link, the website, the mall directory, the price list, and the gift vouchers still sitting in clients' handbags. Miss one and a client will find the old name — and conclude the salon is gone.

The Google Business Profile deserves special care. Keep the old name in the business description for a few months, add a post announcing the change, and reply to any review that mentions the old name so future readers can see the connection. A profile that looks abandoned is a booking lost to the salon next door.

## What not to change at the same time

A rebrand tempts owners to change everything at once: new name, new prices, new menu, new hours. Resist it. Every change is a reason for a regular to hesitate, and several at once feel like a different salon entirely. Hold prices and core services steady for at least two months after the name change. Let clients get used to one new thing, and introduce the rest once the regulars are comfortably booking under the new name.

The same goes for the team. If a stylist leaves in the same month as the rebrand, clients lose two anchors at once. If a departure is unavoidable, tell the affected regulars early and introduce the new stylist personally.

## A rebrand is a retention project

Done well, a rebrand can strengthen loyalty: clients told early, welcomed back warmly and recognised by name feel like insiders in the new chapter.

Six weeks after launch, count how many of your regulars from before the change have booked under the new name. If the number is close to where it was, the rebrand worked. If not, the names on the missing list are your next job — one personal message at a time.
