A client books a full colour for Saturday at 6 pm, the most valuable slot of the week. She arrives and the stylist asks the one question nobody asked at booking: has she had a patch test? She has not. The colour cannot go ahead, the chair sits empty, and the salon loses its best slot of the evening.

Nothing went wrong with the service. The appointment was simply booked the wrong way. For colour, lash and brow services, the real appointment starts a day or two earlier — with a small skin test.

## Why the patch test changes the diary

A patch test is a tiny amount of product placed on the skin, usually behind the ear or on the inner arm, and left for 24 to 48 hours to check for a reaction. It is standard for hair colour and increasingly expected for lash and brow treatments. A client who has never had the service, is trying a new brand, or has had a reaction before needs one first.

That turns one appointment into two: a five-minute test visit and a service visit of hours. The service slot is only safe once the test is done and read. Salons that skip this step cancel services on the day; salons that build it into the diary almost never do.

## Book the test at the same moment as the service

The rule is simple. The moment a client books a colour or lash service that needs a test, the test visit is booked too — in the same conversation, before the call or chat ends.

A new client messages on WhatsApp on Wednesday evening asking for a balayage on Saturday. The reply should confirm the Saturday slot and offer a test time in the same message: "You are booked for Saturday at 6 pm. Your patch test takes five minutes — could you pop in tomorrow or Friday morning?" The client picks one, and both visits go into the diary together.

This matters because a client who leaves with only the Saturday slot will often forget the test. When the test is a diary entry with its own time, it becomes a real appointment, not a vague instruction.

## Hold the service slot behind the test

The second half of the system is protecting the diary. When the test is booked, the service slot is marked as conditional — held for that client, but not yet confirmed as safe.

Two habits make this work. First, the service entry carries a visible flag: "patch test due Fri 11 am". Anyone opening the diary sees the booking depends on the test. Second, the slot is not released to the waitlist until the result is known. Free it early and you lose a client who passes; hold it too long and the chair sits empty.

The cleanest rhythm is a deadline. If the test is due Friday morning and the client has not come in by Friday afternoon, the salon messages her once: a short note that the Saturday slot needs the test today or it will have to move. Most clients come in; the rest free the slot in time for the waitlist.

## Make the test visit worth the trip

A five-minute test visit is easy to skip, especially in a Dubai summer when a client would rather not cross town for something so small. Salons with high test attendance make the visit pleasant rather than clinical.

Keep it quick and welcoming. Offer a cold drink, confirm the shade, price range and time needed, so Saturday starts smoothly. It is also a trust moment: a client who sees the salon take her safety seriously is more likely to book again and to recommend it to friends.

## When the test fails

Sometimes a test comes back with redness, itching or swelling, and the service cannot go ahead. A failed test found on Friday is a problem with a solution; the same result found on Saturday in the chair is a crisis.

Start with a kind, honest message. Explain plainly that the skin reacted and the service is not safe.

Then offer an alternative: a service that does not need the product — a cut, a blow-dry, a treatment — in the same slot, or help her rebook a different colour brand once the reaction settles. A client offered an alternative leaves feeling looked after; one who is simply cancelled leaves feeling dismissed.

Finally, protect the diary. A failed test on Friday leaves a full day to offer the Saturday slot to the waitlist. Record the reaction in her notes, with the product and date.

## Who needs a test — and who does not

Not every client needs a test every time. A regular who has had the same colour, from the same brand, with no reaction in the last six months usually does not need a new one. A first-time client, a client returning after a long gap, one who has been ill or pregnant, or anyone switching brands does.

The decision should be made at booking, not on the day. Whoever takes the booking needs a short checklist: first time, new product, or a gap of more than six months? If yes, the test is booked. ## Fit the test into the quiet hours

Test visits take five minutes and need almost no equipment, which makes them perfect for the quiet parts of the day: weekday late mornings, the early afternoon lull, and the hour before the evening rush. Booking tests into these gaps costs nothing and keeps busy hours free for full services.

Name one person per shift to own test visits, so clients are seen in minutes.
