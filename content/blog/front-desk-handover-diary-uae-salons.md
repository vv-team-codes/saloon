Most UAE salons run long days — open around ten in the morning, last client out close to ten at night, and the busiest hours right at the end, the after-work rush between six and nine. No single receptionist works that whole stretch, so somewhere mid-day the diary changes hands. One person stands up, another sits down, and for about ten minutes the most important document in the business belongs to nobody in particular.

That is where bookings quietly go missing. A confirmation that was promised never gets sent. A client who asked to move from Thursday to Saturday gets moved in the chat but not in the diary. A deposit paid by link is never matched to the appointment it belongs to. Each slip is small. Added up over a month, they are why a salon that looks fully booked still has empty chairs and frustrated clients.

## Why the handover matters more in the UAE

In many countries a salon closes at six and the diary is one person's job. In the UAE the pattern is different. Mall salons follow mall hours, high-street salons stay open late to catch the evening crowd, and weekend demand lands on Saturday rather than Sunday. That means split shifts, part-time cover, and often two receptionists who barely overlap. The morning person knows the day's walk-ins; the evening person inherits the rush. Between them sits a gap, and the diary lives in that gap.

The evening shift is also the least forgiving one. By seven o'clock every chair is taken and the waiting area is full. There is no slack left to fix a mistake made at three. A double-booked chair at eleven in the morning is an annoyance; the same mistake at seven in the evening is a client walking out.

## What actually gets lost

Ask any salon owner what went wrong on a bad day and the answer is rarely one big error. It is usually one of four small things that never crossed from one shift to the next.

First, unconfirmed appointments. The morning receptionist messaged three clients to confirm tomorrow's bookings. Two replied. The third did not, and nobody told the evening shift she still needs chasing. By morning her chair is held for someone who may not come.

Second, deposits. A client paid by payment link at lunchtime and the money arrived, but the note linking it to her Saturday colour appointment stayed in the morning person's head. The evening shift sees an unpaid booking and sends a reminder asking for a deposit she already paid — a small embarrassment that costs real trust.

Third, client notes. The regular who needs the quiet chair because she takes calls during her blow-dry. The bride whose trial ran long and must not be rushed. The client who arrives with a toddler and needs the corner chair. These details live in notebooks, chat threads, or one person's memory, and they vanish at handover — along with tomorrow's first appointments, which the evening shift is the last to see.

## The ten-minute handover ritual

The fix is a short, written ritual that happens every single day, at the same time, in the same way, even when the salon is quiet.

Sit together for ten minutes. The person leaving talks, the person arriving writes. Go through five things: today's remaining appointments and anything that has changed; tomorrow's first three appointments and whether each is confirmed; the waitlist, in order; money — deposits received and deposits still owed; and one line per client who needs special handling this evening.

Write it down in one shared place — a paper sheet, a shared note, or a single WhatsApp group for the front desk. What does not work is each receptionist keeping their own private system. Keep it short: five headings, a line or two under each. The diary holds the detail; the handover holds only what changed, what is still open, and what could go wrong tonight.

## One diary, one language

Agree on a small shared language and use it every day: a tick for confirmed, a question mark plus a chase-by time for unconfirmed, "P" for a deposit paid, "D" plus the amount for a deposit owed, and a number showing a waitlisted client's place in the queue. These tiny habits remove the guesswork that causes most handover mistakes.

The same applies to client notes. "Prefers Sara" is useful; "prefers Sara, hates waiting, arrives ten minutes early" is better. And keep rules separate from preferences: "do not book after 6 PM" is a rule, "prefers mornings" is a preference.

## When there is no second receptionist

Small salons often have one person on the desk all day, or the owner covering the front desk between clients. The handover problem does not disappear — it just moves to the moments the receptionist steps away for lunch or a phone call, or leaves early and the owner takes over. The same ritual applies, in miniature: before stepping away, write down the five headings on a shared sheet. The diary should never depend on one person's memory.

## A quiet safety net

Even a good handover has a weak point: the hours when nobody is at the desk at all. Clients in the UAE message late — after dinner, after the gym, sometimes past midnight — and those messages are often the first contact from someone ready to book. If they sit unanswered until the morning shift arrives, the client has usually found another salon by then.

Do not redesign your whole front desk this week. Start with one thing: a five-heading handover sheet, filled in at the same time every day, kept in one shared place. Try it for two weeks.
