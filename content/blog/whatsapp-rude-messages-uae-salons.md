Most WhatsApp messages a UAE salon receives are lovely: a price question, a photo of the colour she wants, a booking for her usual Saturday slot. Then there is the other kind: the message in all capital letters, the voice note recorded at 1am shouting about a booking that was never made, the threat of a one-star review unless a refund appears by morning.

Every salon owner in Dubai, Abu Dhabi or Sharjah has seen at least one of these. The mistake is treating them like ordinary complaints. A client who is unhappy with a haircut wants a fix. A client who is rude wants a reaction.

## First, know what you are looking at

Not every blunt message is abuse. A client who writes "this is not what I asked for" is direct, not rude. A client who writes "your staff are useless and I will ruin your salon" has crossed a line. Learn to tell the difference quickly. The first deserves a service recovery. The second deserves a boundary.

The patterns are familiar: shouting in capitals or repeated voice notes, insults aimed at a named stylist, threats of bad reviews used as leverage for a free service, messages sent again and again at midnight, then anger that nobody replied.

## The first rule: nobody on your team has to take it

This is the owner's decision, and it has to be made out loud. Tell your team, plainly, that no member of staff is expected to absorb insults on WhatsApp, in the salon, or anywhere else. When the team knows the owner will back them, they stay calm instead of apologising for things that were never wrong and promising things the salon cannot deliver.

Give one person the job of handling hard messages on each shift — usually the receptionist or the manager, not the stylist named in the message. A stylist who has just been insulted should not be the one typing the reply. Keep the reply in the salon's voice, and keep it in writing so there is a record.

## The calm reply: three parts, nothing more

A good reply to a rude message has three parts and no more: acknowledge the point, state the boundary, offer the next step. Keep it short. Long explanations invite long arguments.

For an insult about a service: "Thank you for letting us know you are unhappy with the result. We are happy to look at it together in the salon. We do ask that messages to our team stay respectful."

For a review threat: "We understand you are frustrated. We would like to resolve this with you directly. Please let us know a time you can come in."

For late-night demands: "Thank you for your message. Our team replies during opening hours, and we will come back to you in the morning with available times."

For repeated messages sent minutes apart: "We have received your messages and will reply as soon as we can. Sending the same message again will not speed up the reply."

Notice what none of these do. They do not apologise for something that did not happen, offer a free service to make the problem go away, or argue about who is right. They keep the door open and the tone steady.

## Move it off the chat when it starts looping

Some conversations cannot be fixed in text. If the client keeps repeating the same demand, or the tone keeps rising, move it to a call. Offer one specific time: "Our manager can call you tomorrow at 11am to discuss this. Would that work for you?" A call has a beginning and an end; a chat thread can run for hours.

Do not keep replying to every message in a looping thread. One clear message, then a pause, is stronger than ten defensive ones. If the client continues, a single line is enough: "We have shared how we can help. When you would like to take this forward, please let us know."

## When to stop replying altogether

Most rude clients calm down once they are answered firmly and kindly. A small number do not. If a client keeps insulting staff, sending abusive voice notes, or threatening people, you are allowed to stop. Send one final message: "We have tried to help and we are happy to do so in person. We will not be able to continue this conversation by message." Then stop.

Keep screenshots of the thread and note the dates and what was offered. If the client later leaves a review, that record lets you reply honestly and briefly. WhatsApp also lets you block a number. Use that when messages become genuinely abusive. It is the last boundary, used after the polite ones failed.

## What never to write

A few lines should never appear in a salon's WhatsApp, however provoked you feel. Never insult the client back, never use sarcasm, never argue about the review in public, and never promise a free service to stop a threat. Never share the conversation with other clients, and never name the client in a status.

Screenshots travel, and a salon that stays calm in public keeps its reputation.

## The review threat, handled properly

Most clients who threaten a bad review never leave one, especially once they have been answered calmly and offered a real way forward. If a review does appear, reply once, briefly and factually: what happened, what was offered, and an invitation to resolve it directly. Future clients read review replies as a sample of how you treat people. A calm reply is worth more than a perfect rating.

## Boundaries are part of the service

Salon owners sometimes worry that setting a boundary will cost them a client. The opposite is usually true: clients who respect your team stay for years, and stylists who feel protected do better work and stay longer.

So write your one-page guide this week: who replies, what the calm reply looks like, and when to stop. Save two or three template replies your team can use without thinking. Then, when the message in capital letters arrives on a busy Saturday evening, nobody has to guess. The salon answers once, kindly and firmly, and gets on with the day.
