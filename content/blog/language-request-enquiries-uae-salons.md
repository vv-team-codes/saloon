It arrives on WhatsApp at 10:15 on a Wednesday night. "Do you have a stylist who speaks Tagalog?" Or in Arabic, Russian or Hindi: "Can I book with someone who speaks my language?" It is one of the most common enquiries a UAE salon receives — and one of the easiest to mishandle, because the instinctive reply, "Everyone here speaks English," answers a question the client never asked.

Dubai and Abu Dhabi salons serve one of the most multilingual client bases on earth. One diary can hold Emirati clients, Indian and Pakistani families, Filipina regulars, Russian residents and British expats — often on the same Saturday. For many, English is a second or third language, and a salon visit is a consultation about hair, skin and money, where one misunderstood word can mean the wrong colour or a bill twice the size expected. When a client asks for a stylist who speaks her language, she is trying to make sure she is understood.

## Why clients really ask

The request is rarely about small talk. A client who wants a balayage that grows out softly, or keratin that survives daily swimming, needs to explain that precisely — and to understand the answer. Nuance matters: the difference between "a little shorter" and "much shorter" is three inches, and in a second language that difference is easy to lose.

There is also comfort. A first-time client in a new city is already nervous; describing what she wants in her own language turns a stressful visit into a relaxing one. And there is trust: clients burned before use language as a safety check. If she can explain it properly, she believes it will be done properly.

Salons that treat the enquiry as a preference to honour win clients who stay for years. A client who can finally speak freely does not shop around: she rebooks, brings her sister, and tells friends in her own community.

## The reply that turns the question into a booking

The strongest reply has four parts. First, welcome the question: "Of course — let me check who is in for you." Second, give a straight answer, by name: "Yes, Maria speaks Tagalog, and she is our senior colourist." A named stylist is worth ten vague reassurances.

Third, offer a concrete slot: "Maria is in on Thursday from 2 pm and Saturday from 10 am — would either suit you?" Fourth, confirm the detail: "I have noted you would like a Tagalog-speaking stylist, so we will always match you with Maria." That one line tells her she will never have to ask again — and it turns a first visit into a regular one.

These messages often arrive late at night, once the client has finished work and has time to plan her weekend. A reply the next morning is fine; two days later, she has booked elsewhere. ## When nobody on the team speaks the language

Sometimes the honest answer is no — and no is fine, if it comes with an alternative. "We don't have a Russian-speaking stylist right now, but here is what we can do" keeps the client; "Sorry, English only" loses her.

Start with what you do have: a stylist who speaks a neighbouring language, a receptionist who can translate the consultation, or a team member who joins the first ten minutes to make sure the brief is clear. Photos help enormously — invite her to send inspiration pictures on WhatsApp first, so the stylist can see the look before a word is spoken. A short voice note in the client's language, translated with a phone app and confirmed in writing, is another honest bridge — and the written confirmation is a record both sides can rely on.

The worst answer is a promise you cannot keep. Never say "yes, we have someone" when the only speaker is on annual leave, or a junior who has never done the service she wants. She will discover the truth in the chair, and the disappointment costs more than the booking was worth. A client told honestly and given a real alternative will often book anyway — and will remember the honesty.

## Put the language on the client record

The salons that handle this best treat language as a client note. When a client asks once, the answer goes on her record: preferred language and preferred stylist. Next time she messages, the reply can say, "Shall I book you with Maria again, Thursday at 6 pm?" She feels remembered — and remembered clients stay.

It also helps the diary: if three regulars prefer a Tagalog-speaking stylist, schedule her across the busy Saturday and the weekday evenings when those clients come. Language notes turn scattered requests into a rota decision — and a rota decision into fuller chairs.

The same logic applies to hiring. Knowing which languages your clients actually ask for is worth more than guessing. A salon in a neighbourhood with a large Russian community that hires a Russian-speaking stylist has opened a market, not just filled a vacancy. Keep a simple tally for a month — which languages clients ask for, and how often — and the next hiring decision almost makes itself.

## Say it before they have to ask

The final step is to answer the enquiry before it arrives. List the languages your team speaks on your Google Business Profile, your website and your WhatsApp greeting message. It costs nothing — and a client searching for a salon where she can speak her own language will choose the one that says so.

Put it in the confirmation too: "Your appointment with Maria, who speaks Tagalog, is confirmed for Saturday at 11 am" tells the client the salon remembered. When a client thanks you for the match, ask her to mention it in a review — a Google review saying "they have staff who speak my language" brings the next ten clients who were about to send the same message.

Where every salon competes for the same clients, language is not a small detail. It is the difference between a client who books once and one who books for years. Answer warmly, honestly and fast, match the right stylist, write it down — and let her know she will be understood here.
