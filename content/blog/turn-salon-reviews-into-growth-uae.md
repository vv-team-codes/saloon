A new client moves to Dubai Marina. She searches "salon near me" on her phone, taps the first three results, and reads the reviews. She books the salon with 4.8 stars and 300 reviews, because in a city where most people are new and nobody has a trusted local recommendation yet, reviews are the recommendation.

That is how growth works for UAE salons now. Your reputation is not what you say about yourself on Instagram. It is what the last fifty clients said about you on Google, and whether a stranger believes them.

## Why reviews carry more weight in the UAE

Dubai and Abu Dhabi are cities of arrivals. New residents, new office workers, tourists on a two-week stay — a large share of every salon's potential clients have no history with any local salon. They cannot ask a neighbour. So they read reviews the way people in a small town would ask a friend.

A salon with 4.9 stars and twelve reviews looks promising but unproven; one with 4.6 stars and 400 reviews looks reliable. And one detailed negative review — "they rushed my colour and nobody apologised" — can undo twenty short five-star ones, because people remember stories, not averages.

The good news is that reviews are one of the few growth levers that cost almost nothing. You do not need an advertising budget. You need a habit.

## Ask at the moment she is happiest

Most salons ask for reviews at the wrong moment, if they ask at all. The receptionist hands over a card at checkout, or a generic message goes out the next day, and the client — already thinking about traffic on Sheikh Zayed Road — forgets.

The best moment is earlier: the mirror moment, right after the reveal, when she is looking at her hair and smiling. That is when the stylist says, genuinely, "I'm so glad you love it — would you mind sharing that on Google? It really helps us." A personal ask from the person who did the work beats any printed card or automated message.

Make it easy. A small QR code at the mirror or on the receipt that opens your Google review page directly removes every excuse. If the client has to search for your salon, find the review button and work out what to write, you have already lost her.

Do not offer a discount in exchange for a review. Apart from being against Google's rules, it fills your profile with flat, suspicious praise. A real review written by a happy client in her own words is worth ten bought ones.

## Answer every bad review — and do it well

A bad review is not a disaster. An unanswered bad review is. When a potential client reads a complaint with no reply from the salon, she assumes the salon does not care. When she reads the same complaint with a calm, specific reply, she often books anyway — because she has just seen how the salon handles a problem.

Reply within a day. Keep it short, human and specific. "We're sorry your colour appointment ran an hour late on Tuesday — that's not how we run our diary" is believable. "We value all feedback" is not.

Never argue, never blame the client in public, and never reveal private details. If the situation needs a real conversation, invite her back — "please message us on WhatsApp and ask for me, I'd like to make this right" — and then actually make it right. The reply is written for the hundreds of people who will read it later, not only for the person who wrote the review.

Answer the good reviews too — a "thank you, see you next month" shows a salon that notices its clients.

## Read your reviews like a business report

Reviews are free market research, written by the people who pay you. Once a month, read the last twenty and look for patterns instead of individual praise or complaints.

If three clients mention that the salon runs late in the evenings, that is not three unlucky days — it is a diary problem, and fixing it protects your busiest hours. If several mention one stylist by name, that stylist is a retention asset worth protecting. If two clients say they could not find parking, clearer parking directions at booking time solves it before the next review is written.

Reading reviews feels like admin, but it is the cheapest honest feedback your business will ever get — and the salons that act on it are the ones whose ratings quietly climb.

## Let your reviews do your marketing

A strong review profile is marketing that never switches off — it works at 11 pm when a client is scrolling in bed.

Use your best reviews where new clients actually look. A short quote on your Google Business Profile, one or two on your WhatsApp service menu, a line on your booking page. Pick reviews that describe a result — "my balayage still looks fresh after six weeks" — rather than generic praise. Specific beats superlative every time.

Never cherry-pick so hard that your profile looks unreal. A salon with nothing but five stars looks filtered. A 4.7 with a few honest criticisms and visible, gracious replies looks like a real business that cares. Trust is the product here.

## A simple weekly routine

Growth from reviews does not need a project plan. It needs fifteen minutes a week, done by the owner or the front desk. Monday: check new reviews and reply to every one, good or bad. Wednesday: remind the team to ask happy clients at the mirror moment, and share one recent review with the staff so the team sees what clients notice. Friday: note any pattern from the week's reviews and fix the one thing that matters most.

Over three months, a salon doing this will double its review count — not because the salon changed overnight, but because happy clients finally said so in public, and complaints now get fixed before they repeat.

In a market as new-arrival-driven as the UAE, your reviews are your shopfront. When a stranger chooses where to spend her money, she trusts the people who have already sat in your chairs. Give those voices a reason to speak, answer them when they do, and let them bring you the next hundred clients.
