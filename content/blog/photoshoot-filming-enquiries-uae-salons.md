It usually arrives on WhatsApp late in the evening, when the day's diary is already full. "Hi, we're a production team shooting a commercial in Dubai next week — can we film inside your salon for a few hours?" Or an influencer writes: "Can I shoot a reel in your salon? I'll tag you." Or a bride asks if her photographer can film her getting-ready shots on the wedding morning.

A filming request touches your diary, your clients' privacy and your salon's reputation all at once.

## First, work out which of three enquiries you have

The first is a location request. A production team or brand wants to use your salon as a set. They are not buying a service; they are renting your space, your light and your look. Price it like a commercial hire, not a haircut.

The second is a content creator request. An influencer wants to film a reel in your chairs, offering exposure for access or a free service.

The third is a client request. A regular wants her photographer to capture her getting ready for a wedding or a big event. She is already paying for a service and wants to add one camera to it.

## The five questions to ask before you say yes

Ask these in your first reply. They take two minutes and prevent almost every problem that follows.

**When exactly, and for how long?** "A few hours" is not an answer. Ask for the date, the arrival time and the wrap time.

**How many people are coming?** A photographer and a model are two people. A crew with a director, a stylist, a makeup artist and a videographer is six, plus lights and cables.

**What exactly will they film?** A close-up of a stylist's hands during a blow-dry is unobtrusive. A wide shot of the whole floor with clients in the background is a privacy problem.

**Where will the content appear?** A brand's commercial, an influencer's reel and a client's private wedding album are three different things with three different values to you.

**What are they offering?** For a location hire, the answer should be a fee. For a creator, a concrete deliverable — a tagged reel, posted within seven days, with your booking link in the caption.

## Pricing the disruption honestly

In Dubai, the evening diary — roughly 5pm to 9pm — is where the money is, and weekend mornings run close behind. A shoot that takes your floor during those hours is not free. It costs you the clients you could have served instead.

Work out what a normal hour in that slot earns you, and price against it. If your salon takes in roughly AED 800 an hour on a Saturday morning, four hours of exclusive use is AED 3,200 of lost revenue before a single extra cost. Add the time your team spends moving furniture and cleaning up. A location fee should cover the lost revenue plus a margin for the disruption.

For creator requests, the maths is different but just as honest. A creator with 200,000 followers in another country will send you almost no bookings in Dubai. A creator with 8,000 followers in your neighbourhood may send you five.

## Protecting your clients comes before the shoot

Your clients did not agree to be in someone else's content. A woman having her colour done, a bride in a robe, a client mid-facial — none of them should discover later that they appear in a brand's advertisement or an influencer's reel.

Set the rule before the shoot, not during it. Nobody is filmed without being asked first, in person, on the day. If a shot needs background clients, use your own team as background, or schedule the shoot in a closed section of the salon, or book it before opening hours when the floor is empty.

Tell your booked clients in advance when a shoot is happening during their visit. A short WhatsApp message the day before turns a surprise into a non-event.

## The shoot-day rules that keep the diary intact

Give the crew a hard arrival time and a hard finish time, and hold both. A shoot that runs an hour over eats into your evening diary. Build a thirty-minute buffer between the crew's finish time and your next full booking.

Assign one person on your team to own the shoot — the crew's only point of contact, the one who says yes or no to moving furniture, and the one who checks that clients are not being filmed.

Keep one chair, one mirror and one stylist completely free of the shoot at all times. And agree the payment before anyone picks up a camera — a deposit for a location hire, a posting date for a creator collaboration. Money and deliverables agreed after the fact are almost never agreed at all.

## When the answer is no — and how to say it well

Some enquiries should be turned down. A shoot that wants your whole floor on a Saturday evening, a creator whose audience is nowhere near Dubai, or a production that will not say where the footage will appear — all of these are reasonable noes.

Say it quickly, warmly and with an alternative. "We can't close the salon on a Saturday, but we could offer you Tuesday morning before opening, from 8am to 10am, for a location fee of AED 1,500 — would that work?"

## A shoot that goes well is marketing you did not have to make

A good shoot pays you twice: the fee or the content, and what it leaves behind — professional photos of your salon at its best, a reel that shows your work properly lit, a behind-the-scenes story that makes your salon look like the place where things happen.

Ask for copies of everything. Put it in the agreement: "We'd love a copy of the final photos and video for our own social media, credited to you." Most crews are glad to share. Then use that content properly — post the best shot with a caption about the service in the picture, tag the photographer, and link it to your booking number.
